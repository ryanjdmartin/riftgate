3 October, later still. Your note: start fresh, and the patterns you liked from the audition come back later, not now. So these two share nothing with any cut or with each other, and each is built on a principle nothing so far has used. Pulse code is rhythm first: one voice only, a dry low pip around 300 Hz with a soft front and no tone to speak of, at two lengths, a dot and a dash. Every event is a short cell, Morse-like but no letters, and identity lives in timing: a press is a dot, a lock a dash and a dot, a spend three even dots, the round three slow dashes, the commit a long cell. The telegraph: military, utilitarian, never piercing, never melodic. No oscillator plays a note anywhere; what you hear is a shell answering a touch, and each event is a different way of touching it: a tap, a double tap, a short bow, a long bow swelling, a scrape for no. Both measured for piercing (pulse sits 21 dB under its body throughout, the shell 15 to 28) and levelled by what the phone plays.

The audition

3 October. Your read of the war room: a worse version of the previous ones, more piercing, more distorted, cartoony rather than arcade, the press playing nothing; and the right question, whether the cuts are seeded from the same sounds. They were: every cut kept my grammar, event list, timings and pitch frame and changed the timbre, and I cannot hear any of them. So this is the music pass's method instead, the file of six bass voices you picked number 12 from. Three moments, six directions, nothing shared: no grammar, no source, no pitch frame. A press (any button), an order (a die spent on a foe), the commit (END TURN); then the three in a row over four bars of the riff, with a second press and order in between. Pick a direction, or a direction per moment ("B's press, D's order, C's commit"), and the set gets built from that, not from any earlier cut.

Second cut of the fresh pass (2 October, night, to your notes on the first: too much static for a digital feed; the work verb grating with no story; the knock not sold, since the human register has a digital softness beside its heavy gain and only the weapons hit hard; and the console wanting an identity of its own that sits in the music's atmosphere without drawing on its instruments, since the skirmish rotates three tracks and the map two more). So the console is an FM terminal. That era's cockpits and consoles were FM synths and sample cards, retro-future digital, and FM's glassy, faintly metallic tones are a timbre nothing in the music uses, so the separation is in kind rather than level. Its motions are digital: a tone that starts coarse and resolves to clean as the brackets land; the same run backward for a thing let go; pitches that step and never glide; no noise anywhere. Four verbs and a scramble, each twinning a motion the display already makes (brackets collapsing over a third of a second, digits scrambling at 45 ms a frame or 18 fps, text typing at 12 ms a character). Every button does sound (the first cut's "the finger is nearly nothing" was my misreading of a visual rule, struck from the bible): the key is a soft tick, and the display answers over it.

The phone still decides the pitches and the levels. The voice sits at E5 and B5 with E6 over, an E4 or E3 body under for headphones; every file is levelled by what the phone model plays of it. E and B sit under all five tracks. Say if any of it reads as the arcade: the guard is the slow fronts and the resolving motion rather than beeps.

Third cut, the operations channel (your listen of the FM cut: the direction holds and it reads well over the music, but it is an arcade cabinet, not the grimdark future; and your thought experiment, a war-game RTS). An RTS interface is not a cockpit you sit in but an operations channel: its sounds are traffic, not feedback. An order goes out and the unit answers; the field reports back. So the channel button on every row keeps the FM terminal as the instrument and runs it through a digital comm channel: a hard band from 300 Hz to 4 kHz, compression, codec grain, a squelch as each transmission opens and closes. The key is the channel opening and says nothing. A confirm becomes an order sent and, a beat later, the machine's acknowledgement in its maker's colour (the row below the verbs). The commit sends E, B, E as one transmission and every machine answers in turn: all units committed. The round opening is the field reporting. Pitch is spent on those two lines a round and nearly nowhere else. The squelch is a transmission's edge, not a hiss under anything; you freed this cut from the static note. The acknowledgement's beat after the order is a timing hook the game does not have yet.

Fourth cut, the war room (your listen of the channel: war room is the right call, the digital fracturing works, but the execution is arcade and early Flash, too fast and chirpy, the high-gain sounds piercing on the speaker; humanity's technology is brutal, military, utilitarian; a fresh pass, not an iteration on the FM set). So nothing here is a chirp and nothing is fast. The console is a military data system heard through the war room's monitors: low, slow, vocoded and broken up. Three materials. The carrier, a low tone at the bottom of what a speaker plays (B3, E4), with a slow warble on its loudness and a formant moving over it, so it is a machine's hum that almost talks; the pitch never moves, the mouth does. The voice, a vocoded blurt, a buzz through two formants that move as a mouth would, one to three syllables and no words: humanity's machines answer in grunts, and each maker has a vowel. The breakup, the fracturing you liked: buffer stutters, dropouts, the bit depth collapsing and recovering; the only quick thing in the set. Everything passes through the monitors: a steep roll-off above 3 kHz, a floor at 180 Hz, saturation for weight. Measured as built: the 2 to 8 kHz band sits 12 to 22 dB under the body in every file but the one-syllable lock (9), where the FM and channel cuts had sat 8 to 10 under; that gap is the piercing you heard. The grammar is the war room's: a dull switch for the key; the carrier coming up out of breakup for an acquire; a blurt sent and the machine's grunt a beat later for an order; a thrum building through stutter into a three-syllable statement for the commit, every machine answering after; the field reporting as breakup resolving into a held two-tone for the round opening.

What a pick means

A console pick lands as its recipe in the forge. A shape pick means the blow's sound is chosen by the component's shape rather than its damage type, with plasma for the Arsenal's energy, and the blunt impact for a component whose sound says blunt (the plow and the knuckles carry it; the rest derive their sound from shape and face). A sound list means layers: the first word is the blow, each word after it rides on the landing, and the forge builds the layer as a recipe timed to the blow. The list is game effects only, never gameplay: it is not read from keywords or tags. A metal pick means a hit, block and kill carry the struck machine's maker. The shield hit means the replay tells three things apart that it now plays as two: a standing shield taking the blow (the shield hit), armour turning it on bare hull (the block), and the hull taking it (the hit). A family pick means a foe's blow and arrival carry its faction, and the reveal gets a sound of its own. A footfall pick means the map plays the mover's silhouette. The shield breaking lands as its recipe, no hook needed. A level pick (keep the offset, or come back) lands in the forge's levels table. A voice pick means a proper pass on the words (a bigger piece of work than any sound here) and a line per moment in the game. Each hook is small; none is written yet.
