Working title: Riftgate  ·  art direction, round one

Riftgate
Style Boards

The design is settled; the art is not. Three pixel-art directions for a portrait-phone hex battler with dice skirmishes, each with real numbers, a real palette, and a sprite pair drawn in code so we can argue about pixels instead of adjectives.

Tone target: loose, ironic sci-fi. The machines and the stakes are played straight; the manufacturers, the propaganda and the things coming out of the rift carry the joke. Warhammer 40k grandeur, Doom energy, no grimdark fantasy.

Style A

Chunky low-bit

Sixteen-pixel units, four to six colours per faction, a thick ink outline on everything. Every unit is a silhouette first and a drawing second, so a 7-wide battlefield reads like a board game on a 400 px screen. The joke lands harder when the propaganda posters are lavish and the actual war machines are little tin toys.

References: Slice & Dice, Downwell, Loop Hero, Into the Breach (map readability, not its sprite size).

Palette · 10 anchors

These are style-direction sketches drawn in code by an AI, not art. A pixel artist would do far better in an afternoon; what matters here is the scale, the outline rule and the palette, all of which survive contact with a real artist.

Outline and shading

One pixel of pure ink around every sprite and between limbs, no anti-aliasing. Two tones per material plus one highlight, light from top-left, no dithering. The hazard accent appears once per mech (a stripe, a lens) so it stays a signal, not a colour.

Text and UI

Numbers in a 3×5 or 5×7 pixel face at the same 3× scale, so the die face and the sprites share one grid and never look composited. Long text (unit descriptions, manufacturer copy) on a native-resolution layer; nobody wants to read a paragraph in an 8 px font.

Verdict for Riftgate

  • Readability at 48 px is guaranteed; 3× is a clean integer and the grid fits with margin to spare.
  • Fastest to make: a new unit is 20–40 minutes, an enemy variant is a palette swap.
  • Suits the tone. Tin-toy tanks against a lavish propaganda poster is the joke in one image.
  • Dice, glyphs and sprites live on one pixel grid, so the skirmish screen feels like one object.
  • Apocalyptic war machine is a hard sell at 16 px. They read as cute before they read as heavy.
  • A 16 px sprite at 6× in the skirmish is a 96 px blob of big squares; needs separate 24–32 px skirmish sprites or a lot of VFX to carry the screen.
  • Loadout is the customisation fantasy, and a gun arm versus a missile pod is a 3-pixel difference.
Style B

Mid-res tactical

Twenty-four to thirty-two pixel sprites, 12–16 colours per faction, an outline only on the silhouette, checker dithering for metal. Mechs are drawn as assemblies: a hull, a leg set, hardpoints you can actually see. This is the Advance Wars and Into the Breach register, with the palette pushed toward 40k gunmetal, olive drab and one hazard stripe.

References: Into the Breach, Wargroove, Advance Wars (GBA), FTL.

The flagged rows are the trap. 32 px is where mechs get gorgeous, but 32 × 2 = 64 and 64 × 7.5 = 480 px. Either the grid shrinks to 5–6 columns, or the game accepts a 1.5× scale and looks slightly wrong on every frame. 24 px is the discipline.

Palette · 11 anchors of ~16

Code-drawn sketches again. The 24 px mech is authored by hand as a character grid; a real artist would fix the shoulders, add sub-pixel-feel edge highlights and give the demon a jaw that reads at 1×.

Outline and shading

A dark (not black) outline on the outer silhouette only; internal parts separate by value and by material. Three or four tones per material, 50% checker dither on flat metal, one specular pixel on lenses and barrels. Each weapon is a 4–6 px cluster drawn as a separate layer, which is what makes loadout swaps cheap.

Text and UI

Numbers in a 5×7 pixel face at 2×; die borders, bevels and glyphs at 2× so they sit on the sprite grid. Body text, tooltips and the propaganda cards on a native-res layer. The two layers mix well at 2× because the pixel size is small enough to read as texture rather than blocks.

Verdict for Riftgate

  • One sprite serves both screens at integer scale: 48 px on the grid, 96 px in the skirmish, no second asset set.
  • Hardpoints read. The gun arm is a gun arm, the missile pod is a missile pod, and swapping them is a layer change.
  • 40k grit is available through palette and dither without losing the outline that keeps units legible on a dark field.
  • The die at 2× feels like part of the mech, and the 120 px face has room for four glyphs and a number.
  • 32 px will tempt you constantly and it does not fit; the whole direction depends on staying at 24.
  • 1–2 hours per unit for a solo developer, plus real palette discipline: 16 colours per faction is a rule, not a suggestion.
  • Enemies at this detail level look dead when static; budget 2–3 idle frames each or the horde looks like a sticker sheet.
Style C

Hi-bit grim

Forty-eight to sixty-four pixel sprites, painted rather than outlined, heavy blacks, restrained palettes with one rim light. This is the Doom cover: machines with mass and heat, and things from the rift that are actually unsettling. It is the direction that makes the skirmish screen a poster, and the one that struggles most on the grid.

References: Eastward, Octopath Traveler (sprites, not the tilt-shift), Blasphemous, Darkest Dungeon for mood (not pixel).

1× means one sprite pixel is one logical pixel, about 2.7 physical pixels on a 1080p phone. Still pixel art, just fine-grained; the hi-bit look depends on that density.

Palette · 10 anchors of many

The 32 px sprites here are the roughest of the three, because painterly shading is precisely the thing a character grid typed by hand cannot fake. Treat them as a value study, not a look.

Outline and shading

No outline. Edges come from value contrast and a near-black rim on the shadow side only. Five or more tones per material with soft ramps, heavy shadow mass under every unit, one cold rim light from the rift and one warm light from the gate. Enemies get emissive eyes and wounds; mechs get hot barrels and a single hazard element.

Text and UI

Everything native-res: a vector die face, typeset numbers, vector glyphs. The sprites and the UI are two visual languages by design, which works when the UI is quiet and dark. It stops working the moment the UI tries to be pixel art too, because 1× pixel UI is unreadably small.

Verdict for Riftgate, and the hybrid

  • Mood. The rift looks like a rift, the mechs have mass, and the skirmish screen with 96 px units beside 120 px dice is the game's poster.
  • Doom and 40k badassery are native here; nothing has to be implied.
  • Enemies can be genuinely disgusting, which is where the ironic tone gets its contrast.
  • 4–8 hours per unit for a non-specialist, and animation multiplies it. A solo developer will feel every frame.
  • At 48 px in a 53 px hex on a dark field, no outlines and low value contrast turn a 7×13 board into smudges. Readability has to be engineered back in with drop shadows and faction rim colours.
  • Two visual languages (painted sprites, vector UI) are fine, but they never feel like one object the way A and B do.

The hybrid: chunky (A or B) map sprites on the battlefield, hi-bit sprites in the skirmish, exactly as Fire Emblem splits map and battle sprites. It solves readability and keeps the poster. It also doubles the asset count per unit, doubles the animation sets, and asks the player to accept that the tin toy and the war machine are the same thing. Fire Emblem gets away with it because its map sprites are deliberately abstract; Riftgate would need to commit to that abstraction on purpose.

Recommendation

Style B at 24 px, 2×, and steal C's grandeur where it is cheap

Go with B at 24 px: it is the only direction where one sprite serves both screens at integer scale (48 px on the grid, 96 px in the skirmish) while still showing which gun is bolted to which arm, and that legibility of loadout is the whole customisation fantasy. Take the grandeur from C where it costs no animation frames: painted manufacturer portraits, propaganda posters between waves, and a hi-bit rift backdrop behind a pixel-art horde. Keep A as the fallback if the 24 px units fail the squint test on a real phone, because the hybrid's doubled asset count is the one cost a solo developer cannot argue down.

Every sprite, hex and die on this page is drawn procedurally in a canvas from hand-typed character grids. They exist to test scale, outline rules and palettes, not to set a quality bar. Hex maths: pointy-top, width = √3 × R, an offset grid of n columns needs (n + 0.5) × width.
