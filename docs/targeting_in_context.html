Heat names its machine

Stays

The Red Dragon and the Black Mage. This is the aim the others are measured against.

In whole fights, Dragon's Roost and the Magma Pit, 600 fights: the Red Dragons drew 2593 picks and 2582 went to a hottest machine. The other 11 passed over a machine the round's blows had already wrecked. In 58% of the draws one machine alone was hottest, so the pick was certain. In 35% every machine was cold and the draw was even.

The pick is drawn as the round opens and never moves after it is shown. What you do this turn decides who is hottest when the next round opens: reboot the hot machine, or spread the heat. One state on the screen, an even draw among the machines that match, anyone when none does.

In the engine today the heat belongs to the Red Dragon, so Breath and Crush both follow it. Ryan reads it as a property of the attack, and the proposal puts it there.

A countdown carries its pick. Crush is drawn by heat on the round it gathers and lands a round later on the same machine, whoever is hottest by then. 561 picks were carried so, and 30% of them were still on a hottest machine as they landed. That is the commitment, and the arrow shows it both rounds.

The aim at the exposed picks like chance

Call 2

The Bone Wyvern, the Warrior, the Martial Artist and the Lancer. Its page says it aims at the exposed. Over 600 fights it picked the broken shield no more often than an even draw would.

The engine draws this pick by weight: one, plus the hull the blow would reach, plus half of any shield it would break.

The same position against a smaller blow leans harder, and the Buzzard all but leaves the draw: its 12 holds a blow of 10, so it weighs one.

In whole fights, the Flight at tiers two and three, 600 fights: 1403 times the Wyvern drew with a broken shield standing beside a whole one. It picked a broken one 411 times, 29%. An even draw gives 30%. With every shield up it picked the softest machine 30% of the time, against 28% by chance.

Why it comes out even. The weight counts only the hull left to reach, and the machine with the broken shield is usually the hurt one. Against a blow of 19, a broken machine with 15 hull left weighs 16, the same as the Honey Badger whole. Below that it weighs less.

So the aim behaves three ways, and the screen shows none of them. A blow that breaks every shield is near chance. A shield that holds the whole blow takes its machine out of the draw. A hurt machine is passed over.

A correction. I told you the bare machine is picked eleven times in twelve. That was a built case of two machines, one bare and one whose shield holds the whole blow. It does not describe these fights.

No aim ever sees a rebooting machine. The picks are drawn before you choose a reboot, and the machine opens the next round whole. So a reboot ends the aim's hold a round later, and never draws it.

The armed follows the dice

Call 3

The Basilisk, the Gorgon, the Rust Monster, Gilgamesh and the Thief. It works as written. Its fallback is the loose end.

Its job is to keep a jam or a corrupt off an entry that is no weapon, so it follows the dice. You cannot steer it this round: the picks are drawn after the roll and before a reroll. You can read it, on the dice row.

One pick in two hundred falls to the weights of the exposed, a second rule inside the first.

The buffed. The Djinn's Unmake goes for the machine with the most buffs. The move is only open while a machine carries one, so in 159 picks its fallback never fired. It can only when every buffed machine is already wrecked that round.

Every foe draws a pick, also on a move at nobody. A fall's single blow is the one place that hidden pick is used.

The Cassowary had no arrow on it all round. Holding the die over the kill previews the blow, and that is the only way to learn where it goes.

In whole fights, the Lich's Court, 300 fights: 256 Dread Knights fell, and 24 of them fell on a round they showed no arrow. In 600 staged kills of one showing Reraise, the blow landed on each of three machines about a third of the time: 201, 197 and 202.
