1

Concept and tone

Three nested loops. The campaign is the roguelike run: a sequence of battles, lost for good if the city gate falls. Each battle is a hex-grid battlefield with the gate on the south edge and a rift on the north edge, won by reaching and killing the boss that powers the rift. Each skirmish is a dice-driven fight triggered when mechs and enemy squads end a movement phase adjacent, and it is where the game's depth lives.

Decided

Doom and Space Marine energy.

Over-the-top badassery, played straight. Less overt irony than first planned: propaganda is fine, manufacturer advertisements and Borderlands-style gags are not, because this is the end of civilization. Never edgy, never grimdark fantasy. The city falling is never funny.

Decided

Mechs are grounded military mecha.

Mechwarrior lineage: rugged, brutal, heavy, built from parts that look serviced rather than cobbled together. Not Evangelion. In-world makers exist as provenance and house style, in a maintenance-manual voice, never as marketing.

Decided

Enemies are old Final Fantasy monsters.

Detailed, over-the-top high fantasy, contrasted against the gritty realism of the player units. One genre invading another is the dichotomy the game wants, and it replaces most of the verbal irony.

Decided

Hybrid sprite scales.

Chibi 16 px map sprites that make no attempt at scale, and higher-resolution combat sprites with realistic proportions, in the old Final Fantasy overworld-versus-battle split. Cuteness comes from proportion, not pixel count. Heavies, elites, and bosses overflow their hex on the map, anchored at the hex base, drawn south over north, with the hex as the tap target.

Decided

Few, non-disposable war machines.

Each player unit is an apocalyptic war machine that takes on many foes over many battles. No unit micromanagement. Enemy hexes are squads, so every skirmish is a few machines against a horde.

Decided

Learnable in play.

The game is not simple by nature, but a player must never need a wiki, a build planner, or DPS math. Tooltips and an in-game cheat sheet are fine. External tools are a design failure.

Decided

Mechs come from a procedural renderer; enemies from a hand or an image model.

Decided 14 September. Nobody on the project draws pixels, so each chassis is authored as a few dozen boxes and cylinders in tools/spriter/spriter.py and rendered orthographically into palette-quantised, outlined pixel art. One model yields every map facing and the combat sprite. Art direction for mechs: cold steel base ramp with one bold house colour per maker (Halvard deep red, Kestrel cobalt, Orrin & Pike bone-white plates) and white or amber markings; olive drab is out because it cheapens units at pixel scale. Chunkier legs, hard bevelled geometry, multi-paned cockpits, short-barrelled cannons: more Mechwarrior, more StarCraft Terran. Both sides use a three-quarter front facing in the skirmish, so the mech's face shows; the hangar and codex are the hero-shot screens. Pixel scales may change if that simplifies asset creation. Enemies keep their ornate, organic high-fantasy register, which the renderer cannot do, so they come from an image model or an artist using the sprite sheets as the brief.

Decided

Enemy art comes from an image model on the development Mac.

Decided 15 September. The enemy sheet's hand-typed grids are exported as a floor (tools/sheet_export.py). For real enemy art, tools/foeforge/ runs FLUX.2 Klein 4B locally through mflux (Apple silicon, about eight seconds a candidate), prompts built from each foe's codex text plus a fixed Final Fantasy VI style brief, then a cutter that keys the flat background, crops, scales into the 48x64 or 64x96 frame, flattens the shading to sixteen colours and adds the one-pixel outline. The model's own colours are kept by default; snapping to the faction palette is an option that so far reads muddy. Ryan picks a candidate per foe from a contact sheet. Check the model's licence before shipping.

Open

The game's name.

"Riftgate" is a placeholder.

Design tests

Every mechanic proposed for the skirmish is held to these. They exist because the designer's stated tendency is to overcomplicate for complexity's sake, and the goal is elegant fun.

Decided

A round takes about thirty seconds

of thought on a phone and contains one to three real decisions.

Decided

The build is visible every round.

You watch your components act. They are never just stat bonuses.

Decided

Two sources of randomness, both the player's, both visible.

The die decides what you can do; the damage roll decides how well. Kinetic faces roll a range and may crit; energy faces are static. No hidden hit rolls. Enemies never roll: their intents are telegraphed as exact numbers, as in Griftlands.

Decided

The player always has tools against the roll.

Rerolls before or after attacking, faces that raise the floor or deal the average, execute and finish effects that guarantee lethal, and visible enemy health. Variance is a tactical choice to mitigate, never a punishment.

Decided

Every squad poses a question,

such as finishing the resurrectors or interrupting the big hit, rather than being a damage race with different art.

Decided

Mismatch costs time, never ability.

A wrong loadout for a squad chews longer or eats more damage. It is never unable to act.

Decided

Never helpless, sometimes unlucky.

A bad roll with an interesting answer is where the fun lives. The design removes helplessness, not luck.

Decided

Hard cap on effects in play.

Zero to one chassis passive, where a passive is one idea in at most three rule lines: an upside and its cost count as one passive, two unrelated benefits count as two. Component passives are rare. Every trigger is shown on screen when it fires.

3

Campaign loop

Decided

The campaign is the run.

Battles are its floors. Permadeath applies to the campaign: if the gate falls, everything is lost. In-campaign progression (resources, research, upgrades) is lost with it.

Decided

Between battles: repair, salvage, research.

Resources collected in battle repair mechs and the city, upgrade the city's automated defenses and city abilities, and research new chassis, components, and commander tools. Wrecked mechs are salvaged, repaired, or broken down into components. This is core gameplay, not a menu.

Recommended

Bounded campaign, about ten battles in three acts.

One faction leads each act: undead, then demonic, then eldritch. The final battle seals the source rift. Winning unlocks difficulty tiers and new research for the next campaign. An endless mode can come later. Framing the campaign as a countdown to a convergence, one battle per night, gives the escalation a story for free.

Open

Campaign length and the ultimate goal

are not yet decided beyond "increasingly difficult battles." Target for mobile: a full campaign finishes in a few sittings, each battle resumable at any phase.

4

Battlefield layer

The battlefield decides where, when, and with whom you fight. It is deliberately the layer with positioning, so the skirmish can have none.

Map

Decided

Hex grid, gate south, rift north.

Procedurally generated terrain: water, impassable mountains, and more. One mech per hex. One enemy squad per hex. City automated defenses stand on the field near the gate and can be targeted.

Decided

The battlefield may be taller than one screen.

Seven wide stays the rule for thumbs. Height may exceed a screen and scroll vertically, with the camera following the active unit. Pointy-top hexes make defensive lines straight horizontal rows and force advancing enemies to zigzag. With cube coordinates in the logic, orientation stays a rendering choice.

Decided

Multi-hex objects.

A massive boss can occupy three hexes in a triangle. The gate and the rift are wider than one hex. Squads and mechs stay single-hex. Epic scale without player overhead. Simulation cost: units gain a footprint, and blocking, adjacency, and pathing treat every footprint hex as occupied.

Decided

Map presentation rules.

Squad count is one sprite plus a number badge for now, maybe a small, medium, large count later. Player units face the way they move, with three authored directions mirrored to six; battlefield sprites omit visible customization. Mechs get a per-class idle; enemies get no animation except attacks, in the old Final Fantasy way. If overflow hides something that matters, the overflow allowance is too big; partially hidden objects get an outline highlight.

Phase resolution

Decided

Enemy moves are previewed. Player plots routes and commits.

Every unit has a movespeed. Enemy squads usually head for the gate or the city defenses, occasionally for player units. The player sees where each squad will go, then plots each mech's route and commits.

Recommended

Player routes resolve first, then enemies execute their previewed moves and stop at the first blocked hex.

The preview is exact unless a mech ends its move on a squad's path. Body-blocking is the core operational verb, and forming lines is how the gate gets defended. Flyers can engage but cannot block, or blocking breaks.

Decided

Adjacency after movement triggers a skirmish.

A player unit adjacent to an enemy squad, plus any units touching those, forms a skirmish. Multiple skirmishes in one phase resolve one at a time.

Recommended

A skirmish is the whole connected group of adjacent units.

A clustered wave becomes one dangerous fight, which teaches the player to split blobs and pick off stragglers. Cap participants only if phone readability demands it.

Pacing: a race to the rift

Decided

No in-field repair. The battle keeps pushing forward.

It must feel like a race to close the rift before the city is overwhelmed, not a back-and-forth war simulation. Slow-and-steady versus blitz is the player's choice, including leaving a defender at the gate.

Decided

Damage sticks. Mitigation is a real concern.

Hull never repairs during a battle. Mitigation comes from recharging shields and from defensive tactics, not from healing.

Recommended

Shields recharge fully between skirmishes.

A skirmish solved fast costs nothing. A slow one costs hull. Optional knob: shields recharge only during phases spent out of combat, so pacing a mech has a benefit without a repair bay.

Recommended

One Rift Pressure clock.

A single bar that ticks once per battlefield phase and once per skirmish round, and spawns a fresh squad at the rift when it fills. Dawdling on the map and grinding in a skirmish feed the same meter. It is the race made mechanical, and it replaces any hard limit on skirmish length.

Decided

No persistent movespeed penalties from damage.

Combined with terrain, limping mechs make runs unwinnable with no warning. Hull damage has no secondary penalty in the first prototype.

Recommended

Squads show a likely-loot icon.

Reward types are faction-weighted (section 8). The squad shows the likely type, never the item, so path choice is informed without a table.

5

Skirmish layer

The skirmish decides how you fight. Touchstone: Slice and Dice. Target prioritization, semi-controlled randomness, and build preparation and synergy. Several variants were considered, including a zoomed-in hex grid; that one was set aside because it repeats the battlefield's decision type, fights the no-micromanagement goal, and is hostile to thumbs in portrait.

Round loop

Recommended

No positioning on the player side.

Mechs have no ranks and never shuffle. Enemies organize around them.

Recommended

Attack, then reroll: one interleaved phase, then the enemy, then straight back.

Every mech's die is rolled. Tap to lock, drag to spend, reroll up to twice at any point, before or after attacking, so a low damage roll is information for the rest of the round. End turn resolves the enemy and starts the next round at once, with no click to begin your turn. This is the loop damage ranges earn, and it is the thing that keeps the game from being Slice and Dice with extra layers. Rerolls are per round, never a pooled stock.

Decided

Damage ranges and crits, by weapon class.

Kinetic faces show a minimum and a maximum, roll between them the moment they land, and may crit for double. Energy faces show one number and never roll or crit: reliable and hot against swingy and cool. Crits are where the exciting passives live. There is no undo; the roll is the commitment. Numbers are in tens.

Decided

Hold a die to see every face.

Deciding whether to reroll needs the whole die, not the face showing. Hold lists every face with the current one marked; double-tap opens the same list as a codex box. Passives are not shown on the die; they belong to the mech.

Decided

No hard round limit.

A cap would punish defensive, value, status, reflect, and mitigation builds in favour of burst. Skirmish length is governed by the Rift Pressure clock, the withdraw option, and tuning (typical skirmish three to five rounds; enemy damage must outpace sustain so stalemates cannot happen).

Recommended

Player-initiated withdraw.

Withdraw is offered at the start of any round, the first included (changed 15 September: a player who sees a bad matchup on contact may leave before committing), before any face is spent, so there is never free damage: you always take the enemy's hits for any round you attacked in. Withdrawing ends the skirmish; survivors on both sides carry their damage back to the map and the squad marches on. This is the stay-or-push decision, made by the player.

Decided

Ends on: pool empty (victory), all hulls zero (defeat), or withdraw.

Victory awards resources and a temporary upgrade choice. Defeat turns the mechs to scrap: wrecks to be salvaged after the battle. Surviving enemies continue their assault.

Enemy formation

Decided

A squad is one enemy type. Composition comes from the map.

An enemy hex is one foe type times a strength. What you fight in a skirmish is whatever squads are chained together at contact, so the rift's formation and how the player disrupts it decide the composition. Factions describe the formation they send through the rift together. Bosses are single three-hex units.

Decided

Front and back ranks, enemy side only.

Simple two-rank structure. It adds a second axis to target choice: a front rank of bodies and a back rank of problems.

Recommended

A pool with ranks, in columns.

Two rows by three columns visible at a time. Dead enemies are replaced from the squad's pool, so a horde keeps coming and elites surface at set points. Each back enemy stands behind one front slot, so "exposed" is visible rather than a rule.

Range archetypes

Decided

No damage falloff at range.

Futuristic weapons hitting for less at range is counterintuitive. Range is expressed as archetypes with soft penalties, never smaller numbers.

Recommended

Two reach types plus keywords.

Line of fire hits the front rank freely and a back enemy only where exposed. Direct weapons, melee, and beams share this reach and differ by keyword: cleave for melee, pierce for beams (the whole column). Indirect hits any back enemy over the front; its soft penalty on the front rank is danger close, which splashes your own shields. No delayed shells: delay adds cross-round state for no gain.

Shields, heat, injuries

Recommended

Shields are a timer.

Shield faces add shield. The shield buffer gives a few rounds to solve the squad before hull is at risk. Enemy damage should threaten to break a shield within two or three rounds against a typical squad, so shield faces buy time rather than immunity.

Recommended

Heat is the second resource.

Strong faces add heat. A vent face clears it. Running hot risks internal damage. Passives and support faces (vent an ally's heat) make heat a team problem, not a solo one.

Decided

Injuries are one global rule with two levels.

When a hit gets past shield and takes at least a quarter of maximum hull in one blow, there is a one-in-two chance a face is injured. A cracked face loses its keywords, crit chance, and unique effect but still does its basic action. A cracked face hit again is destroyed and goes blank. The counterplay is shielding before a big telegraphed hit. Per-faction signature injuries were dropped as an unnecessary layer. Threshold and chance are placeholders.

Decided

A damaged face is a damaged component, and it stays damaged.

Cracked and destroyed faces persist across skirmishes and battles until repaired in the hangar. This is the component damage system: there is no separate one. It makes a lost skirmish cost something into the next battle and gives the meta layer's repair and salvage real work to do.

Pinned

Low-hull berserker or last-stand ability.

A mech at critical hull unlocking an overdrive face is Doom energy and an anti-spiral. Parked, not rejected.

Recommended

Terrain is a skirmish-wide condition.

The hexes involved set a modifier for the whole fight, so choosing where to engage still matters without positioning inside the skirmish. Water dampens burn, ruins give the squad cover, and so on.

6

Die faces and iconography

Decided

Primary icon, a number, small secondary icons, one catch-all star.

The primary icon is the face's type: the reach archetype for attacks, a shield for shield faces, and so on. The number is its power. Small superscript or subscript icons mark common attached effects. One catch-all icon marks a rare, unique effect (legendary faces). Long-press a face to inspect secondary numbers, unique effects, and temporary boosts. An icon cheat sheet is available from the pause menu.

Recommended

Every face does something.

The only blank faces are cracked ones from injury. A pristine war machine is reliable; an injured one is not.

Recommended

A keyword vocabulary of about ten.

Variety comes from combining number, reach, keywords, and heat cost with passives, not from more rules. Slice and Dice gets hundreds of distinct faces from a vocabulary this size.

Decided

Keywords are a list, not a single slot.

A face may carry several keywords, so faces can be upgraded during a battle or in the meta. The content validator caps authored faces at three keywords; upgrades may push past that at runtime. Iconography stays readable because the primary icon and the number are fixed, and extra keywords show as small secondary icons.

Decided

Minimal chrome in the skirmish.

No cards, no boxes. The log stays in the centre for now because it is needed for testing and the space is free; it can hide behind a button later. Health and status are small floating slider bars beside the sprite. Mechs face north-east and enemies south-west; mechs get a per-class idle, enemies only attack animations. A massive elite may occupy both rank slots of a column. Tap, double-tap, or hold shows full numbers and details. Passives are simple enough not to be displayed at all times. Enemy health stays visible (the JRPG-style hidden-health option was dropped with the Griftlands damage model).

Decided

The vector HUD is the UI.

Adopted 14 September from the layout canvas. Everything that is not a sprite is a flat vector overlay: slanted gauges, rounded-square dice with corner brackets when locked, bracketed [ATK 10] readouts, a terminal-style log. The HUD colour (green by default, amber, red, blue) is a player setting saved to the user settings file. Screen order, top to bottom: header tag, enemies, log, dice with Reroll on the left edge (square, circular arrow, count) and the intent eye on the right edge, mechs with their bars above the sprites at the bottom, End turn bottom-left and Withdraw bottom-right, away from the palm. Withdraw always asks; End turn asks only when usable dice are unspent. Keyword chips hug the die's edge as floating annotation tags with no leader lines and flicker only every few seconds. No help text on screen; a tutorial popup is owed if the gestures are not intuitive.

Decided

Skirmish readouts, second pass.

Decided 15 September from playtest notes. No unit names on screen: hold or double-tap for them. Gauges hug the drawing, not the frame, so the team sits low near End turn and Withdraw. One chip row under every sprite: a foe's intent as a fantasy-register icon with its number (sword for attack, kite shield for defend, heart for heal, arrows for buff and debuff, so the enemy's icons read as the other genre) and its statuses (burn, suppressed, exposed); a mech's statuses and its warning signs. Warnings come from a forecast of the end of the round that recomputes after every action: shield break, component risk, wreck, overheat; the gauges pulse over the part about to change, and the heat gauge shows what the current face will add. No rank labels. The die face: primary glyph large at the top centre, the number beneath, heat bottom-left, crit bottom-right, lock top-right. Reroll is one wide button centred right under the dice, the dice's own control and far from End turn; the intent eye sits in the header at the right, over the formation it concerns. Quotes are set upright, since neither bundled face has an italic and a sheared face renders badly. Passives read NAME: description.

Decided

Three rerolls, and a reroll always shows a different face.

Decided 15 September. Two rerolls did not feel like enough even before consumables and commander powers; the base is three. A reroll never lands on the face showing nor on one equivalent to it, as a known global rule: these are not really dice, and a reroll that changes nothing reads as a bug rather than as bad luck. A die whose faces are all alike keeps its face. The count shows on the Reroll button only, not in the header. This is a rule the player must be told: the Reroll button's tooltip and the die codex say it now, and the gesture tutorial should too. Knock-on to watch: on a four-face die three never-repeating rerolls almost always find the wanted face, so d4 may be too small a size to exist (see open questions).

Decided

Intents show the action, not the target.

Each foe shows what it will do and for how much. Holding a foe or a mech draws the arrows for that unit; an overlay button draws every arrow. This is Slice and Dice's model.

Decided

A foe type is not limited to one action.

Each foe in content/foes.json carries an actions roster: attack, defend, buff, or debuff, each with a player-facing line and a status. Sometimes a foe attacks, sometimes it shields, sometimes it buffs the horde or weakens a mech. The engine still runs only the basic attack, so the validator insists on exactly one implemented attack per foe and refuses anything else marked implemented; planned actions live in the file and stay out of the codex until coded.

Decided

Tap a die to lock it, hold it for details.

No separate Keep button. A locked die is highlighted and skipped by reroll.

Decided

Double-tap a unit for its codex entry.

Hold is the quick read: numbers and arrows while the finger is down. Double-tap opens a box that stays: the codex text, stats, actions, faces shown visually in a column with the current face highlighted, the passive with its rules, and last the flavour line as an italic quote. The head-on sprite sits top-right of the box across from the name. Every foe has its own flavour line, not the faction's. The design question a unit poses is never shown; fantasy and question are internal design fields, and any hint the player needs goes into the codex text. A tap outside closes it.

Decided

Numbers in tens.

Hull, shield, damage, and heat scale in tens, so upgrades can be incremental and big numbers feel big. Done in one pass with the damage ranges: burn is one stack per twenty damage dealt at ten per stack, danger close is half the damage rolled, heat overflows one for one.

Decided

The vocabulary is data.

content/vocabulary.json is the single source of truth for every mechanic term, written fantasy-first with a rule, a status (implemented, planned, idea), and a holes field. docs/vocabulary.md is generated from it and lists the holes at the bottom, so it shows where the mechanics are thin.

7

Mechs and the builder

Mechwarrior's build depth, simplified and dialled back. The feel to keep is that a loadout is a real identity. The habits to drop are strict weight classes and gated equipment.

Decided

Three to four mechs per battlefield.

Enough for a real decision between splitting up and staying together for skirmish strength, without tedious planning. Recommended: design for four, start a campaign with three, unlock the fourth slot early.

Decided

Chassis are not classes and not only bipeds.

Tanks, hovercraft, quadrupeds, and flyers are all candidates. No light, medium, heavy labels. Chassis diversity comes from base stats: hull, shield, movespeed, hardpoints, and similar simple numbers that do not need prominent display.

Recommended

Hardpoints set die size.

Each component contributes one face; the chassis fills any remainder. A chassis with four hardpoints rolls a d4 and is consistent and focused. One with eight rolls a d8 and carries more but sees each face less. That is the deckbuilder's thin-deck tension as a chassis stat. First pass: d4, d6, d8. The builder shows the odds of each face.

Decided

Zero to one chassis passive, and it may be a skirmish passive.

Two flavours. A double-edged niche definer: a skirmisher aircraft with big shield, high movespeed, low hull, few faces, and "double damage, double heat," positioned by its manufacturer as a hit-and-run assassin, that becomes a carry beside an ally who vents its heat. Or a simple multiplier fantasy: "doubles all burn," where the player stacks burn and burns everything like a maniac.

A passive is one idea. Two complementary rules, such as "when I gain shield, one random ally gains the same amount" and "my shield does not recharge outside combat," are one passive with an upside and a cost, not two. The test is whether it can be named in a phrase and understood as one thing. Cap: three rule lines.

Recommended

A passive multiplies or redirects what faces already do.

It never introduces a verb of its own. If it needs a new mechanic to explain, it is a component, not a chassis.

Decided

Components are free.

Untyped hardpoints, any component in any slot. A niche you can leave is an invitation; the player steps outside it for experimentation or because resources force them to make it work.

Recommended

Silhouettes become terrain traits.

Hovercraft cross water, quadrupeds ignore rough ground, flyers pass over mountains but cannot body-block, tanks move faster on roads. Team composition becomes a decision about the map as well as the fight.

Recommended

Makers with house styles, not brands with adverts.

Three or four in-world makers give chassis families a coherent silhouette and a maintenance-manual voice, and give the research tree a structure that is not a tier list. No marketing copy. No warranties, no brochures.

Recommended

Passive-only components trade a face for a passive.

Choosing one means a smaller die that behaves better. A build identity in one decision.

Decided

Component damage is face damage.

A component's face is intact, cracked, or destroyed, and the state persists until the hangar repairs it. Nothing finer. Salvage recovers the intact parts from a wreck.

8

Commander and city

The mechs are the team; the player is the commander. Commander tools do what mechs cannot: manipulation, mitigation, information, and movement. They are never a fifth gun.

In the skirmish

Recommended

One command die, rolled with the mech dice.

Its faces are rechargeable abilities, so recharge is the roll and nobody hoards a die result. Examples: smoke that blanks the back rank's intents this round, a drone screen that absorbs the next hit, focus fire that adds one to every face on a single target, a scan that reveals the squad's next two rounds. Die size (d4, d6, d8) is a research path with stronger faces and a backfire face or two on the larger dice.

Decided

Reusable commander abilities never deal direct damage.

One-shots may. Damage one-shots belong at the battlefield layer (soften a squad before contact) so mech dice stay the killing power inside a skirmish.

Decided

Command faces come from the hangar, never from skirmish rewards.

Thematically wrong as loot, and they would dominate the reward pick because they affect every skirmish equally.

Open

Is the command die needed at all?

The alternative is one-shots only, with meta upgrades to inventory size and effectiveness, and reroll bundles among the rewards. Claude's view: keep the die, because a rolled face is inherently non-hoardable and the one-shots-only model pushes everything into the inventory the designer already knows they would hoard.

One-shots and the hoarding problem

Decided

Spending a consumable must not feel bad.

The designer is in the camp that hoards potions for a rainy day that never comes. If one-shots are core, the design has to make spending the default.

Recommended

Three fixes.

One-shots picked up in a battle expire when the rift closes, so there is nothing to save them for beyond this battle. The pouch holds three, so a fourth pickup forces use-or-discard on the spot. A starting stock chosen in the hangar restocks every battle; research raises capacity and quality. Doom Eternal solved the same problem the same way: resources regenerate on a short loop, so spending is the default and saving is the anomaly. Saving a full pouch for the boss remains a legitimate blitz strategy with a real cost in missed upgrades.

Skirmish rewards

Recommended

Pick one of three.

A modifier on a mech face you already carry (lasts the battle), a one-shot for the pouch, or a battle-wide boon such as an extra reroll per round. Temporary upgrades amplify the build you brought and never replace it, so the pull is toward synergy.

Decided

Reward types are faction-weighted, never guaranteed.

Undead might give a greater chance of multiple or rarer one-shots, for example. Flavour to be determined per faction.

On the battlefield: city abilities

Decided

Battlefield-level abilities belong to the city.

Sprint, airlift, teleport, tractor beam, orbital strike, and any number of silly ideas. They are upgraded and managed as city systems, and no command die is rolled on the battlefield.

Decided

Cooldowns in battlefield phases are acceptable here.

Each movement phase is a turn, so a cooldown is legible and cannot be hoarded. City research shortens cooldowns, adds charges, and unlocks abilities. Cooldowns still do not exist inside the skirmish, where the die is the recharge.

Recommended

Some city abilities are limited by distance from the gate.

Artillery reaches the southern half, airlift only lifts from the gate, the tractor beam has a range. The push north leaves city support behind, which is a real cost to blitzing and comes out of flavour rather than a rule.

9

Enemy factions

Decided

Archetype-based, not elemental.

Each faction has a behavioral signature that demands a different tactical answer, with internal diversity and general paradigms to guide decisions rather than strict rules. Elements are flavour and the reward currency, never a damage-type triangle. Certain faction combinations are favourable or unfavourable for a given team, which is what makes path choice build-driven.

FactionSignatureWhat the build must answer
UndeadPlentiful, damage mitigation, lower health but regenerates. Hard to put down and keep downBig hits, damage over time to negate regen, armour piercing
DemonicHard hits on a telegraph, fire and energyMitigation, interrupts, tempo
EldritchBends the rules: corrupts faces, scrambles rerolls, swaps ranksRedundancy and cleansing

Push and pull: squads heading for the gate are the push, upgrades you want from specific squads are the pull. That tension is the battlefield layer's best decision, and faction alignment creates it for free.

10

Meta and resources

Decided

Two kinds of gain.

Persistent resources, spent between battles. Temporary upgrades, lasting the current battle only. Skirmish victory yields both.

Decided

Wrecks are recoverable.

Defeated mechs become scrap, salvaged, repaired, or broken down into components between battles. Losses hurt but stay recoverable, which is what prevents a campaign from being lost three battles before it ends.

Decided

Research trees.

Chassis and components, city defenses and city abilities, commander dice and one-shot capacity. Manufacturers give the chassis tree its structure.

11

Technical plan

Recommended

Godot 4 with GDScript.

Chosen on the project's merits, independent of any other codebase. A 2D-first renderer with pixel-art settings built in, hex tile layers, a strong UI toolkit for a game that is mostly panels and dice, Android export out of the box, no build step, and a permissive license with no runtime fee. Unity wins only if a deep ads and in-app-purchase SDK ecosystem is a day-one priority.

Recommended

Keep the simulation pure.

Hex math, movement, skirmish resolution, and map generation are plain scripts with no scene dependencies, driven by a seeded random generator. That makes them testable headless and gives reproducible runs for replays and daily seeds.

Recommended

Save at every phase boundary.

Android can kill a backgrounded app at any moment. If the whole state serializes at the end of each battlefield phase and each skirmish round, resuming is free rather than a retrofit.

Recommended

Cube coordinates for hexes.

Pointy-top rendering with cube or axial coordinates in the logic, so distance, neighbours, and lines are trivial and orientation can change later.

Recommended

Prototype the skirmish first.

It is the riskiest unknown and it defines what a component is, which defines the builder and the meta. It can be played with physical dice and index cards before any code exists, which is also the fastest way to catch overcomplication.

Repository

The game lives in its own folder and repository under Code/riftgate. Design docs live in docs/, the pure simulation in sim/, scenes and presentation in game/, and headless tests in tests/.

Content pipeline

Concepts are authored fantasy-first in docs/concepts/ using the template there, in batches by category. Vetted entries become data in content/*.json (vocabulary, factions, foes, chassis, components), which the game loads at startup. A validator in the test suite enforces the rules in this document, so a concept that breaks one fails the build. New mechanic terms enter the vocabulary first, with a status, and the readable vocabulary document is regenerated.

Open questions and next steps

Open

  • Campaign length and the ultimate goal.
  • Whether the command die exists, or one-shots only.
  • How deep the builder goes, and what repairing a cracked or destroyed face costs in the hangar. (Component damage itself is decided: it is face damage, and it persists until repaired.)
  • Enemy targeting logic among mechs inside a skirmish (lowest shield, highest heat, last attacker, random) and how it is telegraphed.
  • Whether a skirmish participant cap is needed for phone readability.
  • Exact numbers: squad sizes, face powers, shield and heat thresholds, injury thresholds, Rift Pressure tick rate.
  • The name.
  • Enemy actions beyond the basic attack: which defend, buff, and debuff actions the engine runs first.
  • Whether a four-face die is too small now that rerolls never repeat a face. Three rerolls on a d4 nearly guarantee the face you want; d6 may be the floor, or rerolls may scale with die size.
  • A proper iconography source. The line-drawn glyphs are placeholders; a licensed icon set (game-icons.net is CC BY and covers swords, shields, flames, skulls) rendered to textures would replace them in one pass.

Pinned

  • Low-hull berserker or last-stand ability.
  • Command die faces with a second, battlefield meaning. Rejected for now because it doubles the learning load per face.
  • Endless mode after the bounded campaign.
