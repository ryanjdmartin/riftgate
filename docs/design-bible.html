Decided

How to read a row.

A Decided row is one of three things: a design test (what the player feels, section 2), a rule the engine runs, or a content call for a named piece. A maker's or a side's theme, word or keyword is a Lean, never a Decided rule. A "never" carries its reason and the condition that would reopen it; a "never" without either records the current state, not a law. A row that retires a mechanism names the form that failed and the test it failed, so a new form that passes the test is free to be proposed. Every row is open to debate (section 2) and a passive may break any rule as a one-off (section 7): a proposal argues for the change in the open rather than designing around the row, and its bench shows the ideas a row killed, so Ryan can overrule the row and not only the idea. Decided 13 October, after the review of that day.

What a row holds (Ryan, 23 October: the bible is "primarily used for" session context): the decision as it stands, its reason, what reopens it, and Ryan's words where they set its strength. The rest has a home of its own: how a thing is built is the code's, docs/rules.md's and git's; a run's numbers are docs/measurements.md's, a row keeping the conclusion and a pointer; a placeholder number is content's, so a row quotes a number only when the number is Ryan's call or the rule itself; a design session's rounds are its brief's; a task is docs/todo.md's.

1

Concept and tone

This document records what is decided and why. The rules as they stand are in docs/rules.md, how things stand today in docs/status.md, and what comes next in docs/todo.md. Each row says what holds now; history lives in git.

The game

Decided

Three nested loops, and the depth lives in the skirmish.

The campaign is the roguelike run. A battle is a hex field, the gate at the bottom and the rift at the top, won by killing the boss that powers the rift. A skirmish is the dice fight opened on one or more packs at a time.

Decided

A few machines against a horde.

The player fields a few machines, none of them disposable, through many battles, with no unit micromanagement. An enemy hex is a pack, so a skirmish is a few machines against many foes.

Decided

Learnable in play.

Tooltips and an in-game cheat sheet, never a wiki, a build planner or damage-per-second maths.

Open

The game's name.

"Riftgate" is a placeholder.

Tone

Decided

Doom and Space Marine: over the top, played straight.

Propaganda is in; manufacturer adverts and Borderlands-style gags are out, because this is the end of civilisation. Never edgy, and a city falling is never funny. Grimdark is how this game plays it straight: the world is grim and nobody in it winks, though a player may take it as dark humour.

Decided

Grounded military mecha.

The machines are in the MechWarrior and StarCraft Terran line, never Evangelion's demon-mecha: rugged, brutal, heavy, serviced rather than cobbled together.

Decided

The Arsenal is twisted by the occult, never merged with it.

Each of its machines is a battlemech with a sealed occult power source where a fusion reactor would be, drawn by an engineer who has had things whispering in his ear and twisting his thoughts. Mechanical only: no flesh and no gore. One may borrow another line's frame, twisted (a corrupted Aile Strike, an Evangelion frame), and it still moves with the game's weight (section 7); none has a mind of its own. CARE pilots fly them, and CARE would never let anything else into its hangars.

Decided

The makers are provenance, a livery and a voice, never advertising.

In-your-face advertising turns the tone to black comedy. A maker calling the commander its best customer is fine.

The world

Decided

The Council rules the cities.

The Council for the Ultimate Liberation of Terra; its acronym is never written or spoken. It is a martial-law regime pretending otherwise, believing in its cause while profiting from it: a god-emperor's court closer to Pacific Rim than to BattleTech. It rewards innovation that helps the fight, so the makers compete for its favour, and it keeps the best hardware for its own military.

Decided

The player is CARE's Battlefield Commander.

CARE, the Civil Auxiliary for Rift Emergencies: the Council chose the name in condescension. The commander is a general, never a politician. CARE's people are the true believers, pragmatic and rebellious, played straight; the Council takes the credit and none of the responsibility.

Decided

The Arsenal is the Council's own maker.

It sells CARE a trickle and never its best. It is the one research path the player cannot force: its tiers open only as the campaign turns dire.

Decided

What the Council names a machine and what it manifests as can differ.

Ryan, 11 October: the Council, and by extension the Arsenal, are self-righteous seekers of knowledge and power who think of themselves as the saviours of humanity, so the names they give their machines and what those machines turn out to be can be incongruent.

Decided

The occult is the Council's, and the Arsenal says it in liturgy.

The domains moved the invasion to the genre's high fantasy, so among the makers the demonic and the eldritch are the Council's and its Arsenal's: the one place the player meets the dark on our side. The invasion meets its own dark at depth (section 9: the Convergence's last tier wears the final dungeon's cosmic register), which is the enemy's and never a maker's. The Arsenal is demonic in look and effect and liturgical in what the player reads: its manual never says demon; it says devotion, penance, judgement. That is the incongruence above made a rule: names in the register of justice and vengeance (section 7), machines that manifest as Hell, and a player who looks a name up finds both. Machines that named their demons would make the Council's nature the premise instead of a reveal, and that stays open (below).

Decided

Lore is Ryan's.

design.fantasy in content holds his homages and is never shown.

Open

The lore holes.

Is CARE an Auxiliary or an Agency? What does the Council do when the player fails, can CARE's contract be lost, and how does the Council's credit-taking become plot?

Pinned

Room to expand, left open on purpose.

Whether this is mankind's last bastion, whether other colonies look away, whether the Council had a hand in the rifts (its rise may become the campaign's spine).

The invasion's register

Decided

The JRPG genre invades, played dead straight.

Final Fantasy is the spine; homage spans the genre (the Gel is Dragon Quest's slime, the Needler the Cactuar made sinister). Every foe is drawn in one style of our own, never in a series' design language. Homage is by archetype (Ryan, 15 October): a series' own coinages take names of ours, as the Cactuar became the Needler, a homage's look is never the series' (the Exor homage need not even be a sword), and a mechanic its publisher holds a patent on stays out (Nintendo and The Pokémon Company sued over Palworld on patents, 2024, not on looks). Detailed high fantasy against gritty realism is the game's dichotomy, and it replaces most of the verbal irony (section 9).

Decided

Our side is 3D geometry; theirs is painted 2D.

The genre mash is literal: everything human is geometry, the foes are flat sixteen-bit sprites. The map is the overworld and the skirmish is the battle screen. On the map a machine is its skirmish model and a pack is a flat token; in the skirmish everything is full size.

Art direction

Decided

Ally art is mid-nineties 3D, built by code.

tools/lowforge builds each machine from a written brief: a few hundred flat-shaded facets wearing small generated tiles in its maker's metal, no hand modelling, no unwrapping, and no image model touches a machine (section 7 has the rules for models and motion).

Decided

Each maker has its metal.

Minerva matte steel and military green; Kestrel chrome, silver and blue; Wainwright & Sutler matte iron and warm brown; Aphelion pearl white and red; the Arsenal black chrome and red glass.

Decided

Foes are painterly sixteen-bit battle sprites in ornate high fantasy.

The anchor is the SNES Final Fantasy IV to VI enemies: realistic proportions with a small, low-set head, a frozen mid-menace pose, eyes without pupils, a weapon only where the brief names one. A brief names the genre's tradition, never a series' design, and a domain's look is a mood, never a palette. Heroes are drawn in the elaborate boss register, and the party is as varied as people are: genders, skin tones, ages, builds and postures differ across the eight (Ryan, 13 October), and their costumes are to differ too (Ryan, 19 October; the to-do). Each caster's power is its own shape and colour, never shared: the Cleric's crystal, the Augur's dark omen-smoke, a green so deep it is nearly black with pale edges (Ryan, 19 October: dark, not light), the Black Mage's flame, the Summoner's deep violet rune circles, showing no fire and no rock since the Elemental is the lava field's (Ryan, 13 October), and deep because mid violet is the map's rift-intent field under a deutan simulation.

Decided

Foe art comes from an image model on the development Mac, picked by Ryan.

tools/foeforge runs FLUX.2 Klein through mflux: four candidates per foe from its one-line brief (design.sprite), its codex and its design takes; Ryan picks on a page, at the desk or on the phone. A flaw his notes name is tried by the model before any hand, by kind (Ryan, 18 October: image to image is the better outcome most of the time): a flaw of structure (a limb, a hand, a grip, a weapon) goes through image to image from the picked render at strength 0.6, which keeps the composition; a flaw of colour alone through the edit mode on the picked source, which leaves the drawing untouched; a change of costume is a fresh render from a rewritten take, since image to image keeps the costume with the composition. Image to image drifts a little each pass, so it chains from the pick Ryan liked, never from the last pass. The model's licence is checked before shipping.

Decided

Foes are cut small and sharp, and readability is the cut's job.

72x96 (96x144 when massive, 36x48 for a hero's chibi), gently sharpened, sixteen colours with dithered shading and a one-pixel outline, shown at whole device pixels, never smoothed and never lit by the scene: the clash is painted pixel art against flat-shaded geometry. Each pick's source render is kept with its prompt and cut options (tools/foeforge/picks), since AI art wants fixes on the source before a recut.

Decided

The battlefield is CARE's holographic reconstruction.

Gritty, sunlit 3D ground, gate, walls and machines against the rift's bright, flat 2D world bleeding in through a hard dithered seam. Height is landscape only. The skirmish is fought on the enemy's ground: machines face the foes in a rear three-quarter view turned left, foes face left. The rift vortex is four frames of dithered pixel art, never smoothed or lit.

Decided

The gate and walls are huge armoured Arsenal models.

They fill the bottom of the field with tower guns, and the gate projects the field over the city, its mouth the one gap every pack heads for.

Open

The gate's look.

A more sci-fi gate than the gothic one, how the projected field shows, the walls as emitters.

Sound and music

Decided

Industrial, grooving and grounded.

Music is a heavy industrial core carried by the beat, never fast (Tool, Nine Inch Nails, The Omnific, Teho, Hozho); effects are weight and machinery, never space opera. The grounded sound carries the seriousness that lets the game be silly elsewhere. Everything is synthesised from recipes in tools/soundforge/forge.py.

Decided

Two palettes, mixed on every screen.

Human: drums, chugged guitar, driven bass, electric piano, synth lead, metal chime, and a held saw drone in every track. Rift: orchestra, choir, long-echo bells, backwards swells and written dissonance. Bells and choir play in both.

Decided

The music never builds, drops the bass or plays a speed snare.

A section changes by what plays, lasts eight bars or more, and adds instruments, not loudness. Complexity comes from harmony and counterpoint, never fills; the kick never moves, so a change of metre sits over a bar of the same length. Boss themes are epic, not intense.

A domain may have a boss theme.

It starts from its top when the boss's fight opens; a domain without one takes the skirmish's pick.

Open

Boss themes for the Meadow, the Waste, the Peak and the finale.

Decided

One master, judged through the phone.

A gentle tilt, a presence lift at 1 to 2 kHz, no hard clip; the weight sits in the 150 to 800 Hz band a phone can play, the sub a bonus for headphones. The mix is measured through the phone model (a 220 Hz high-pass, a 9 kHz roll-off).

Open

Level the other five tracks against Hold The Line, through the phone model.

Decided

Sound lands by A/B on Ryan's phone.

A sketch never overwrites a shipped file; a pick ships from the function that rendered it, at the level he heard (the forge's SHIP table); melodies come through the Melody Desk.

Decided

Nothing glides in pitch.

Filters and loudness may move, so every effect is an impact, a mechanism or a burst. The exceptions are Aphelion's falling death whine and a short glide into a console note.

Decided

Combat has Doom's punch for both sides, never comical.

Each spend is deliberate, so a blow is drawn out, with a boom tail and weight in the 250 to 2000 Hz band. The scan and the idle are silent.

Decided

Effects fall into four registers.

The console; the iron (a blow by its weapon, then the hit, block or shield in the struck machine's metal, and footfalls by way of moving); the rift (each sound family's blow, heavy blow and arrival, and the boss reveal); the red (contact, the cut, the gate and the outcome, tuned to E).

Decided

A blow sounds by its component's shape and entry.

Content names a sound word only where those cannot (barrel, twin_barrel, rack, blade, blunt, laser, plasma, deflector, coil). The blunt weapons share one blow: a car hitting a wall.

Decided

Deaths are short for foes and personal for machines.

A foe dies in one short blast. A machine dies at the end of the enemy's turn, one at a time, in its maker's own explosion with a gap left for Jenny: Minerva two booms, Wainwright & Sutler winding down, Kestrel crash and fire, Aphelion discharge and beeps, the Arsenal flames over a ghost. No knocks; a shield breaks in a discharge, without shards.

Decided

The console is a nineties war room.

StarCraft, MechWarrior 2, Descent: retro-future and weighty, never arcade, chirpy or fast. Bowed glass and plate that groan, a boop in the register of StarCraft's nuke warning, a pulse, a soft click. Each kind of event has its own figure, since shared pitches read as confirmation; heat signals never fall (cancel) and never rise (confirm).

Recommended

Calls made while landing the effects stand until overturned.

A hovercraft steps like a flyer, random pitch goes only on footfalls and struck plates, and there is no route-drawing tick.

Pinned

The weapons' blows are acceptable as they are.

A burn or shock modification adding its own layer is a stretch goal; each family's own foe death comes later.

Jenny

Decided

The console speaks as Jenny.

The Jenny dataset (Dioco), rendered offline by Piper through the mid chain, slowed by a third, plosives tamed, on her own bus about ten decibels over the music, credited "Voice: Jenny (Dioco)".

Decided

Terse and military, and always on information.

She speaks only sometimes, but every time on information: a machine lost, a component destroyed, heat past three quarters, an overheat, the gate breached. A rare personality layer (slang, references, Irish turns of phrase) is the only break in the fourth wall. She comments on the machine from outside it, censors herself under a tone, and calls the flattening corruption.

Decided

A line Ryan has heard stays unless he cuts it.

An unheard line comes in only when he adds it, every pool is rendered in full so each line can be judged, and the buff pool stays generic until the vocabulary is locked.

Pinned

The idle nag ("awaiting orders"); the loss pools stay thin.

Design tests

Every mechanic is held to these tests. Slice and Dice is the touchstone: target priority, semi-controlled randomness, build preparation and synergy.

Decided

Elegant fun, never complexity for its own sake.

Simple is fine: if every ability is a wall of text, the game is not playable on a phone.

Decided

A round is about thirty seconds of thought, with one to three real decisions.

Decided

The build is visible every round.

Components act on the die, never as mere stat bonuses.

Decided

The randomness is the player's, and it shows.

The die decides what a machine can do; a rolled weapon's damage decides how well. No hidden hit rolls and no crits. Enemies never roll damage: they draw their move and target from the seeded stream as the round opens and show both, exact, before the player acts (Griftlands); the one chance on their side, Numbed's resist, shows on its chip. One source of randomness means one the player can see and shift: the die, a rolled range, a rider's chance.

Decided

The game does the arithmetic.

A flat weapon never rolls, however many things feed its number, and the die shows the number its blow will deal, for the foe it is dragged over.

Decided

Chances live only on riders, and riders are additive.

Never a chance on the damage (a rolled range is variance the player sees and can reroll, not a chance) and never a binary win or lose. A chance the player can shift by their own choices is a decision; one they cannot touch is noise.

Decided

A damage rider on an action the player takes anyway needs a limit.

It costs no tempo, so nothing else holds it back (Ryan, 13 October, of the Wainwright & Sutler boiler, the Whip Scorpion since 20 October, whose vents blast a random foe for the heat they shed: the chance is its limit, and the player times it, so it stays a decision).

Decided

The player always has tools against the roll.

Rerolls before or after attacking, flat weapons, modules that raise a floor, visible enemy health: variance is a choice to mitigate, never a punishment.

Decided

Avoid binary play where possible.

A soft counter, taking back part of what the other side gains, beats a hard one wherever it makes sense: burn never stops healing, so a burning healer is a race, not a wall. Hard counters are not banned; the game has a stun.

Decided

Asymmetry over parity.

Pieces need no numeric parity with each other; asymmetric balance is harder, and it is where the interesting choices are. The guard is against must-picks (pick share in the runner, section 11), not against difference.

Decided

Builds grow from soft synergies, never a lynchpin.

The player crafts what works from what they find. A piece without which a build does not work (an ARPG's chase item) makes the player farm for it; this game avoids that (section 7, tags).

Decided

Every pack poses a question.

Finish the resurrectors, interrupt the big hit: never a damage race in new art.

Decided

Rank and file die to a die or two; the big foes open a gap between smashing and playing.

Ryan, 21 and 22 October, from the scale discussion: three tests the numbers are held to. Kill times: a rank-and-file foe dies to one good blow or two ordinary ones, so the round's question is which die goes on which foe and no combo is needed to clear trash ("One to two for rank and file makes sense"); a massive foe takes a team a round or two of focus, a boss three to five with its mechanics. The coordination gap: on the big foes a team that sets up and cashes (a Shock under the hitter, Flak under the rest, area on a bunched row) should need about half the rounds of a team that spends each die on the nearest foe, since armour makes basics bounce and setups pay and the HP span lets several rounds show it; on trash there is no gap, by design (Ryan: the big foes "might need to be higher in order for strategies to actually emerge during the skirmish that aren't just smash your face against it"). The wear: hull never heals in a battle, so a machine fought through about four hard fights without a withdrawal arrives at the boss worn, not wrecked ("Four hard fights might be okay"), read on a bot that never withdraws.

How the runner reads them: rounds to win per cell beside the win rate, and the gap as blind play's rounds over the planner's (section 11's rungs) on the big fights and on the rank and file, counting only the cells both rungs win a quarter of; the wear in the campaign report. Blind play is a rung of its own because the random rung's waste read as slowness on trash too, which is not the team working together. What they are read on (Ryan, 23 October: "I think your suggestions are sound"): the kill time on a squad of four, the squad the player fields (section 4), a trio's figure a diagnostic beside it; the gap on built teams fitted with the setup pieces a run finds, since a stock is basics (section 10's tiers row) and a team of basics has nothing to set up. Set aside: lower massive HP, which would close the room the gap needs (if the planner still matched blind play on a built team, its valuation was read before any content moved); and for Wainwright & Sutler's light trio, whose far higher wear on a formation is accepted since its two armour-0 machines are where its decisions are, a point of armour on its cars (against armour by role, section 5) or softer rank-and-file blows. What the runs leave is section 12's. Numbers moved to pass these are placeholders until the bands say so.

Decided

Mismatch costs time, never ability.

A wrong loadout fights longer or takes more damage, but always acts.

Decided

Never helpless, sometimes unlucky.

The design removes helplessness, not luck.

Decided

Rule text is a budget, not a cap.

About three rule texts on a chassis's description, its passive and its modules; a component's rule shows in its own popup and is not counted (Ryan, 12 October). Each is one idea in three lines at most (an upside and its cost are one idea). Common components carry none; a rare one carries a unique rule (the chase: the player may break the game now and then); a module is a small number and a stat line. Judged running on a phone with every trigger firing; the Clydesdale overruns it on purpose.

Decided

A map action that buys a fight buff is not a condition.

Moving or standing still, joining a fight, being caught: the player does these anyway, so the buff they switch on is a boost for a good part of the fight, and completely free.

Decided

A one-off exception for balance is a symptom: find the root cause first.

Ryan, 18 October: "If a situation occurs where it seems reasonable to propose a one-off exception for balance reasons, look into the root causes as to why that seems like a reasonable solution." The case in point was the revolver: "Rapid once a round on it" looked reasonable because a Rapid chain on a stepping die was free, and the root was that Rapid pieces paid no heat, which the stock pass priced, not a rule on one chassis. A passive may still break a rule as a one-off for its design (section 7); this row is about exceptions reached for to fix a number.

Decided

Every rule and guideline is open to debate.

A good reason moves any of them: the tests in this section, a maker's profile, a settled row. That is how the game iterates and improves, so a proposal argues for changing a rule in the open rather than designing around it.

Campaign loop

The campaign is the run ("run" never means a battle), and battles are its floors. The rules are in docs/rules.md, the campaign part.

Decided

Losing the capital loses the run.

Everything in the run goes with it; only the pool, the codex, difficulty tiers, a Sponsorship piece and livery carry over (section 10).

Decided

Five cities.

The capital at the centre (the Council and the Arsenal) and four around it, each hosting a maker. A city's gate is its only health number and carries between battles.

Decided

The mission map is a choice of front, never city management.

A front's card names its domain and guest, its ground, the Convergence's tier and how long its gate holds if ignored; its formation is fuzzy until scouted.

Decided

The whole roster goes to one front; the fronts left alone bleed.

Ryan, 13 October: the intent, which the records had let blur, is to bring every machine to one front and fight it out while the other open fronts weaken their cities by attrition; the fronts keep moving and new ones open, so every front is tackled in time. Splitting the squad across two fronts was never the intention. A hangar larger than the squad is the answer to a battle that goes badly: spare chassis let the next front be fought without sitting the whole team out. The rules already say one battle a turn with up to four ready machines, and the campaign runner sends every ready machine to one front; what thins its team today is wrecks sitting out with no spares, which the hangar and the economy answer, and fights inside a battle that one machine fights alone (section 4's pacing row).

Decided

The capital's gate is the run's life bar.

Into the Breach's grid power: fallen cities drain it, drawn as hordes marching on the centre, and the finale is fought on what is left. Its repair is the one priced repair, so it weighs survival against the team.

Decided

A city falls when its gate reaches zero, fought or not.

It costs that battle's salvage and its maker's future blueprints, research and stock, never the player's kit; its rift stays open and its horde drains the capital.

Decided

No forced last stand, and a won battle restores the gate free.

The map keeps its one choice and no city runs on a one-way clock. The restore is free of salvage, not of Favour: the win's fee is docked a point per gate point lost, which is what pushers let through cost (section 3's tally row). The free restore is a lever for higher difficulty tiers.

Decided

A front is a rift whose domain is rolled as it opens.

Never fixed to a city: a fixed pairing would solve the map.

Decided

The Convergence makes the finale inevitable; finals are a choice.

Three bosses of one domain beaten draw its hero into a final, whose win beats that domain, gone from the rifts (the row below) and the finale (section 9). Four finals are impossible by design, so which domains to chase is always a choice: one or two the goal, three a feat.

Decided

A beaten domain's rifts close.

Ryan, 25 October: "if a domain gets beat, other rifts for that domain should close. That probably helps answer our questions on how to achieve a perfect run - closing off domains gets you the turn or two you need to make it possible. That's the difficult but achievable difference." His calls of 26 October: beaten means its final won, and every standing city's rift it leads closes then, a free reprieve: the city is quiet for a turn as a won city is ("if that is too easy then we take it away"), its gate stays as it stood ("Only a win. Again, a tuning point"), and nothing is paid for the closure ("You probably get paid extra for beating a domain final, though": the economy pass's number). The domain leads and guests no new rift, and keeps its place as the guest of a rift it was dealt into before. A fallen city's rift stays, as art: "there's no reason to interact with it anyway, so the domain it was doesn't matter and it shouldn't be selectible to find out". It also ends a second final of one domain refighting the hero it was beaten of (the mechanics audit). The exits: the quiet turn if the reprieve plays too easy, the gate if it should mend. Built 26 October.

Decided

The Rupture meter and Anathema fill by the threat-weighted tally.

Ryan, 13 October: what a foe is worth by its threat powers both the battle's meter and each domain's bar, because a count of fights or kills would make picking off small packs with flyers the optimal play and leave a strong foe worth seeking only for its drop. The gate is the other price on that play: a won battle restores the gate free, but the win's Favour is docked a point per gate point lost, so pushers let through cost the roster's growth; whether that bites is the dock rate and Favour's worth, the economy pass's numbers. Threat-weighted, a detachment's kills add little meter, so its worth is defence and tempo (section 4). The meter counts live during the battle; salvage still tallies at its end. Today a count of three bosses stands in for Anathema. Not built.

Decided

Difficulty grows by smarter foes and nastier compositions, never by inflating the rank and file.

Aims, conditions, behaviours, back ranks, elites and pack count, so upgrades buy adaptability against questions, not damage against hull. A boss, a final's hero and the finale's line are the exception (Ryan, 26 October, overruling "a new foe, never an old one with a bigger number" for them): their numbers scale with the battle's tier (section 9's boss row), since components gain power by tier and not only options and the columns are few ("we have a limited number of columns to stuff enemies in"), so "the numbers do need to scale to keep the challenge up, with both ramp and tier"; the rank and file never do ("I don't really want to have a 5hp goblin in tier 1 ramp 1 and a 50hp goblin in tier 3 ramp 10, that's lame"), and a promoted form keeps what its class change adds. A battle has one lead domain and at most one guest until the last tier, whose signature is anything from the roster. Section 9's recolour tier (the same foe repainted with a bigger number) is open against this; section 12.

Recommended

The curve by stage.

Ryan's goals, 23 October, general and kept only once tested (formation density or the rift timer may prove polarizing for some teams and be dropped as levers). Ryan, 24 October: three stages make early, middle and late easy to name but can make the difficulty spiky across the campaign, and he is not so convinced of the split; how the levers move along the run is section 12's. Early: simpler foes with basic mechanics, less dense formations, a longer rift timer, the domains' identities general leans, about one elite a battle, the harder-fight kind. Battlefield layer

The battlefield is the layer with positioning, so the skirmish has none. Its rules are in docs/rules.md, the battlefield part.

The field

Decided

A pointy-top hex grid nine wide, gate south, rift north.

Taller than a screen and scrolled, one unit to a hex. Nine is the thumb's limit, holds two fights apart and gives a detachment room; the two-and-two split it was sized for is an emergency now (the four-machines row), and nine stays until phone reads say the hexes are too small (Ryan, 13 October); pointy-top makes defensive lines straight rows; a hex is at least 48 px wide on screen.

Decided

Every map guarantees the horde a crossing at least three hexes wide, and one real water feature.

Holding every pack takes three machines. The water is a river with fords or a lake band. Height is landscape; no rule reads it.

Decided

Five map types, and the name is the intel.

The player reads a word, never a share. Mixed Ground (the base generator); Open Steppe (mostly open, a lake: wheels and the horde fly); Rubble Field (a ruins belt, the road its one lane: the walker's map); Badlands (crag ridges, tight crossings: the flyer's map); Flooded (a river, a lake, puddles: hover and tracks ford). Each passes "mismatch costs time, never ability". The type shows on the front card beside the domain and tier (not built: campaign battles all use Mixed Ground; the harness picks any).

Decided

Each way of moving calls each ground fast, normal or slow, and a slow hex ends the move.

No move buys through a stop, so every way keeps a good and a bad ground and the player never weighs two numbers. Moves follow size and role; the content check flags, never refuses, a ground chassis outpacing another way everywhere (flyers exempt).

Decided

Foes move binary.

The Final Fantasy overworld rule: broken ground delays only machines, and terrain is never a bonus to foes.

Decided

The player never does arithmetic on the map.

The map draws reach; a slow end hex is dashed with a stop sign in its middle (structure, not hue; the sign since Ryan asked, 25 October, what the dashes meant); move is a pip gauge with a slow last step hatched; the codex gives move in open-ground hexes; a terrain tap gives each way's word. Move rewards come in tens.

Decided

A pack is one sprite and a number badge.

Machines are their skirmish models with their mounts, turned the way they move; foes breathe and animate to attack; partly hidden objects get an outline.

Decided

City defences stand near the gate and can be targeted.

The gate's flanking guns first, turrets later; how they work is open (section 12). Not built.

Open

The multi-hex boss.

When and how a boss takes three hexes in a triangle again; the engine keeps the footprint, and the boss stands on one hex meanwhile.

The round

Decided

Packs show where they walk; you fight one fight at a time; survivors walk on.

The enemy never attacks a machine on the map: hunters catch machines, pushers threaten only the gate.

Decided

A move is tapped or drawn; an attack is an order.

Tap for the cheapest way, or draw the route. An order is a tap or drag onto a pack, and nothing opens until ENGAGE, so a mis-drop is harmless. No panels or toggles.

Decided

Moves are planned and played on ENGAGE or END TURN; UNDO runs in the order things were done.

Ryan, 25 October, from play: "should not need to wait for a move animation to complete before performing other actions. Actually, it's probably better to highlight and display the planned move like we used to, and then on engage or end turn actually animate and resolve, with engagement happening after the animations complete. Makes it easier to see who's moved." So a move shows as its route from where the machine stands, the next move or order reads from its end, and ENGAGE and END TURN play every planned move before the fight opens or the packs walk; a move into a hex another planned move left plays after it. And "undo should happen in global order, not on a per-unit basis. So the undo stack is stored in order, and undos in order, always", attack orders included (his "attack orders on the map should be able to undo"): an order with its move into range is one step. A fight is the stack's floor (Ryan: "yes"): what came before it has been played out and fought over, so UNDO reaches back to the last fight or the turn's start, where the per-machine undo took a machine back until it fought. Retired: a move walked at once, which made every next action wait on its walk, and UNDO MOVE by machine. Open, after these (his "resolve the other issues then let's look at this"): a move given in one go, or a clearer mark that a machine has move left, since the gauge boxes on a dragged route sit under the thumb.

Decided

The enemy's rings are always on the map, in red; a selected machine shows where it could attack from.

Ryan, 25 October, from play: "Enemy engagement ranges need to be a lot more clear. They need to be always visible, and become more visible when the enemy is tapped, and done in red not hud colour", and "when a unit is highlighted to move, it needs to show not only its available move spaces, but its available attacking spaces at the edge of that move, in red". Every pack's ring is a dashed red edge at rest, and the pack tapped (or one that would join the fight shown) has its ring tinted under a heavy solid edge with bright dashes crawling on it, in place of the display's-light reach a tap used to show. A selected machine's lit move is ringed by a red band, tinted under one solid edge, every hex within its attack range of where its move reaches, and a pack it could be ordered onto is bracketed. The two reds part by structure as well as hue (a dashed edge against a filled band; the move's lit cells against one band), since Ryan is colourblind. The hex grid's resting light came down a fifth so the marks stand out ("base hex outline brightness can be reduced a bit"). A hunted machine wears a tag in place of the orange target under it (Ryan: "a tag hovering by the unit with the hunter's portrait (probably cropped) and some kind of target symbol"): a plate a hunter, up and left of the machine, the hunter's map picture and a target turning. Exit: his read on the phone.

Decided

The button pulses once nothing else is left to do.

Ryan, 25 October: "when no actions remain in a skirmish turn (excluding rerolls), end turn should pulse. Same with the map phase, when all units are out of actions". In a fight, once no die has anywhere to be spent, rerolls left or not; on the map, once every machine has fought, is ordered, is caught, or can neither move nor reach a pack where it stands (Ryan agreed: nothing useful left, "since technically that incorporates eventual commander actions if they appear"), so ENGAGE pulses too when orders wait. Rings breathe out past the button and its face brightens, by motion as well as light; RIFTGATE_PULSE_DEBUG=1 keeps it pulsing to judge it running.

Decided

A machine moves before it fights, never after.

A fight spends its action however it ends, so a hunter pins what it catches until the team kills it. The reason is the pin; a machine that moves after it fights is pinned below, not rejected, and a passive could bend this as a one-off.

Decided

Four machines, moving as one; a detachment is a decision priced by time.

Ryan, 13 October, after the one-to-four measurement (docs/measurements.md): the squad stays four, and the fights come in sizes, formations for the body and small packs that one or two machines take for a tenth of their hull. Concentration halves the hull a fight costs with every machine added, so the squad moves as one by default; a detachment is a decision the map prices with time (the gate under pushers, a flank pack, the meter) and the skirmish withdrawal bails out. The roster needs detachment-capable identities among its fast machines: the cars cannot fight alone and the Honey Badger can, which balancing for real measures as solo value beside team value for every chassis. The stock pass's bands (22 October) read a team of three meeting its decisions only in the formations and a machine alone meeting walls; the numbers stay (Ryan, 23 October, agreeing with Claude's lean): the tanks' base armour walling the rank and file's blows is the armour row working as decided (section 5), three machines against one wildlife pack is the mismatch this row sizes the fights against, and the sizes are the foes' fresh start's. It reopens if the fresh start's fights still hand a squad free wins. The map stays nine wide (the grid row).

Decided

Fights are picked on the map, by ring and range.

Packs join a fight by their ring (front-rank foes small, back-rank foes large); machines by their attack range (two hexes); a hunter that ends its walk with its prey in its ring forces that fight first. So formation and the order of attack set each fight's composition. The radii and the range are placeholders for play.

Decided

The boss is attacked like any pack, and its fight takes every machine and cannot be left.

It guards the rift, immune until the Rupture meter fills (Ryan, 24 October; the seal is not built). Its fight opens with the reveal and the domain's boss theme. Ryan, 25 October, from play: "The boss fight should automatically make all units attacking when one unit targets it, regardless of their range. Disable withdraw for the boss fight - the player is committed to closing the rift or getting scrapped trying. That sorts out the defense issue, and there's no reason to move any units once engage is clicked, just go right into the boss animation. Doesn't matter if a unit has fought this turn already, the only way that would actually happen is 1) the player is greeding for farm, 2) a hunter needs to get resolved first, which is fair." So one order onto the boss brings every machine, ENGAGE goes straight to the reveal, and the fight ends the battle, the rift sealed or the team scrapped. A fight the boss joins by its ring is its fight too, so a machine sent at a pack beside the boss is never locked in alone with it, and the packs whose rings cover the boss join its fight as any fight's do (Ryan, 26 October: "I think it's correct since it forces the player to think about when to engage, but if it's really unbalanced we can re-evaluate"; the boss joining from its own ring is "fine for the same reason, and that can be fixed by removing the boss ring entirely if needed"). Retired: withdrawing from the boss's fight (13 October), kept then because a lock read as the forced last stand section 3 refuses and the withdrawal already cost what it should; Ryan's commitment to the rift settles it, and engaging the boss is now the battle's last decision. What reopens it: a boss that is fought more than once (a regrowing boss, the Rupture re-sealing).

Open

A battle withdrawal: not encouraged, not yet refused.

Ryan, 13 October: never built, by intent, since what the gate then takes is hard to evaluate and easy to abuse, and he would rather not encourage it; then, the same day, that a team wipe already grades a lost battle, so a withdrawal may be possible. Claude's proposal: the map prices it. To withdraw, the machines walk out through the gate: hunters catch stragglers, pushers strike the gate while they retreat, Rift Pressure spawns, flyers leave cheap because that is what flyers are, and a team deep north pays six turns for leaving late. The city then takes the wipe's unopposed phases; the machines are not wrecked. A player forced to play out a twenty-minute loss is the helplessness section 2 refuses. Ryan to decide. Ryan, 13 October, later: the walk-out makes thematic sense; decided later.

Decided

Damage sticks.

Hull never repairs during a battle and damage never slows a machine. The battle is a race to close the rift; blitz or slow and steady, even a defender left at the gate, is the player's call.

Decided

One clock, Rift Pressure, ticking on the map and never in a fight.

It spawns a pack at the rift when full, in place of any cap on a fight. Ryan, 13 October: the tick per round fought went. It stood in for the round cap he refused, but it could not tell an epic fight from a stall, taxed the five-to-eight-round fights the pacing wants, and punished care invisibly, since nothing in the fight said a pack was forming; measured the same day (docs/measurements.md), it had been spawning packs into the plain player's long solo fights. A long fight now costs one map turn like any other. The guard against a stall is enemy damage outpacing sustain (section 5), the runner marking fights past sixty rounds, and a backstop held in reserve and not built: pressure ticking late in a fight, from round eight or so, and saying so in the HUD. A tick per fight is the lever not pulled: it would price many small fights against one big one, turn-efficient against hull-efficient, and the game starts without it. Built 13 October.

Skirmish layer

The skirmish is where the depth lives. Its rules (the round, dice, reach, keywords, buffs and debuffs, mitigation, heat, injury, foes) are in docs/rules.md; this section says why they are so.

Shape

Recommended

The player side has no positioning, and the round is one interleaved phase.

Roll, lock, spend and reroll in any order before the enemy acts; the interleave is what damage ranges earn. A zoomed-in hex grid was set aside: it repeats the battlefield's decision and fights thumbs in portrait. Built and played.

Decided

The enemy stands in two ranks of four, never wider.

A phone-readability cap. Ranks are enemy-side only, bodies in front and problems behind, and they hold.

Decided

Foes strike in the order they stand, and the heaviest hitters deploy left.

No initiative numbers.

Decided

Reinforcements fill gaps at the end of each enemy turn, and a kill clears its slot at once.

A brief window on the back rank.

Open

Ways for the player to act on the reinforcement phase.

Decided

No round limit.

A cap punishes defensive and status builds, and so did a Rift Pressure tick per round (section 4). Withdrawal and tuning keep a fight finite, enemy damage outpacing sustain, with a meaningful fight five to eight rounds (section 4's pacing row); a stall, which the runner marks past sixty rounds, is a number to tune, never a clock.

Decided

Withdrawal is open at any point of the player's turn, and always costs one enemy turn.

Nothing may skip that turn: forced contact is bad strategy, not bad luck.

Decided

The enemy's turn replays blow by blow.

Parting blows included, before the next dice or the outcome. The beat is the ENEMY PACE setting.

Decided

Every kill counts, however a fight ends.

Toward salvage and the Rupture meter. Defeat wrecks the machines (section 3).

Weapons

Decided

A weapon is rolled or flat by its niche.

Flat is reliable (a flat blow kills a foe of its number every time, a range only some of the time) and the finisher; rolled is not, and is the opener and the boss chipper. Ryan, 13 October: heat is a resource and a balancing attribute beyond damage, the secondary cost beside a cooldown (section 8), and like reach it is set by the component's niche. Rolled or flat and hot or cold are four corners, never two pairs: flat weapons making heat and rolled ones none was the old kinetic and energy classes' split, never the rule, and the stock pass priced heat by niche. Where a flat weapon's average matches a rolled one's, the reliability is paid for somewhere, heat the usual place, or flat becomes the default; where it does not, heat is free to say something else about the piece.

Decided

No weapon classes, no crits, no Execute, no Steady.

Each failed a test of section 2, and the test is the rule: crits put a chance on the damage; Execute was a binary kill; Steady was an average, a number dressed as a rule; classes asked for parity. A new idea that is none of those is free to be proposed. Chances live only on riders. Tags carry no rule of their own (section 7). Damage that grows with the target's missing hull is a unique rule on one piece today (the Coriolis Accelerator); a second carrier would make it a keyword, as with the Boar's spill. A foe that gets back up is answered by Suppress, Disrupt and kill order.

Decided

Two reaches, and no falloff.

Direct reaches the back only through a hole; Indirect reaches anyone. A third, Close, was a worse Direct whose price existed only against a back rank: two archetypes are plenty. Melee is a tag; the old close weapons came down to Direct's band with the stock pass.

Decided

Reach is priced.

At equal heat Direct hits harder than Indirect, unless the runner shows heat is the better price for Indirect: the numbers carry the price (measured 11 October, docs/measurements.md).

Decided

Indirect is reach only, and area belongs to a keyword.

Ryan, 11 October, agreeing the line-up's proposal: danger close goes, so a beam or a phased emitter can be Indirect without exploding, and an explosive says so with an area keyword on any reach; danger close priced nothing and only steered Indirect toward the back. Gone since 22 October, the Artillery Rangefinder's rule that spared it replaced by the Ram Frame's mirror (the catalogue row); which explosives carry the area keywords is the row below's.

Open

Area by shape, never by strength.

Ryan, 11 and 12 October: three shapes, three keywords: Penetrate the column, Spread the row (the neighbours, never the whole row, so the aim still matters) and Splash the plus; one rule for all, the target taking the blow and every other foe reached half, never built: the engine hits every foe reached in full (section 12). His condition held (measured 12 October, docs/measurements.md: an explosive needs no price on the firer), so Splash folds into Spread and Penetrate and an explosive carries both; if the plus proves too strong where back ranks stand, the lever is its number or an explosive with one shape, never a price on the firer. Chain stays the kill's shape, the single-target machine's answer to a swarm; area is no part of Kestrel's brief, so the Kite is never balanced around it. The column keywords earn their keep once the foes' fresh start puts back ranks in more fights. Open: which explosives carry the two (section 12); none does since the stock pass, whose tables gave no shell both (the Airburst Shell sprays; the Musk Ox's two locked pieces carry both).

Decided

The whole die is in view in the codex; holding a die shows the entry showing.

A reroll needs the whole die in view, so the codex, a double-tap on the machine, lists every entry; the hold turns the entry showing into text, a number a passive moved highlighted there and never its source listed (the tap-hold-drag row, Ryan, 25 and 26 October); passives belong to the machine.

Control and statuses

Decided

Burn never stops healing.

Damage over time soft-counters healing, taking back some or all of what is healed.

Decided

The Pocket Paradox is a meter.

It counts applications of other statuses, never stacks, so tripping it takes several components or a hit carrying several statuses, and it tunes by its threshold. A chance to suppress was binary; a debuff that zeroes damage was toxic against fodder and bosses alike. A trip that Numbed resists still spends the meter, so it never sits primed to try again, and so does a trip on a foe already suppressed (Ryan, 17 October: "Suppressed is suppressed - they lose their next action. It does not stack, it does not carry. That way lies madness"). Its existence never dictates another design: a status that feeds it counts, and the count is tuned for the final ecosystem (Ryan, 17 October).

Decided

Numbed is a buff nothing removes, and nothing touches it.

A buff with a cannot-be-removed line, not a kind of its own (Ryan, 18 October: its own kind was one type too many, "no reason to have a billion types of objects"), which nothing reads, deepens, spreads, cleanses or purges: a stun resistance that effects can change is where other games' combat loops broke. It decays, so rotating stuns is a slope, not a wall, and its slope says spread the stuns. No foe resists by nature: the chip shows who resists and for how long, and a phone has no room for an innate figure. If a sure first stun on a boss proves too strong, this is revisited. A suppress on a foe already suppressed for that action does nothing and numbs it no further (Ryan, 17 October: Suppressed never stacks and never carries, a Paradox's trip included). Built 17 October.

Decided

A status stacks or renews, as its definition says.

Ryan, 17 October: renewal without stacking must be supported, "a pretty common thing for a powerful or non-number-related buff to do". The engine reads the policy off the status: add, the default, a stack a grant, a timed grant keeping its own end (Ryan, 16 October: "Timed stacks should decay based on when they started. Not stack and refresh."); renew, one grant whose end is the later and whose count the larger (Suppressed, Retaliate; and the untimed one-stack statuses, on which a renewal changes nothing: never stacking and renewing are one state, parted only by a timer). Section 6's table checks its stacking word against the policy. A status either runs down to zero on its own clock or is timed, never both (Ryan, 18 October: "Things either decrease to zero, or are timed, not both"), and the validator refuses rounds on a halving status. Built 17 and 18 October.

Decided

Armoured and Overcharged count in points, and Overcharged halves.

Ryan, 17 and 18 October. Both sides say Armoured, and a stack is an armour point, so the chip's count is the armour ("Easy to read at one glance"); its duration is the row below's. Overcharged is a damage point a stack and halves at the end of each round, rounded down, whatever granted it, until it is gone: a stack fading a round let a source feeding two a round climb without limit, where a halving one settles near twice its feed ("to prevent stacking to the moon and fueling massive ramp strategies"), and Ryan would grant it to more things on that clock. Rounded down, because a status decays to zero or is timed, never both (the row above): rounded up, one stack would never end. The Ammit's Quintessence Tap keeps every Overcharged on it for the battle whatever granted it (Ryan, 18 October: "that's the rules text, it doesn't care where it came from"), never between battles, everything resetting at the hangar; its release valve, should it need one, is an enemy silence or purge (Ryan: ok), and nothing else is considered unless the Ammit "proves stupid overpowered". The Overcharge Coil grants 2 (Ryan, 18 and 22 October: +10 for a round was "silly high" for a support action spent the same turn as a combo, and at 1 the halving leaves next to nothing). The halving stays the clock of every status fed by events (Ryan, 22 October): a stack a round climbs without limit under a steady feed, so one clock everywhere would need a cap on every feed by another name. Built 18 October. Reopens if the count moving by halves reads badly on the chip in play, or if a buff wants a run-down the halving cannot give.

Decided

Burn is a numbered keyword, and Burning a damage point a stack.

Ryan, 18 October: "It should be fixed to a numbered keyword, like a weapon applies 'Burn 2' for 2 stacks, irregardless of damage. Also, in the spirit of flattening numbers, burn should only do 1 damage per stack." So a Burn weapon says its stacks (Burn 3: three stacks of Burning on each foe hit, whatever the blow dealt), the first numbered keyword; a foe's Burning loses a damage point a stack at its action, so Burn 3 is 3, 2, 1 over three actions. On a machine a stack is a heat point (section 11's scales row). Each piece's number is content's. Built 18 October.

Decided

Stun is the best mechanic in any game, so adding hard control is always weighed first.

Ryan, 13 October: Suppress is prevalent and needs dialling back, and designs kept reaching for a stun or a burn in place of build space. The roster analysis's caps (no hard-control piece below tier two, one locked on a die, one hard-control passive a maker) were struck as artificially limiting (Ryan, 15 October) for a rule: adding hard control is always carefully considered, and other options explored, before it is suggested or committed to. Ryan, 17 October: a cooldown is no price for a Suppress, since Numbed is in effect one (on one foe the landed rate settles near one stun in three rounds however often it is tried); the more mitigation the game adds, the more Suppress must be priced so the other options stay attractive, since scarcity would make its carriers must-picks; without hard control a tank can become required, so damage must stay a viable way to win, Suppress may be allowed more widely if gated by a condition worked for or a price paid, and off-tank mitigation (a Barrier) is part of the answer. Ryan, 18 October, on what prices it: not the damage it deals, which is incidental, since a stun's worth is a turn where the party is not under threat (Darkest Dungeon's lesson, taken less far); so a Suppress is gated by a condition, a setup, a self-sacrifice or its timing, and the player decides whether to build a team that makes it worthwhile. The Paradox is the model. Retired: a Suppress of no damage (Claude's), which priced the stun where it is weakest, on fodder, and not where it is strongest, on a boss.

Decided

Shown and committed. Mitigation, heat and injury

Decided

A shield absorbs its number, and the excess passes to the hull.

Ryan, 14 October. Under the old rule (a standing shield blocked a whole blow, however small) a small shield entry was secretly "negate one blow", worth the biggest blow it met rather than its number, and the content kept rediscovering it (small stand-up shields; a screen on every ally negating four blows for one spend). Now a shield is an honest number, a breach ignores it outright (the shield stands), and the one-blow block is a priced, single-target buff of its own (the Barrier, below). What follows: shield answers the big blow by its size, not by standing; the Peak's telegraph wants a shield sized to it or a Barrier; the Buzzard's regen and the Puffin's overshield are hit points. Shield returns only at the field rate out of fights, so withdrawing is no reset. Built 21 October.

Decided

Barrier and Ward: the block is a buff, one machine at a time.

Ryan, 14 October, the upside of the honest shield. A Barrier on a machine means the next blow a foe lands on it deals no damage, so it never injures, until the round's end; its riders land, and a blow of nothing leaves it standing; a blow it takes shocks no striker. A Ward means no rider a foe lays lands on it that round, a blow's or a move's of its own, breach included; a blow's damage lands (Ryan, 25 October: "Should stop all riders, even from non-damaging attacks", after the mechanics audit found it passing every special by, the Peak's Eruption, Fira and Pyroclasm, the Drake's Stoke and the Summoner's Diamond Dust among them). Both up is the old whole block at twice its price. They answer different shapes, the Barrier the Peak's telegraph and a boss's blow, the Ward the Waste's breach and the Peak's scorch, so a team carries what its fronts ask. A Barrier is never granted to every ally at once: four blows negated for one spend is the exploit the shield rule removed. The Ward lasts the round because riders come in volume where the big blow comes alone (Ryan), and its worth is thin while few domains lay riders on machines, which the foes' fresh start may change. Ward is the buff's word, so the Oblation Ward is the Oblation Mantle and the Votive Ward the Votive Screen (Ryan, 15 October). Ryan, 17 October: Barrier and Ward want the same care as Suppress, and if a Barrier grows common the foes need more damaging riders to carry past it (damage over time, on-hit effects, a countdown's blow, a Barrier's intended prey). The carriers since the stock pass (22 October): the Blast Door (Minerva's) grants the Barrier and the Decoherence Veil (Aphelion's, a working name) the Ward, self or ally, tier one, in no stock. Built 21 October.

Decided

Armour is a flat number off every blow, set by role, and a blow it covers is nothing.

Shield answers the big blow, armour the swarm, and straight reduction stays the rule on both sides: a blow at or under the armour does nothing, a bigger one loses the armour, never a damage floor. Ryan, 21 and 22 October, at the single-digit scale (section 11), where a point of plate walls a whole class of blow. Base armour is priced by role, never by size ("I would price it based on role"): a tank 2, a heavy worker 1, a car, a hover, a flyer or a glass cannon 0; timed armour (a brace until the round's end, the Armadillo's seal until it opens) lifts a unit well past its base and is meant to wall ("armour numbers that are only reachable conditionally should allow complete blocks"). A standing point is the dear one, since it walls one more blow size for the whole game, so module armour is scarce, +1 at most, tier two or three, and paid in a big stat. The forms tried and the test each failed, so a new one is free to be proposed: a cap at half the blow (no wall anywhere, and not sharp enough at low armour for Ryan); the same cap with a Braced state walling ("Might as well have no base armour and the flat armour stack as an ability"); a window that stops a blow up to the armour and nothing at twice it ("armour is useless against a boss"); the armour squared over the blow ("the math is cryptic. You need a chart to understand it"); a percentage a point (it rounds to nothing at single digits); a saving throw (randomness the player cannot see or shift; Ryan: no); base armour folded into hull with Armoured the only armour (the Elemental's plate by nature goes with it). What answers a wall is the pack, not the rule: a breaching move (the breach row) and a rally's Kindled lifting a small blow over the plate, while a walled tank takes only its share of the blows unless it lures (a foe without an aim picks at random, section 5's aim row), so tanking is lure plus plate. On our side Flak and Overcharged add before armour, Shock doubles before it, Exposed sets it to nothing, Burning and Vitriol pass it. What reopens it: the tank rows reading as free wins against packs with no breacher, or the bracing foes' cells reading as walls for a basics team, in the runner's bands.

No keyword the player learns while it is the Boar's alone (a second source granting it would make it one): the engine runs it as Spillover, a keyword the passive grants every weapon the Boar fires, written out on the die (the star, the passive's line in the hold, no chip). A carry past a kill stays the Boar's: a multi-hit weapon strikes its one target, because that is intuitive (Ryan). Breach is not the spill's word: it is one rider on both sides (the breach row). Built 15 and 22 October.

Decided

What it costs: the Waste becomes the anti-mitigation domain outright, the Barrier and the Ward its dear answers; plate keeps its showcase in every swarm that does not breach. The foes' carriers: the Waste the most, one mid foe in each other domain by fantasy, bosses mostly not, so the seal keeps its job against the big single blows. Built 22 October.

Recommended

Shields are a buffer, and heat is the second resource.

A typical pack wears a shield down in two or three rounds; the Barrier is the timer now, one blow and one turn; heat is a team problem.

Decided

Machines cool at the start of the round and overheat at the end of the player's turn.

So a hot machine can vent first, and an overheat is the machine's own doing on its own turn: with volatile, the one way to die on your own turn. A withdrawal ends the turn too, so running hot and leaving is no way round the burn. A foe adds no heat outright: its Burning lands at the end of the player's turn, before the overheat, and its Scorched heats only the weapons fired (below). An overheat is hull damage for every passive. Cooling stays well under a weapon's heat.

Decided

Heat bites where it is carried; a cold build pays elsewhere.

Ryan, 13 October. Heat is the one cost that competes with damage on the same die (a cooldown is the other, printed on the pieces that carry one: section 8), and what makes rolled against flat a choice; priced per piece but never felt, it is decoration. Before the stock pass it was barely felt outside the Peak; the stock pass's loops lowered the maxima so the hot kits bite and the cold ones stay cold (22 October, docs/measurements.md). The test, Claude's placeholders until play: a build that runs hot overheats by about its third round of firing without a vent, so a formation fight forces a cooling turn or two, by vent or by cool weapon, and a short fight forces none; a cold build is a healthy build and pays somewhere else, in a rolled range, a short reach, a lower number or fewer strikes, never for free, and collects in the long fights; the Peak's Burning and Scorched press a resource the hot builds already feel. Ryan's caveat: heat is not judged from a lack of content, so whether it bites is judged once the roster has hot builds to feel it. A cool weapon is also soft cooling (Ryan, 13 October): spending it instead of a hotter one is a cooldown turn, so heat builds are a spectrum from a kit tuned to its passive cooling that never vents to a hot kit that buys its heat back with vent turns. Heat is a budget (the maximum plus cooling times the rounds) against a demand (what the damage-optimal spends would add); the efficient build ends near the maximum, which is good play. The worst case for balance is slack: a kit with the hot build's damage that ends the fights that matter with budget unused never paid for its damage, and everything converges on it. What the runner reads, per row, spikes rather than actions: peak heat as a share of the maximum and the rounds above four fifths of it; overheats and the hull they burned; forced choices, where the best-damage entry would have crossed the maximum; the damage forgone to heat (near zero on a hot kit is the worst case); and the slack left at a fight's end. The levers are the maxima, cooling, the weapons' heat and the vents' numbers.

Decided

Foes heat machines only through debuffs: Burning and Scorched.

Heat applied straight is so polarising that it needs several avenues of counterplay, or it spirals. Ryan, 12 and 13 October: direct heat goes, and two debuffs carry it, the player's words reused and acting differently on a machine. Burning, the harsher, adds a heat point a stack at the end of the player's turn, before the overheat, then loses a stack, so the player can vent the heat already there or cleanse it: two answers. Metal does not burn, it heats: one word with two effects by body, the hold saying which, and the first debuff that means something on either side, which an Arsenal cleanse moving debuffs onto a foe would need (section 7). Scorched, the softer, makes every weapon the machine fires run a point hotter a stack until the end of the player's turn, when every stack goes, so equipment or a cool weapon plays round it; the die shows the hotter heat. Both cleanse. A move on every machine takes the softer, since four machines ticking at once is the spiral direct heat had (Claude's fill, Ryan's to move; which foe lays which is content's). Burning on a machine has its own glyph (Ryan, 13 October): Burn's flame filled solid with the thermometer cut out of it, apart from the overheat's bare thermometer and Primed's hollow flame. Heat reads adds before multiplies, as a blow's damage does, so a multiplier on a gun's heat multiplies Scorched's extra too. Retired: Scorched losing a stack a turn, which piled up under several scorchers. Built 13 October. Ryan, 24 October: heat needs to be more threatening, and because the heat debuffs can be countered, with multiple release valves (a vent, a cleanse, a cool spend round Scorched, the Ward, the caster killed or suppressed), they can be significantly buffed; a foe whose jam is heat tries to heat when no machine is hot, and one may aim at the hottest to overheat it, as a boss would try to execute an easy target. The first cut doubles every foe heat stack, its riders numbered as the player's Burn is (24 October; docs/measurements.md): the cold teams feel the Peak now and the hottest starters are walled on the greedy bot, so Ryan's play judges it, and a wall that survives a player working the valves brings the stacks down a step. Exit: the Buzzard's loss (the old direct tick paid its shield every round) and the Peak's numbers are Ryan's play to judge.

The die

Decided

A die entry reads at a glance from fixed places.

The primary glyph top centre (what it does when spent), the number beneath, heat bottom left, its place on the die top left, a chip per keyword down the right edge, a star chip for a unique effect. Its copies are not (Ryan, 15 October): skipping them too was abusable, since a die of one key entry and copies of one filler sent every reroll from a filler onto the key, so a copy comes up at the odds its copies give it, and the entry's numeral shows that the die moved. The vocabulary

Decided

A keyword and the buff or debuff it creates share a name.

Ryan, 16 October, a global convention: "unless there's a really good reason not to, if a keyword creates a buff or debuff, the keyword and the chip should have the same name." So a foe's brace leaves Armoured, the machines' status (Ryan: "If it's effectively braced, it should be braced too"). The rule binds the words the player reads to their chips; a foe's effect in content (rally, revive once) is no keyword and takes its chip's name. The good reason to differ: one status from many sources keeps its own name, as Overcharged does from the Overcharge Coil, the Soulcutter Talons and the Grievance Reliquary. Still open (section 12): Kindled, the same effect as Overcharged on the other side; and Reraise, which takes a name of ours under the homage rule. Built 16 October.

Decided

A foe has HP; a machine has hull.

Ryan, 22 October: "hull is the label for a machine, not for a goblin." The shown text says HP of a foe and hull of a machine, the vocabulary has both, and heat stays one word, since only machines carry it; the content's and the engine's field stays hp, its comments changing as they are touched. 7

Mechs and the builder

A loadout is a real identity: MechWarrior's build depth, simplified, with no weight classes and no gated equipment. The words: a component is one piece of equipment and exactly one entry on the die; a hardpoint is a free slot; a chassis's fixed components are built in and use no hardpoint; a module sits inside the machine and is never spent.

Machines and the die

Decided

Chassis come in any shape and differ by plain numbers.

The silhouette is a terrain trait (section 4), so a team is picked for the map as well as the fight.

Decided

Four squad slots; a run starts with three machines and buys the fourth.

A real choice between splitting up and staying together.

Decided

The die is the equipment.

Fixed components plus hardpoints, one entry each. A small die is consistent; a big one carries more but shows each entry less often (a thin deck as a chassis stat). Fixed components are never removed or bought open, because restriction makes builds fun.

Decided

Hardpoints are untyped.

The die is already the slot limit (a shield entry costs a gun entry on the same roll), and a niche the player can leave is an invitation.

Pinned

What a hand holds: locked, or a hand that takes more.

Ryan, 12 October: a gauntlet on the Nemesis's hip cannon loses too much immersion; tabled, the fists back as the Nemesis's locked piece. The shape he meant, if it returns: a hand as a superset, never a restriction. A hardpoint on a hand takes anything a mount takes and also what only a hand can hold (a gauntlet, a sword, a handheld rifle, a shield on the arm); a mount takes no hand-held piece; nobody loses a build and a chassis with hands gains the fantasy. It pays only with enough chassis with hands: almost every Arsenal chassis, many of Aphelion's, few of Minerva's and Wainwright & Sutler's, none of Kestrel's. It also raised a word: most players read Melee as a sword (the tags row).

Decided

A chassis's design is its passive, its numbers and its fixed components.

The stock in its hardpoints is generic kit the player replaces, so a passive must work with generic kit, and no machine has one straight-line build.

Decided

Toughness is not slots.

A tank is tough through its numbers and passive, never through a locked component that is only a stat line (plating by another name); its locked component is what only it can do, and a locked shield that does something passes (the Puffin's Backwash Ionizer). If a machine proves both unkillable and hard-hitting, the fix is its hull, not its slots.

Decided

One passive, one idea.

Nameable in a phrase (an upside and its cost count as one), at most three rule lines: a double-edged niche definer (the Kite) or a plain multiplier fantasy.

Decided

A chassis's identity must open build space.

Its passive, numbers and locked components together, never a convenient hole filled or a nice-to-have. A passive that only feeds its own fixed components adds nothing: it might as well sit on the components. The interesting part is how the player gets there: the Nemesis banks rage on any hull damage, so overheating on purpose, or anything that hurts it, becomes a build. A map rule has its place as a passive, but on its own it is a weak identity. Ryan, 15 October, keeping two of the roster analysis's tests: a new chassis earns its place by a new read (a state nobody reads), a new product (a state nobody produces), a new price, or a change to what a kind of piece does, since niches can and should overlap across a larger roster and the test reads the mechanics (a mechanic is its pattern in its context, never one trait it shares, such as a random target: Ryan, 16 October, "if you say 'can't do this thing because that already kind of does it,' then you quickly run out of things"); and a passive aims to be fed by, and to feed, other makers' pieces, ideally two routes in and two payoffs, an aspiration and never a pass or fail.

Recommended

A passive multiplies or redirects what components already do.

A verb several components share belongs on them as a keyword (so overshield is one).

Decided

A passive may break a rule, as a one-off.

That is the point of a passive: the rule bends for one machine and holds for every other, so the player learns the rule once and the exception where it lives.

Decided

A weapon is spent on a foe; equipment on a machine.

A weapon need not deal damage. Equipment (a shield, a vent, a buff) says whom it reaches. A buff grants one of the machines' own buffs; a debuff on a foe stays a weapon.

Decided

A component is intact or destroyed, for the whole battle.

Ryan, 25 October: "Cracked is gone." The mild state went, and with it the mechanics audit's finding that a crack made a Volatile piece better (its hull price a keyword, stripped with the rest). An injury destroys the entry the die lands on, at a quarter of the maximum hull. The threshold is set by what it asks of the player, never by a loss rate or a win rate (Ryan, the same day: "It's not about win rate, it's about forcing player choices and balancing compositions"): half, set to keep the two states' losses, was retired within the day, since a machine could lose but one piece before it fell (docs/measurements.md). A wreck loses only what was destroyed, as any machine does: its price is being wrecked and out for a battle (Ryan: "That's pretty big"), and if it needs to bite more, the levers are the recovery time or a cost. The Honey Badger's "never destroyed" holds every injury off until its identity session moves it to a module (the open Badger row). What reopens it: a fight that wants a mild injury again.

The codex has base numbers and battle-long rewards, never a combat effect.

Decided

Decided

Every machine has its own idle acts and its own movement, on both screens.

Decided

Machines are piloted, not animals.

An act is what a pilot does: looks round through the head, turrets or sensors, flexes limbs and weapons, test-charges a weapon, shuffles impatiently. Cockpits are fixed, except the Clydesdale's head; the Ammit's head is still by design.

Decided

Heavy and mechanical, with inertia.

MechWarrior or Star Wars, not Gundam or Evangelion. Every part chases its act on a servo slightly under critical damping, timed by the machine's heft; a blow is keyed as a slam that accelerates into the hit and stops dead.

Decided

Acts are loud and never in step.

Each moves several joints and the body at once, with glows hot enough to read across the map on a phone. A machine picks acts at random, never the same twice running, with long rests between.

Decided

A grounded machine stands still between acts.

Only a flyer rides a slow swell; a hovercraft balances like a platform on water, tipping in gusts, catching itself and settling with a heave.

Decided

Turrets turn slowly, not far, and pause before turning back.

At most 70 degrees from the bow, 35 degrees a second, and a hold of 0.6 seconds before reversing; lowforge refuses an act that breaks this. Under way a turret turns with the hull.

Decided

Walkers stomp and plod.

Decided

A move on the map is one smooth curve with real acceleration.

Decided

Each Fracture of the Rupture meter grants the reward pick.

Unweighted by domain (section 4).

Decided

Cooldowns in a skirmish: a printed property on specific pieces.

Ryan, 14 October, after the roster analysis weighed it (its section 7): a cooldown is a printed property on a specific piece, never a general mechanic, shown on the entry as dark with the turns left, so it is paid in the die's reliability and read like any other rule; he designed the funnel-web's web bomb and the Gundam's parry with it the same day. Meta and resources

What a run earns and spends, and what outlives it. The numbers in sim/campaign.gd are placeholders until the economy pass.

Decided

Two currencies, split by what earns them.

Salvage (materials), from kills and loot, makes things. Favour (the Council's fee), from won battles docked for gate damage, buys services and stock. A loot detour earns salvage but risks Favour. A resource per domain would let the map solve itself, so there is none.

Decided

A kill is a kill.

Every foe killed pays salvage however its fight ends, tallied only at the battle's end, so there is no count to farm against. Kills are to be weighted by danger, like the Rupture meter. Hit-and-run farming is accepted: a withdrawal gives the enemy its hit, the survivors return whole, and the fight cost a map action.

Decided

Loot lies on the map.

Wrecks and alien remains, common, seeded with the map, claimed by ending a move on one. Each holds salvage plus components or, in the rarest kind, a chance at a blueprint (modules too, never a research token). A tap shows its kind and likely contents, never the contents: the decision is the detour. Not built.

Open

The loot's shown names and density.

Working names Derelict, Carcass, Relic; never "chest".

Decided

Only a blueprint builds.

For salvage, without limit; any other component is one copy. No chassis blueprints (unlimited machines flatten the roster), and a locked component is never a blueprint.

Decided

Tiers are depth, and a maker never maps to a tier, with one exception.

A chassis's tier is complexity and a component's is power (below). Any maker may field a tier three. The one exception, a chassis rule only (Ryan, 13 October): the Arsenal fields no tier-one chassis and makes no basics, because it is the Council's trickle (section 1), and it is never strictly better, or it becomes the endgame chase; its tier-one components exist, and the Nemesis stocks them under "a chassis stocks only below its own tier" with no rule changed. A run starts with three tier-one machines, four squad slots and the basics' blueprints (tier zero).

Decided

A chassis's tier is complexity, a component's power; modules are flat.

Ryan, 12 October, agreeing Claude's lean: a chassis's tier is a sharper passive and more locked pieces, a higher ceiling and a lower floor, never more power, so the starting machines stay viable all run; with unlocked components free to move, a new chassis is a new sandbox if it is compelling enough. A component's tier is power: blueprints are the growth curve and the basics are meant to be replaced. Modules are flat, their worth in fit and their count capped by slots. Blueprints of tiers one to three are found, or their components come on a bought chassis of tier two or three. A tier-one machine's stock is basics beside its locked piece, Slay the Spire's opening (Strikes and Defends beside a card of its own); most tier ones lock one, and a tier one shows its identity with basics alone, with a locked piece or without (Ryan, 15 October, keeping the roster analysis's test). Ryan, later on 12 October, agreeing Claude's proposal: a component carries its tier in its entry, and a chassis stocks only below its own tier (the validator holds it), so tier one needs no special case and a tier-three component is only ever found, free to be a real heavy hitter or a game changer. Tier three holds the most unique effects and the most raw power, tier two most of the synergy pieces, so no build has to farm tier three for its capstone. Ryan, 24 October: tier three is for "highlight, chase pieces", and four machines in all tier-three gear is not "a healthy endgame", so the run deals it as a chance (by the rift's tier, in docs/curve_proposal.md). A bought chassis brings one copy of each component it stocks, free upper-tier pieces to play with, never their blueprints (owning a component never teaches building it, 7 October; only a blueprint builds, above).

Open

Modules as a priced stat bundle and a priced rule?

Ryan, 12 October: what if a module were a random stat bundle and a random rule, each priced? Claude: possible, and the prices (below) are the tool. Generated to one budget, a rule's price taken from it and the rest spent on stats, no module is bigger than another, only shaped differently, so an offer of three asks which fits rather than which is best, the decision rolled stats lack. The holes: a rule's price is an average over machines (the Booster is worth nothing on a flat kit and a great deal on a rolled one), so fit decides, which is the point; the generator must not pair a rule with the stat that cancels its cost; generated modules need names; and a module that is a single item, never a blueprint, keeps a lucky shape from being farmed. A middle way: the catalogue's rules, authored and named, each with a stat line generated to the budget. Discuss first.

Permanent loss stays refused (wrecks are recoverable). Discuss first.

Decided

Balance

Decided

Balance is measured by a ladder of bots and read as bands.

Random play (never rerolls, spends anywhere legal) beating a pack means too easy; greedy play (the best one-round trade on the player's own forecast) losing means a wall; the rungs landing together mark a cell with no decision; personas sit on top; two more rungs read the coordination gap, blind play below the greedy and the planner above it (the next row). tools/balance.gd runs every chassis alone, the starting team and each maker's first three against every pack, boss and domain into docs/balance.md. It is read by stage (Ryan, 23 October): which foes and compositions are harder for an early stock trio than for an endgame four, never how hard the roster is as a whole; the starter is a trio the tender deals, stocked, and the endgame four is fitted from pieces and modules up to tier two, tier three being powerful and rare by design, until the components' power curve is known. Pick share is the component signal. Play tests decide feel; no win rate is chased to a decimal. What a run can say (13 October): one cell near 50% carries a band of about 18 points at 30 seeds and 7 at 200, a row mean over twenty packs about 4, so a cell is read at 200 seeds or as a band, a quick run is a direction and a row that cites one says so, and every run a decision cites goes in docs/measurements.md, dated with its seeds, where the row keeps the reason and the exit. The greedy bot values only what it knows: a verdict on a mechanic it cannot value (a sentence, a doubling, a turn of the ranks, a wound) is a verdict on the bot, and says so.

Decided

Two rungs read the coordination gap: blind play, and a planner that looks two rounds ahead.

The greedy rung is a one-round bot that sets up only where the setup pays within the round, so the gap could not open on it (docs/measurements.md). Blind play is the tests' own (section 2: "a team that spends each die on the nearest foe"): the greedy's rerolls, then each weapon on the nearest foe in reach, each shield, vent and buff on its own machine, a die held only where its heat or its Volatile bite would wreck the machine, the gauge being in view. The planner plays the greedy's game and, at every spend with more than one worth making, plays its best few out on copies of the fight through the next round and its enemy turn, the greedy rung playing the same seeded futures for every candidate, and keeps the spend whose fight is then worth most, the greedy's pick on a tie (sim/policy.gd). So it values a mechanic the greedy cannot price by what two rounds of play make of it, and nothing past two rounds. It costs about four greedy fights a fight, so it runs on the team rows. Its rollouts once priced a Shock at the team's average blow, so a Shock of no damage read as a bad roll and was rerolled away; a blow's Shock is now worth the biggest blow an ally still to act shows on that foe (23 October). What reopens it: a changed share near nothing where setups should pay, or a mechanic slower than two rounds (a boss's), which a longer horizon or more futures would read at more cost.

Decided

A component is balanced against the components of its tier; a chassis alone is a diagnostic.

Ryan, 12 October: chassis serve different roles, so a solo win rate is no main indicator, and a weapon is never balanced because a lone Prairie Dog or Kite loses four on one, a fight it should lose or win only at a real cost in hull. The measures that matter: a component against its tier's others in the same hardpoint of the same machines (its value and its pick share), the team rows, and whole runs. One machine that warps its kit never dictates the whole roster of components (Ryan, 13 October, of the gunship).

Decided

The roster is widened so compositions can be read.

A support machine is never balanced against a damage machine as like against like; the unit of balance is the composition, and a solo row is a diagnostic (above). If every machine is a solo carry, a battle with four is trivial, so the roster needs machines that are only good together.
