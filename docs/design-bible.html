1

Concept and tone

Three nested loops. The campaign is the roguelike run: a sequence of battles, lost for good if the city gate falls. Each battle is a hex-grid battlefield with the gate on the south edge and a rift on the north edge, won by reaching and killing the boss that powers the rift. Each skirmish is a dice-driven fight triggered when mechs and enemy squads end a movement phase adjacent, and it is where the game's depth lives.

Decided

Doom and Space Marine energy.

Over-the-top badassery, played straight. Less overt irony than first planned: propaganda is fine, manufacturer advertisements and Borderlands-style gags are not, because this is the end of civilization. Never edgy, never grimdark fantasy. The city falling is never funny.

Decided

Mechs are grounded military mecha.

Mechwarrior lineage: rugged, brutal, heavy, built from parts that look serviced rather than cobbled together. Not Evangelion. In-world makers exist as provenance and a recognisable livery, in a maintenance-manual voice, never as marketing.

Decided

The Council for the Ultimate Liberation of Terra, and the world it runs.

Decided 16 and 17 September. Humanity's remaining cities are governed by the Council, formally the Council for the Ultimate Liberation of Terra; its acronym is never written or spoken in the game, however obvious, and the joke belongs to the player who notices. Plainly a martial-law regime pretending it is not, it believes its own cause even as it profits from it, which is how it has kept power: the populace has real believers, and the propaganda is sincere. It rewards innovation that helps the fight, which sets the manufacturers competing for its favour, and it keeps its own private military with the best hardware. The register is a god-emperor's court, not a junta; the tone is not the end of the world but "let them try, nobody stands against the march of human progress", said while everything quietly goes to shit. Closer to Pacific Rim than to BattleTech. Open on purpose, as room to expand: whether this is the last bastion of mankind at all, whether other colonies are doing fine and looking away, and whether the Council had a hand in the event that opened the rifts, its rise to central authority being the point of the play, which may become the campaign's spine. Retired: "houses" for the makers, and MARCH as the council's name. Lore is Ryan's; the fantasy field under design is where he keeps inspiration and homages, never shown.

Decided

The player commands for CARE: the underpaid true believers, not the politicians.

Decided 16 and 17 September. The Council pays for the city's defence through CARE, the Civil Auxiliary for Rift Emergencies: an arms-length body with the feel of an independent agency or a contracted corporation, named by the Council in its own condescension (civil, not military; auxiliary, not principal; the apocalypse filed as an emergency) and deliberately lame beside the aptitude of its people. If Ryan decides they named themselves, Agency replaces Auxiliary and the letters stay. The player came up through its ranks to Battlefield Commander: the general, never a politician. CARE's people are the war's true believers, who know why they fight and it is not the Council's reasons: practical, pragmatic, determined, genuine badasses with a rebellious streak, the lone-gunslinger action-hero register played straight and never winked at. The Council expects results and takes the credit, none of the responsibility. Its own R&D house, the Arsenal, sells CARE a trickle of its technology, the good stuff but never the best, and is the one research path the player cannot force: higher tiers come only as the campaign turns dire enough for the Council to loosen its grip. Holes to fill: what the Council does when the player fails, whether the contract can be lost, how the credit-taking turns into plot.

Decided

Enemies are old Final Fantasy monsters.

Detailed, over-the-top high fantasy, contrasted against the gritty realism of the player units. One genre invading another is the dichotomy the game wants, and it replaces most of the verbal irony.

Decided

Map tokens are chibi; combat units are full size.

Chibi 24x28 map tokens that make no attempt at scale, and full-size units in the skirmish, in the old Final Fantasy overworld-versus-battle split. Heavies, elites and bosses overflow their hex on the map, anchored at the hex base, drawn south over north, with the hex as the tap target. Whether map tokens stay sprites or become small 3D is open now that units are 3D.

Decided

Few, non-disposable war machines.

Each player unit is an apocalyptic war machine that takes on many foes over many battles. No unit micromanagement. Enemy hexes are squads, so every skirmish is a few machines against a horde.

Decided

Learnable in play.

The game is not simple by nature, but a player must never need a wiki, a build planner, or DPS math. Tooltips and an in-game cheat sheet are fine. External tools are a design failure.

Decided

Player units are native 3D; enemies stay painted 2D. The genre mash is literal.

Decided 16 September from the art lab (sections 8 to 10). The human side is real geometry in the engine. Ryan masses a mech in Blender from boxes and cylinders with materials named by role (steel, steel_dark, livery, glass, amber, rubber, bore, hot, white; "detail" on parts a map token drops); tools/blendforge polishes it by rule (bevels, recessed panels and rivets, tracks with cleats and road wheels, chamfered caps) and exports it with baked occlusion and edge wear as vertex colour; the engine draws it in a viewport at the sprites' angle with the toon shader (four tones per material, one fixed light, the livery a shader colour per maker, an outline pass, wear in the clean look) so it sits beside the painted foes. Target quality: an early-to-mid-2000s RTS model, not a painting. Everything human follows: gate, defences, the hangar. What it buys: every facing free, animation, livery swaps, damage states as hidden parts, no image model near a mech. Art direction that carries over from the sprite era: cold steel with one bold livery per maker and amber markings, no olive drab, chunky hard-bevelled geometry, Mechwarrior and StarCraft Terran rather than Evangelion, a three-quarter view 30 degrees down and 45 off head-on. History, for the record: hand-typed grids (13 September), a primitive raycaster spriter (14), isometric 80x96 sprites refined by the image model (15), Blender rendered into the spriter's style with edit-mode finish (16); each was superseded within a day, and the 2D sprite path remains only for the map tokens until they too go 3D. Still owed: wear tuning, the hp_<n> hardpoint Empties, the 3D view as the default.

Decided

Enemy art comes from an image model on the development Mac.

Decided 15 September. The enemy sheet's hand-typed grids are exported as a floor (tools/sheet_export.py). For real enemy art, tools/foeforge/ runs FLUX.2 Klein 4B locally through mflux (Apple silicon, about eight seconds a candidate), prompts built from each foe's codex text plus a fixed Final Fantasy VI style brief, then a cutter that keys the flat background, crops, scales into the 48x64 or 64x96 frame, flattens the shading to sixteen colours and adds the one-pixel outline. The model's own colours are kept by default; snapping to the faction palette is an option that so far reads muddy. Ryan picks a candidate per foe from a contact sheet. Check the model's licence before shipping.

2

Design tests

Every mechanic proposed for the skirmish is held to these. They exist because the designer's stated tendency is to overcomplicate for complexity's sake, and the goal is elegant fun.

Decided

A round takes about thirty seconds

of thought on a phone and contains one to three real decisions.

Decided

The build is visible every round.

You watch your components act. They are never just stat bonuses.

Decided

Two sources of randomness, both the player's, both visible.

The die decides what you can do; the damage roll decides how well. Kinetic faces roll a range and may crit; energy faces are static. No hidden hit rolls. Enemies never roll: their intents are telegraphed as exact numbers, as in Griftlands.

Decided

The player always has tools against the roll.

Rerolls before or after attacking, faces that raise the floor or deal the average, execute and finish effects that guarantee lethal, and visible enemy health. Variance is a tactical choice to mitigate, never a punishment.

Decided

Every squad poses a question,

such as finishing the resurrectors or interrupting the big hit, rather than being a damage race with different art.

Decided

Mismatch costs time, never ability.

A wrong loadout for a squad chews longer or eats more damage. It is never unable to act.

Decided

Never helpless, sometimes unlucky.

A bad roll with an interesting answer is where the fun lives. The design removes helplessness, not luck.

Decided

Hard cap on effects in play.

Zero to one chassis passive, where a passive is one idea in at most three rule lines: an upside and its cost count as one passive, two unrelated benefits count as two. Component passives are rare. Every trigger is shown on screen when it fires.

3

Campaign loop

Decided

The campaign is the run.

Battles are its floors. Permadeath applies to the campaign: if the gate falls, everything is lost. In-campaign progression (resources, research, upgrades) is lost with it.

Decided

Between battles: repair, salvage, research.

Resources collected in battle repair mechs and the city, upgrade the city's automated defenses and city abilities, and research new chassis, components, and commander tools. Wrecked mechs are salvaged, repaired, or broken down into components. This is core gameplay, not a menu.

Recommended

Bounded campaign, about ten battles in three acts.

One faction leads each act: undead, then demonic, then eldritch. The final battle seals the source rift. Winning unlocks difficulty tiers and new research for the next campaign. An endless mode can come later. Framing the campaign as a countdown to a convergence, one battle per night, gives the escalation a story for free.

Open

Campaign length and the ultimate goal

are not yet decided beyond "increasingly difficult battles." Target for mobile: a full campaign finishes in a few sittings, each battle resumable at any phase.

4

Battlefield layer

The battlefield decides where, when, and with whom you fight. It is deliberately the layer with positioning, so the skirmish can have none.

Map

Decided

Hex grid, gate south, rift north.

Procedurally generated terrain: water, impassable mountains, and more. One mech per hex. One enemy squad per hex. City automated defenses stand on the field near the gate and can be targeted.

Decided

The battlefield may be taller than one screen.

Seven wide stays the rule for thumbs. Height may exceed a screen and scroll vertically, with the camera following the active unit. Pointy-top hexes make defensive lines straight horizontal rows and force advancing enemies to zigzag. With cube coordinates in the logic, orientation stays a rendering choice.

Decided

Multi-hex objects.

A massive boss can occupy three hexes in a triangle. The gate and the rift are wider than one hex. Squads and mechs stay single-hex. Epic scale without player overhead. Simulation cost: units gain a footprint, and blocking, adjacency, and pathing treat every footprint hex as occupied.

Decided

Map presentation rules.

Squad count is one sprite plus a number badge for now, maybe a small, medium, large count later. Player units face the way they move, with three authored directions mirrored to six; battlefield sprites omit visible customization. Mechs get a per-class idle; enemies get no animation except attacks, in the old Final Fantasy way. If overflow hides something that matters, the overflow allowance is too big; partially hidden objects get an outline highlight.

Phase resolution

Decided

Enemy moves are previewed. Player plots routes and commits.

Every unit has a movespeed. Enemy squads usually head for the gate or the city defenses, occasionally for player units. The player sees where each squad will go, then plots each mech's route and commits.

Recommended

Player routes resolve first, then enemies execute their previewed moves and stop at the first blocked hex.

The preview is exact unless a mech ends its move on a squad's path. Body-blocking is the core operational verb, and forming lines is how the gate gets defended. Flyers can engage but cannot block, or blocking breaks.

Decided

Adjacency after movement triggers a skirmish.

A player unit adjacent to an enemy squad, plus any units touching those, forms a skirmish. Multiple skirmishes in one phase resolve one at a time.

Recommended

A skirmish is the whole connected group of adjacent units.

A clustered wave becomes one dangerous fight, which teaches the player to split blobs and pick off stragglers. Cap participants only if phone readability demands it.

Pacing: a race to the rift

Decided

No in-field repair. The battle keeps pushing forward.

It must feel like a race to close the rift before the city is overwhelmed, not a back-and-forth war simulation. Slow-and-steady versus blitz is the player's choice, including leaving a defender at the gate.

Decided

Damage sticks. Mitigation is a real concern.

Hull never repairs during a battle. Mitigation comes from recharging shields and from defensive tactics, not from healing.

Decided

A standing shield blocks the blow whole; in a fight, shield comes only from components.

Decided 18 September, after discussion. However small the shield and however big the blow, nothing bleeds through to the hull; the blow still costs the shield its damage. That makes a ten-point shield a real decision every round a big hit is telegraphed, gives glass cannons tension, and leaves no room for an in-combat trickle (dropped the same day: a passive may bring it back for one chassis as a niche). Out of combat, shield returns each battlefield phase at the chassis's field rate; never a free refill between skirmishes, so withdraw-and-re-engage is not a reset. Foes get past a shield by striking twice (double strike) or, for bosses, by breaching: the excess overflows into hull. Rates and sizes are placeholders.

Decided

Armour is a flat number off every bare-hull blow, and zero is fine.

Decided 18 September. A chassis stat (components may add to it later), never a percentage, taken off each blow that lands on hull with no shield standing. A blow smaller than the armour does nothing: a Badger drives its treads over a horde of ghouls laughing, and that is the point. A ghoul that only ever claws for ten deserves to be walled; the answer is monsters that do more than claw (double strike, breach, abilities), not a damage floor. The Raptor has no armour and stays afraid of ghouls. Shield is not armoured: a blow on shield costs the shield its full damage, so shield answers the big blow and armour answers the swarm, and a bruiser and a glass cannon are different builds. The per-round Rift Pressure tick is what keeps a long wall from being free.

Decided

Foes strike in the order they stand, and the heaviest deploy left.

Decided 18 September. Every blow resolves on its own against the shield standing at that moment, so strike order decides what a small shield blocks. No initiative numbers, nothing to micromanage: front rank left to right, then the back rank. At first contact the heaviest hitters (attack times strikes) deploy left, ties in arrival order, so the player sees the big blow coming from the jump. A round cap on skirmishes was rejected: it breaks the flow like an advertisement in a show.

Recommended

One Rift Pressure clock.

A single bar that ticks once per battlefield phase and once per skirmish round, and spawns a fresh squad at the rift when it fills. Dawdling on the map and grinding in a skirmish feed the same meter. It is the race made mechanical, and it replaces any hard limit on skirmish length.

Decided

No persistent movespeed penalties from damage.

Combined with terrain, limping mechs make runs unwinnable with no warning. Hull damage has no secondary penalty in the first prototype.

Open

The battlefield as a landscape: ravines, mesas and gulleys, half chosen and half rift-twisted.

Ryan, 17 September, direction to design against. The ground before the gate is not a field with obstacles on it. It is a landscape the defenders shaped on purpose, ravines and gulleys that split and funnel the horde toward the killing field in front of the gate, and that the rift twists a little more every battle as pieces of the other side bleed through, which is why no two battles share a map. Consequences to work out: the procedural map should group features (contiguous roads, rock ranges, water courses) rather than scatter them; terrain should have high and low, cliff faces and rises that can be leaped and that favour whoever holds the top, alongside plain impassables like mesas and boulders, presented in a way that is not jank; new terrain types bleed in from the rift and grow more frequent near it, bone yards, lava fields, an eldritch nether; and pathfinding should be a decision, not "go around the rock". Nothing built yet.

Recommended

Squads show a likely-loot icon.

Reward types are faction-weighted (section 8). The squad shows the likely type, never the item, so path choice is informed without a table.

5

Skirmish layer

The skirmish decides how you fight. Touchstone: Slice and Dice. Target prioritization, semi-controlled randomness, and build preparation and synergy. Several variants were considered, including a zoomed-in hex grid; that one was set aside because it repeats the battlefield's decision type, fights the no-micromanagement goal, and is hostile to thumbs in portrait.

Round loop

Recommended

No positioning on the player side.

Mechs have no ranks and never shuffle. Enemies organize around them.

Recommended

Attack, then reroll: one interleaved phase, then the enemy, then straight back.

Every mech's die is rolled. Tap to lock, drag to spend, reroll up to twice at any point, before or after attacking, so a low damage roll is information for the rest of the round. End turn resolves the enemy and starts the next round at once, with no click to begin your turn. This is the loop damage ranges earn, and it is the thing that keeps the game from being Slice and Dice with extra layers. Rerolls are per round, never a pooled stock.

Decided

Damage ranges and crits, by weapon class.

Kinetic faces show a minimum and a maximum, roll between them the moment they land, and may crit for double. Energy faces show one number and never roll or crit: reliable and hot against swingy and cool. Crits are where the exciting passives live. There is no undo; the roll is the commitment. Numbers are in tens.

Decided

Hold a die to see every face.

Deciding whether to reroll needs the whole die, not the face showing. Hold lists every face with the current one marked; double-tap opens the same list as a codex box. Passives are not shown on the die; they belong to the mech.

Decided

No hard round limit.

A cap would punish defensive, value, status, reflect, and mitigation builds in favour of burst. Skirmish length is governed by the Rift Pressure clock, the withdraw option, and tuning (typical skirmish three to five rounds; enemy damage must outpace sustain so stalemates cannot happen).

Decided

Player-initiated withdraw, and its price is one enemy turn.

Decided 19 September. Withdraw is offered at any point of the player's turn, the first round included. Its price is always the same: the enemy takes its turn, then the team flees at the end of the round, so there is no leapfrogging around contact unscathed. Being forced into contact is bad strategy, not bad luck, and gets the same price. Withdrawing ends the skirmish; survivors on both sides carry their damage back to the map and the squad marches on. Replaces the 15 September rule (start of round only, before any face was spent).

Decided

Ends on: pool empty (victory), all hulls zero (defeat), or withdraw.

Victory awards resources and a temporary upgrade choice. Defeat turns the mechs to scrap: wrecks to be salvaged after the battle. Surviving enemies continue their assault.

Enemy formation

Decided

A squad is one enemy type. Composition comes from the map.

An enemy hex is one foe type times a strength. What you fight in a skirmish is whatever squads are chained together at contact, so the rift's formation and how the player disrupts it decide the composition. Factions describe the formation they send through the rift together. Bosses are single three-hex units.

Decided

Ranks hold, and reinforcements step in at the end of the enemy turn.

Ryan, 17 September. A front-rank foe only ever stands in the front, a back-rank foe only in the back, even when the other rank has an empty slot: a ghoul in the back row raises "how is that hitting me" and the answer is never worth it. The pool is now called reinforcements, in the interface too. At the first deployment they take slots in the order they arrived, so a formation is predictable; at the end of every enemy turn the reinforcement phase fills each empty slot, left to right and front rank first, with a random waiting foe of the right rank, and the log says how many stepped in. Ryan wants ways for the player to interact with that phase; open. Facing, also decided: player units face the foes (a rear three-quarter, turned to the left so the claw side shows; Ryan, 18 September, replacing the front-right convention of the day before) and enemies face left; the ghoul already does, and the foeforge prompt now asks for it.

Decided

One fixed semantic palette; the HUD picker changes chrome only.

Decided 18 September from swatch pages Ryan judged by eye (he is protanomalous: warm greens against yellow, cool greens against blue and purple against red are his confusions, red against green is not). Rules: no green or purple ever carries meaning; shield is the only blue (#2f63d8); enemy (#ff1e1e) and heat (#ff6a00) are both warm and a full lightness band apart; hull is bone white; good and selection are white; ink #f2f4ee over muted #7b857c; every state also carries a glyph or a shape. Chrome is one of three HUD colours (green #33ff55, amber #fff21a, cyan #5cd8ff), pushed away from the orange; red was dropped because it is the enemy colour. No coloured text sits on a map tile: numbers go on a dark plaque with a coloured border, a boss is a double outline, an elite tag a double border, and the rift is its own art with no palette role. Gauges show a pending loss dimmed and a pending gain hatched, on all three bars, shield above hull.

Decided

The gate and the walls are Arsenal-built models on the bottom row; the gate carries the first base defences.

Decided 19 September. The bottom row of every map is the gate (three hexes) with indestructible walls across the rest; nobody stands on any of it. Both are lowforge models like the units (gate and wall in tools/lowforge/models_low.py, maker arsenal, rebuilt in the locked register on 21 September; blendforge is gone) and placed on the 3D battlefield as geometry at true scale: metal, heavily armoured, weathered, slightly gothic, built to survive worse than this. Reference: Ryan's 40K fortress images, toned down. Amended 20 September: the first build read as a dinky door in a dinky wall. The gate is there to keep giant demons out and must look it: proportionately far bigger, taller than wide in feel, and its footprint may grow if the height needs it. No crenellations, which make it small (sand-castle squares); fins, blades or other texture as ornament instead. The gate has a pair of guns flanking it on its towers: the first foray into base defences, how they attack and are attacked to be decided; deployable and destructible turrets follow, placed in the hangar phase or as temporary city abilities in the battlefield phase.

Decided

The battlefield is 3D, and the rift's world bleeds into it as flat 2D.

Decided 19 September from the RIFTGATE_MAP3D experiment. The ground, the gate and walls, and the player's mechs are geometry under the map camera. Enemies are never modelled and never have facing: each foe type is a flat token sprite, Final Fantasy style, exaggerated for identification, and on the battlefield it is drawn as a flat image with no 3D cues at all (no shadow, no base, no standee edge). The 2D is not a stylisation of the enemy; it is the other world. Two effects carry that, and they stack: a bleed gradient toward the rift, where terrain height flattens, colour posterizes into the foes' register and the painted terrain marks (as a texture) take over from geometry; and the same flattening as a radius around every squad, moving with it, so where the enemy stands the third dimension itself collapses into a flat painted map. Invaded ground loses height and perspective, not just props. The rift hexes read as the source of it. When a skirmish opens, the camera flattens into the pixel look of the skirmish screen: the fight is the seam between the worlds. Terrain marks live in a ground texture, since that texture is what the 3D fades into. Technically: two layers, the 3D viewport and the existing 2D painted map, composited by a mask (north gradient, squad radii, the rift) with a dithered, pixel-stepped edge; tall props collapse toward the mask before the 2D takes over.

Decided

The clash must jar: a gritty, elevated reconstruction against a bright flat overworld.

Ryan, 20 September, after the first compositor read as too gentle. The 3D side is gritty, muted and realistically coloured: a grainy ground texture with cracks, simple lit models, one sun casting shadows, and real elevation, every hex a column with a height by terrain (rough and ruins a step up, mountains tall, water sunk so its far bank shows). The overlays stand on the terrain (a raised hex's routes, plaques and taps lift with it). Elevation is a commitment: once height is visible players expect it to matter, so terrain costs and high ground come due. The other world is an old overworld, ironically bright and saturated, flat, with chunky marks at a coarse scale and no pretence of detail; it need not match the foes' finer sprites. The seam is hard: a dithered band a few cells wide, no soft fade, and the columns collapse just outside it. A camera shift between the two sides was considered and rejected: the compositor depends on the 3D ground projecting exactly onto the 2D grid. Tilt-shift on the 3D side only (a focus band around the middle of the screen, blur above and below, the flat world always sharp) is in as a trial for Ryan to keep or drop. The bright green of the overworld sits beside the green HUD chrome; amber or cyan chrome separates them if it fights.

Decided

Ally art is the mid-nineties 3D register, built by code from a brief.

Decided 21 September, replacing every earlier ally-art direction (the 2D spriter, the pixel and clean toon looks, the hand-polished Badger). Reference: Descent and MechWarrior 2. A unit is 300 to 900 deliberate flat-shaded triangles (components under 150, structures under 1,500) wearing small tiled textures, 64 px, generated by code in its maker's metal and applied by box projection, so nothing is ever unwrapped or hand-painted. One sun, hard shadows, a thin ink line at skirmish scale. Under zoom the texels pixelate and the facets stay crisp, which is the register, not a fault. Both worlds are now honest retro: nineties 3D against sixteen-bit 2D. The metal is the first differentiator between makers: Minerva steel and military greens, matte; Kestrel chrome, silver and blues, glossy; Wainwright & Sutler iron and warm browns; Aphelion whites and reds; the Arsenal dark steel and gunmetal. The build: each unit is one readable function of numbers in tools/lowforge/models_low.py (lofts of cross-sections, tapered booms, six-sided prisms, boxes, wing plates), named by its chassis id; Ryan briefs in words, Claude turns the brief into a first pass, both iterate by render, and Ryan may edit the numbers or drop a hand-made Blender part into the same file. Tens of crafted units, not hundreds. Wireframe as the reconstruction's telemetry drawing (a destroyed component losing its fill) is a mode to build later. Reducing a detailed model never produces this look; it is built low from the start.

Decided

The battlefield looks like the commander's holographic tactical display, over the top.

Ryan, 21 September, after the modelled terrain read as an amateur tactics sim: push the reconstruction frame all the way, like the HUD. The hex grid is the display's own light, drawn in the scene so raised ground hides it and the bleed takes it with the ground; scanlines run across the whole battlefield, both worlds; player assets and the gate carry ink on their creases; lamps, engines and light strips glow; the corrupted side is input the display cannot hold, and it tears and flickers in bands. Terrain height is landscape only, decided the same day: a relief field in terraces, decoupled from what the ground is, kept for the 3D-against-2D contrast and never read by a rule; crags sit at their ground's level with the spikes on top, and the ground under a crag keeps its flavour (crag-water, crag-ruin). Still open in this direction: how far to push terrain toward wireframe, and a sci-fi rather than gothic gate.

Decided

The skirmish is fought on the enemy's ground: the foes stand on the other world, the mechs on the seam.

Ryan, 21 September: a skirmish opens where a squad stands, and where a squad stands the ground has already gone flat, so the fight is never on the reconstruction's terrain. The skirmish screen is the feed the reconstruction hands over to at contact, and its ground says so. Under the foes, the other world's flat bright overworld of the contact hex's terrain, at its two-frame animation. Under the mechs, the reconstruction's tile with the other world eating into it from the top edge through the map's own dithered, pixel-stepped seam: the one place the seam is close enough to read as a texture. The display's grid, the traces on the machines and the scan bar sit over both, the same display on both screens with only the ground changing. Later the seam's depth may follow how far the corruption has come. The map, before a plan is locked, shows the phase exactly as it will resolve (the resolver itself run dry, every unit put back): every contact, every squad a body block will stop, every route that will be rejected. Ryan, 21 September: there is never any ambiguity about what Resolve will do. The movement phase plays: mechs along their routes, then squads along their intents, each cut where it really stopped, and the skirmish opens after.

Decided

Foe art register, restated for the battlefield.

Foes are painterly, sixteen colours, at a coarser pixel scale than anything the player owns, with dithered shading; mechs are clean toon geometry. On the map this contrast is the point: the enemy sprite is drawn at its own pixel grid, never smoothed, never lit by the scene, and the terrain it stands on posterizes toward its register. Restated 19 September from the 15 September style split.

Decided

The battlefield screen is CARE's tactical reconstruction of the field.

Decided 20 September. Ryan's framing was a war table in-world: an approximated satellite view with the HUD, grid, mechs and labels projected over it, the only explanation for seeing the corrupted 2D landscape at all; he took Claude's refinement as the same idea with more to hang on it. The screen is the reconstruction CARE builds from drone survey and mech telemetry, which is why terrain and structures are real geometry and the mechs are clean models drawn legibly large (they are the system's own markers, telemetry made visible, not a camera image). The reconstruction cannot hold what came through the rift: sensors return it flat, so the enemy and the ground it corrupts appear as a flat picture, and at contact the reconstruction gives up and the feed itself takes over (the skirmish screen). This makes the 2D literal rather than decorative, licenses a pitched camera and a zoom, and settles scale without a tabletop: real ground at a small scale (a hex is the room a machine takes), mechs drawn at marker size. Sensor language follows and is wanted for its strategic depth: a squad not yet seen is a contact, the edge tags are sensor returns, the intent lines are projections; the reconstruction's quality is a possible research path later. Scale is a feel question, not a plausibility one: the mechs need not fit through the gate, but the gate must look like it keeps behemoths out.

Decided

The city is under a field, and the gate is its projector.

Ryan's hole, 20 September: a wall with one reinforced gate makes no sense against things that climb and fly. Decided the same day: the city sits under an impenetrable energy field, the Arsenal's one technology the Council funds in full, and the gate is its projector. The field is cast from the gate outward and over the city, and the projector stands in its own shadow: the mouth is the one place the field cannot cover, which is why every squad heads for it and why the gate falling loses the campaign (the projector goes, the field goes, the city is open). Rift Pressure is literally pressure on the field; the sortie exists because the field strains as pressure climbs and the rift must be closed before it fails. The walls on the bottom row are the projector's emitter line, and the blades on every top edge are its vanes, so the ornament has a job. Known holes, accepted: why not a second field over the mouth (every projector has a mouth; the innermost still has one). Dome and mountain were considered and dropped: the dome explains nothing the field does not, and a cliff loses the built, Arsenal look. Still to do in art: the field itself, if it shows at all (a faint edge at the bottom row, or nothing), and the walls reshaped as emitters. The gate's later direction (Ryan, 21 September): keep the fortress brief but add StarCraft Terran, more machinery and lights, fewer vanes, a wider door that shows how it opens.

Decided

Every machine can ford water; each way of moving has its own worst ground.

Ryan, 21 September: machines this size cross water, so it is a penalty, not a wall, and terrain should make the ways of moving feel different rather than "slow and slower". Tracks wade slowly (3), a walker wades (2) and shrugs off rough and rubble, hover skims water (1) but chokes on rubble (3), a flyer ignores everything and is the only thing that crosses a crag. Numbers are placeholders.

Decided

Front and back ranks, enemy side only.

Simple two-rank structure. It adds a second axis to target choice: a front rank of bodies and a back rank of problems.

Recommended

A pool with ranks, in columns.

Two rows by three columns visible at a time. Dead enemies are replaced from the squad's pool, so a horde keeps coming and elites surface at set points. Each back enemy stands behind one front slot, so "exposed" is visible rather than a rule.

Range archetypes

Decided

No damage falloff at range.

Futuristic weapons hitting for less at range is counterintuitive. Range is expressed as archetypes with soft penalties, never smaller numbers.

Decided

Three reaches plus keywords.

Decided 18 September with Ryan's vocabulary audit. Melee hits the front rank, and the back rank only when the whole front is empty, so a melee build can be stranded by a held back rank and that is its cost. Line of sight hits the front rank freely and a back enemy only where its column is exposed. Indirect hits any foe in either rank; on the front rank it pays splash, half the damage off your own shield. Keywords ride on top: spread for neighbours, penetrate for the column. A passive may change a component's reach while a condition holds (the Badger's cracked weapons go melee). No delayed shells: delay adds cross-round state for no gain.

Shields, heat, injuries

Recommended

Shields are a timer.

Shield faces add shield. The shield buffer gives a few rounds to solve the squad before hull is at risk. Enemy damage should threaten to break a shield within two or three rounds against a typical squad, so shield faces buy time rather than immunity.

Recommended

Heat is the second resource.

Strong faces add heat. A vent face clears it. Running hot risks internal damage. Passives and support faces (vent an ally's heat) make heat a team problem, not a solo one.

Decided

Injuries are one threshold and one roll of the die, on bare hull only.

When a blow lands on bare hull (no shield standing) and what gets past armour is at least a quarter of maximum hull, the mech's die is rolled and the component it lands on is injured. An intact component cracks: it loses its keywords, crit chance and unique effect but still does its basic action. A cracked component hit again is destroyed and goes blank. A destroyed component or an empty hardpoint takes the blow and nothing happens, so a badly broken mech shrugs off more of them. A passive may hold a cracked component short of destruction (the Badger's). The counterplay is shielding before a big telegraphed hit. The one-in-two coin on top of the threshold was cut on 17 September as randomness with no decision behind it; per-faction signature injuries were dropped earlier as an unnecessary layer. The threshold is a placeholder.

Decided

A damaged face is a damaged component, and it stays damaged.

Cracked and destroyed faces persist across skirmishes and battles until repaired in the hangar. This is the component damage system: there is no separate one. It makes a lost skirmish cost something into the next battle and gives the meta layer's repair and salvage real work to do.

Pinned

Low-hull berserker or last-stand ability.

A mech at critical hull unlocking an overdrive face is Doom energy and an anti-spiral. Parked, not rejected.

Recommended

Terrain is a skirmish-wide condition.

The hexes involved set a modifier for the whole fight, so choosing where to engage still matters without positioning inside the skirmish. Water dampens burn, ruins give the squad cover, and so on.

6

Die faces and iconography

Decided

Primary icon, a number, small secondary icons, one catch-all star.

The primary icon is the face's type: the reach archetype for attacks, a shield for shield faces, and so on. The number is its power. Small superscript or subscript icons mark common attached effects. One catch-all icon marks a rare, unique effect (legendary faces). Long-press a face to inspect secondary numbers, unique effects, and temporary boosts. An icon cheat sheet is available from the pause menu.

Recommended

Every entry on the die is a component.

There is no generic equipment and nothing fills a slot for you: an entry is a component, or an empty hardpoint that does nothing when it comes up, or a destroyed component. A pristine, fully fitted war machine is reliable; a light or injured one is not. Decided 18 September, replacing "every face does something".

Recommended

A keyword vocabulary of about ten.

Variety comes from combining number, reach, keywords, and heat cost with passives, not from more rules. Slice and Dice gets hundreds of distinct faces from a vocabulary this size.

Decided

Keywords are a list, not a single slot.

A face may carry several keywords, so faces can be upgraded during a battle or in the meta. The content validator caps authored faces at three keywords; upgrades may push past that at runtime. Iconography stays readable because the primary icon and the number are fixed, and extra keywords show as small secondary icons.

Decided

Minimal chrome in the skirmish.

No cards, no boxes. The log stays in the centre for now because it is needed for testing and the space is free; it can hide behind a button later. Health and status are small floating slider bars beside the sprite. Mechs face north-east and enemies south-west; mechs get a per-class idle, enemies only attack animations. A massive elite may occupy both rank slots of a column. Tap, double-tap, or hold shows full numbers and details. Passives are simple enough not to be displayed at all times. Enemy health stays visible (the JRPG-style hidden-health option was dropped with the Griftlands damage model).

Decided

The vector HUD is the UI.

Adopted 14 September from the layout canvas. Everything that is not a sprite is a flat vector overlay: slanted gauges, rounded-square dice with corner brackets when locked, bracketed [ATK 10] readouts, a terminal-style log. The HUD colour (green by default, amber, red, blue) is a player setting saved to the user settings file. Screen order, top to bottom: header tag, enemies, log, dice with Reroll wide beneath them, mechs with their gauges hugging the sprites at the bottom, End turn bottom-left and Withdraw bottom-right, away from the palm. Withdraw always asks; End turn asks only when usable dice are unspent. Keyword chips hug the die's edge as floating annotation tags with no leader lines and flicker only every few seconds. No help text on screen; a tutorial popup is owed if the gestures are not intuitive.

Decided

Skirmish readouts.

Decided 15 September from playtest notes and refined since. No unit names on screen: hold or double-tap for them. Gauges hug the drawing, not the frame. One chip row under every sprite: a foe's intent as a fantasy-register icon with its number (sword, kite shield, heart, arrows) and its statuses (burn, suppressed, exposed); a mech's statuses and its warning signs (shield break, component risk, wreck, overheat), from a forecast of the round's end that recomputes after every action, with the gauges pulsing in ink-white over the part about to change. No rank labels. Reroll is one wide button right under the dice; the intent eye sits under the foes at the right edge, within a thumb's reach of the arrows it toggles. Quotes in italic (Barlow Condensed Italic). The codex is a spec plate: designation as a stamped tag, nickname in quotes, maker and silhouette, a stat grid beside the portrait, the passive, faces in two centred columns, a rule, codex text, quote.

Decided

Three rerolls, and a reroll always shows a different face.

Decided 15 September. Two rerolls did not feel like enough even before consumables and commander powers; the base is three. A reroll never lands on the face showing nor on one equivalent to it, as a known global rule: these are not really dice, and a reroll that changes nothing reads as a bug rather than as bad luck. A die whose faces are all alike keeps its face. The count shows on the Reroll button only, not in the header. This is a rule the player must be told: the Reroll button's tooltip and the die codex say it now, and the gesture tutorial should too. Knock-on to watch: on a four-face die three never-repeating rerolls almost always find the wanted face, so d4 may be too small a size to exist (see open questions).

Decided

Intents show the action, not the target.

Each foe shows what it will do and for how much. Holding a foe or a mech draws the arrows for that unit; an overlay button draws every arrow. This is Slice and Dice's model.

Decided

Few machines against a horde is the fantasy, and the hooks come from it.

Decided 15 September. With every foe on the field striking each round, incoming damage on the lowest shield is the pressure the whole skirmish is built around, and the answer is design, not a cap: area damage that earns its keep against a full formation, taunt and mitigation that shape who gets hit, and enemy behaviour that is not always optimal. The horde is an invasion, not an army: most rank and file may target badly or sometimes not attack at all, while the higher tiers of the demonic and eldritch factions execute plans that look thought through. A direction to build: squads that move with or natively include a commander unit; kill it and the squad's targeting scrambles, even mid-turn. Formation width is a setting (Skirmish.columns, three by default, four for the horde feel) rather than a fixed rule, chosen per fight as content decides. The current foes and squad compositions are proof-of-concept content and will be replaced.

Decided

A foe type is not limited to one action.

Each foe in content/foes.json carries an actions roster: attack, defend, buff, or debuff, each with a player-facing line and a status. Sometimes a foe attacks, sometimes it shields, sometimes it buffs the horde or weakens a mech. The engine still runs only the basic attack, so the validator insists on exactly one implemented attack per foe and refuses anything else marked implemented; planned actions live in the file and stay out of the codex until coded.

Decided

Tap a die to lock it, hold it for details.

No separate Keep button. A locked die is highlighted and skipped by reroll.

Decided

Double-tap a unit for its codex entry.

Hold is the quick read: numbers and arrows while the finger is down. Double-tap opens a box that stays: the codex text, stats, actions, faces shown visually in a column with the current face highlighted, the passive with its rules, and last the flavour line as an italic quote. The head-on sprite sits top-right of the box across from the name. Every foe has its own flavour line, not the faction's. The design question a unit poses is never shown; fantasy and question are internal design fields, and any hint the player needs goes into the codex text. A tap outside closes it.

Decided

Numbers in the magnitude of tens.

Hull, shield, damage, and heat live in tens, not ones, so upgrades can be incremental and big numbers feel big; a 15 or a 33 is fine, and the validator only refuses negatives (clarified 18 September). Burn is one stack per twenty damage dealt at five per stack, splash is half the damage rolled, heat overflows one for one.

Decided

Content text is split by shape: shown at the top level, internal under design.

Decided 16 September, from Ryan's cleanup. Every content entry (chassis, foe, component, faction, vocabulary term) has exactly two shown texts, codex (the inspect entry, in combat and in the hangar) and flavour (an in-world quote), plus a passive's name and description and a foe action's text. Everything internal sits under one design object: fantasy (the pitch, the inspiration, deliberate homages), niche (the role it fills or the problem it poses, the old foe-only "question" generalised), holes on vocabulary terms, signature and answer on factions, notes. The validator refuses any of those keys at the top level, so nothing internal can leak into a codex by accident. Passives lost their rules text: the description says what the passive does in the player's words and the effects list is what the engine runs, capped at three. Components now carry codex and flavour too, since they are inspected.

Decided

The vocabulary is data.

content/vocabulary.json is the single source of truth for every mechanic term, written fantasy-first with a rule, a status (implemented, planned, idea), and a holes field. docs/vocabulary.md is generated from it and lists the holes at the bottom, so it shows where the mechanics are thin.

7

Mechs and the builder

Mechwarrior's build depth, simplified and dialled back. The feel to keep is that a loadout is a real identity. The habits to drop are strict weight classes and gated equipment.

Decided

Three to four mechs per battlefield.

Enough for a real decision between splitting up and staying together for skirmish strength, without tedious planning. Recommended: design for four, start a campaign with three, unlock the fourth slot early.

Decided

Chassis are not classes and not only bipeds.

Tanks, hovercraft, quadrupeds, and flyers are all candidates. No light, medium, heavy labels. Chassis diversity comes from base stats: hull, shield, movespeed, hardpoints, and similar simple numbers that do not need prominent display.

Decided

The die is the equipment: fixed components plus hardpoints, one entry each.

Decided 18 September. A chassis has fixed components (built in, never removed or sold, no hardpoint used: the Badger's claw and plating) and hardpoints (free slots, any component in any one). Die size is fixed count plus hardpoints; the Badger rolls a d6 from two fixed and four hardpoints, and its model carries four pins. Every component is exactly one entry: multi-slot components were considered and scrapped the same day as invisible complexity (where would it render, and would anyone pay for the fragility). The player never sees a polyhedron, so shown text says component, never face; Face stays the engine's word for one entry.

Earlier, recommended: hardpoints set die size.

Each component contributes one face; the chassis fills any remainder. A chassis with four hardpoints rolls a d4 and is consistent and focused. One with eight rolls a d8 and carries more but sees each face less. That is the deckbuilder's thin-deck tension as a chassis stat. First pass: d4, d6, d8. The builder shows the odds of each face.

Decided

Zero to one chassis passive, and it may be a skirmish passive.

Two flavours. A double-edged niche definer: a skirmisher aircraft with big shield, high movespeed, low hull, few faces, and "double damage, double heat," positioned by its manufacturer as a hit-and-run assassin, that becomes a carry beside an ally who vents its heat. Or a simple multiplier fantasy: "doubles all burn," where the player stacks burn and burns everything like a maniac.

A passive is one idea. Two complementary rules, such as "when I gain shield, one random ally gains the same amount" and "my shield does not recharge outside combat," are one passive with an upside and a cost, not two. The test is whether it can be named in a phrase and understood as one thing. Cap: three rule lines.

Recommended

A passive multiplies or redirects what faces already do.

It never introduces a verb of its own. If it needs a new mechanic to explain, it is a component, not a chassis.

Decided

Components are free.

Untyped hardpoints, any component in any slot. A niche you can leave is an invitation; the player steps outside it for experimentation or because resources force them to make it work.

Recommended

Silhouettes become terrain traits.

Hovercraft cross water, quadrupeds ignore rough ground, flyers pass over mountains but cannot body-block, tanks move faster on roads. Team composition becomes a decision about the map as well as the fight.

Decided

Every chassis has a maker's designation and a nickname.

Ryan, 16 September. The makers' names are deliberately dull (the EX-45XLE Combat Miner Frame); the names people use are not (the Badger). Content carries both: designation, the long name, and name, the nickname, which is what the game says everywhere short. The codex draws them as a spec plate: designation small above, nickname large, maker and silhouette under. The first Bulwark was renamed the Badger, id and all; the "adjective animal" convention floated the same day is dropped in favour of this pair.

Decided

Five makers at most, each an identity through the machines it builds, never a battlefield niche.

Decided 16 and 17 September. Minerva Heavy Works (minerva; was Halvard): tracked hulls, armour, fists; maintenance-manual voice; deep red; plates as industrial catalogue codes (EX-45M). Kestrel Dynamics: interceptors and light frames; flight-test log; cobalt; type numbers and marks (K-9 Mk III). Wainwright & Sutler (wainwright_sutler; was Orrin & Pike, a sitcom wink Ryan keeps; the name is two trades, wagon-builder and army-provisioner): quadrupeds, spider tanks and wheels with an old-West iron heritage, holding ground against numbers with indirect fire, area and suppression; field-almanac voice; bone white; pattern numbers (Pattern 7 Walker). Aphelion (just that, delightfully pretentious): descended from the deep-space programme, back since the rifts opened, perhaps still answering to someone off-world; humanoid strike frames, hover platforms, variable-geometry frames; mission-control checklists and callsigns; gunmetal with signal orange; programme names and flight numbers. The Arsenal: the Council's own R&D house, which need not compete for favour; heavy walkers, plasma and energy that runs hot and never crits, big single hits, execute, burn; a manual written as liturgy; hazard yellow and black, the HUD's own colours; plates as decrees (Arsenal Instrument IV) and nicknames as zealot words (Crusader, Inquisitor, Justicar). Rules across all five: makers give provenance and a voice, never marketing (no adverts, warranties or brochures); designations mean something in-world and the plate's shape says the maker before you read it; a chassis's class, not its maker, decides its battlefield skills, so Minerva and the Arsenal both drive straight at the enemy for different reasons and it plays the same. Considered and dropped: a precision house (Vernier), a big-boom house. Placing chassis and components under makers is Ryan's; Aphelion and the Arsenal have no content yet.

Decided

Fixed components stay fixed: no paid unlock.

Ryan, 18 September: restriction is what makes builds fun, so a fixed slot is never bought open. The earlier tabled idea is kept below for the record.

Was: some hardpoints are hardwired into the chassis.

Ryan, 16 September; tabled 17 September until the campaign economy exists, since paying to free a hardpoint may have no value as a mechanic without it. Of a chassis's hardpoints, some are free for components and some are locked: faces that come with the machine and cannot be swapped out. The Badger might have seven, four free and three hardwired. Locked faces carry the chassis's flavour and can hold effects that would be broken on another mech but are balanced in that one's context, which is a cleaner home for identity than the single passive. The hangar can sell an unlock, freeing a locked hardpoint for a nominal sum, so a build can grow out of its chassis over a campaign. Data shape when built: a fixed list of faces on the chassis, each with an unlock cost; hardpoints stays the die size and loadout fills what is free. Not built.

Recommended

Hardpoints are marked in the model, not guessed from it.

If the game goes 3D and components are visual swap-outs, the model needs to say where they mount. Convention proposed: an Empty named hp_1 … hp_n at each socket, its +Y pointing where the weapon points, parented to the part it rides on; everything else is cosmetic and needs no tag. A component model has its origin at its own mount point, so attaching is placing it at the Empty. The count of Empties should match the chassis's hardpoints. Not built; waits on the 2D-versus-3D decision.

Recommended

Livery is set by the maker and repainted by the player, for a fee.

Ryan, 16 September. A chassis ships in its manufacturer's livery (Halvard deep red, Kestrel cobalt, Orrin & Pike bone white), which is one material role in the art pipeline, so a repaint is a palette swap and costs nothing to produce. The hangar can sell a change of livery for a nominal sum: a small resource sink that lets a player mark their team without touching balance. Not built; the hangar comes first.

Recommended

Passive-only components trade a face for a passive.

Choosing one means a smaller die that behaves better. A build identity in one decision.

Decided

Component damage is the component's state.

A component is intact, cracked, or destroyed, and the state persists until the hangar repairs it. Nothing finer. Salvage recovers the intact parts from a wreck.

Decided

One pipeline for every effect; numbers are questions, never stored totals.

Decided 18 September. Chassis passives, component passives, skirmish rewards, buffs and debuffs, and effects timed to a round are all modifiers with a scope and a source, evaluated by sim/effects.gd on every read: suppression first (shorter-lived wins, a suppressed effect suppresses nothing), then the gate (live, trigger fires, condition holds, component not destroyed), then adds, then multiplies, then floors and caps, then overrides. A mech's hull, shield, heat, armour, move and recharge are a base from the chassis plus everything live; writing a maximum sets the base. Nothing is written to a component: a cracked weapon sent to melee is melee because its owner's passive says so when asked, so an ally reading the same entry gets its own answer and a suppressed passive stops answering. A destroyed component keeps its stat bonuses and loses its abilities. Statuses are modifiers with stacks and a decay rule, on both sides: Burning on a foe and Overheating (placeholder name) on a mech are the same object, ticking per stack at the owner's turn and losing a stack; Suppressed is a one-round modifier; Numbed stacks and does not decay. Order of operations is in the vocabulary as a rule.

8

Commander and city

The mechs are the team; the player is the commander. Commander tools do what mechs cannot: manipulation, mitigation, information, and movement. They are never a fifth gun.

In the skirmish

Recommended

One command die, rolled with the mech dice.

Its faces are rechargeable abilities, so recharge is the roll and nobody hoards a die result. Examples: smoke that blanks the back rank's intents this round, a drone screen that absorbs the next hit, focus fire that adds one to every face on a single target, a scan that reveals the squad's next two rounds. Die size (d4, d6, d8) is a research path with stronger faces and a backfire face or two on the larger dice.

Decided

Reusable commander abilities never deal direct damage.

One-shots may. Damage one-shots belong at the battlefield layer (soften a squad before contact) so mech dice stay the killing power inside a skirmish.

Decided

Command faces come from the hangar, never from skirmish rewards.

Thematically wrong as loot, and they would dominate the reward pick because they affect every skirmish equally.

Open

Is the command die needed at all?

The alternative is one-shots only, with meta upgrades to inventory size and effectiveness, and reroll bundles among the rewards. Claude's view: keep the die, because a rolled face is inherently non-hoardable and the one-shots-only model pushes everything into the inventory the designer already knows they would hoard.

One-shots and the hoarding problem

Decided

Spending a consumable must not feel bad.

The designer is in the camp that hoards potions for a rainy day that never comes. If one-shots are core, the design has to make spending the default.

Recommended

Three fixes.

One-shots picked up in a battle expire when the rift closes, so there is nothing to save them for beyond this battle. The pouch holds three, so a fourth pickup forces use-or-discard on the spot. A starting stock chosen in the hangar restocks every battle; research raises capacity and quality. Doom Eternal solved the same problem the same way: resources regenerate on a short loop, so spending is the default and saving is the anomaly. Saving a full pouch for the boss remains a legitimate blitz strategy with a real cost in missed upgrades.

Skirmish rewards

Recommended

Pick one of three.

A modifier on a mech face you already carry (lasts the battle), a one-shot for the pouch, or a battle-wide boon such as an extra reroll per round. Temporary upgrades amplify the build you brought and never replace it, so the pull is toward synergy.

Decided

Reward types are faction-weighted, never guaranteed.

Undead might give a greater chance of multiple or rarer one-shots, for example. Flavour to be determined per faction.

On the battlefield: city abilities

Decided

Battlefield-level abilities belong to the city.

Sprint, airlift, teleport, tractor beam, orbital strike, and any number of silly ideas. They are upgraded and managed as city systems, and no command die is rolled on the battlefield.

Decided

Cooldowns in battlefield phases are acceptable here.

Each movement phase is a turn, so a cooldown is legible and cannot be hoarded. City research shortens cooldowns, adds charges, and unlocks abilities. Cooldowns still do not exist inside the skirmish, where the die is the recharge.

Recommended

Some city abilities are limited by distance from the gate.

Artillery reaches the southern half, airlift only lifts from the gate, the tractor beam has a range. The push north leaves city support behind, which is a real cost to blitzing and comes out of flavour rather than a rule.

9

Enemy factions

Decided

Archetype-based, not elemental.

Each faction has a behavioral signature that demands a different tactical answer, with internal diversity and general paradigms to guide decisions rather than strict rules. Elements are flavour and the reward currency, never a damage-type triangle. Certain faction combinations are favourable or unfavourable for a given team, which is what makes path choice build-driven.

10

Meta and resources

Decided

Two kinds of gain.

Persistent resources, spent between battles. Temporary upgrades, lasting the current battle only. Skirmish victory yields both.

Decided

Wrecks are recoverable.

Defeated mechs become scrap, salvaged, repaired, or broken down into components between battles. Losses hurt but stay recoverable, which is what prevents a campaign from being lost three battles before it ends.

Decided

Research trees.

Chassis and components, city defenses and city abilities, commander dice and one-shot capacity. Manufacturers give the chassis tree its structure.

11

Technical plan

Recommended

Godot 4 with GDScript.

Chosen on the project's merits, independent of any other codebase. A 2D-first renderer with pixel-art settings built in, hex tile layers, a strong UI toolkit for a game that is mostly panels and dice, Android export out of the box, no build step, and a permissive license with no runtime fee. Unity wins only if a deep ads and in-app-purchase SDK ecosystem is a day-one priority.

Recommended

Keep the simulation pure.

Hex math, movement, skirmish resolution, and map generation are plain scripts with no scene dependencies, driven by a seeded random generator. That makes them testable headless and gives reproducible runs for replays and daily seeds.

Recommended

Save at every phase boundary.

Android can kill a backgrounded app at any moment. If the whole state serializes at the end of each battlefield phase and each skirmish round, resuming is free rather than a retrofit.

Recommended

Cube coordinates for hexes.

Pointy-top rendering with cube or axial coordinates in the logic, so distance, neighbours, and lines are trivial and orientation can change later.

Recommended

Prototype the skirmish first.

It is the riskiest unknown and it defines what a component is, which defines the builder and the meta. It can be played with physical dice and index cards before any code exists, which is also the fastest way to catch overcomplication.

Repository

The game lives in its own folder and repository under Code/riftgate. Design docs live in docs/, the pure simulation in sim/, scenes and presentation in game/, and headless tests in tests/.

Content pipeline

Concepts are authored fantasy-first in docs/concepts/ using the template there, in batches by category. Vetted entries become data in content/*.json (vocabulary, factions, foes, chassis, components), which the game loads at startup. A validator in the test suite enforces the rules in this document, so a concept that breaks one fails the build. New mechanic terms enter the vocabulary first, with a status, and the readable vocabulary document is regenerated.

Open questions and next steps

Open

  • Campaign length, the ultimate goal, and the economy: what repair, research and the Arsenal's gated tiers cost, and over how many battles. Nothing that prices anything (livery repaints, hardpoint unlocks) can be designed before this.
  • The lore holes: what the Council does when the player fails, whether CARE's contract can be lost, whether the Council opened the rifts, the other colonies.
  • Whether the command die exists, or one-shots only.
  • How deep the builder goes, and what repairing a cracked or destroyed component costs in the hangar.
  • Where uncertainty lives in the economy now that component stats are fixed (18 September): randomness in what is offered, never in what a thing is. Ryan's ideas: wrecks the rift belches out across dimensions, salvaged on the battlefield for alien tech; research tokens dropped by monsters, unpacked in the hangar lab into a random design themed on that monster (a fire demon's flamethrower, a flame shield that burns melee attackers). Both feed one hangar screen. Open.
  • Enemy targeting logic among mechs inside a skirmish (lowest shield, highest heat, last attacker, random) and how it is telegraphed.
  • Whether a skirmish participant cap is needed for phone readability.
  • Exact numbers: squad sizes, face powers, shield and heat thresholds, injury thresholds, Rift Pressure tick rate.
  • The game's name; "Riftgate" is a placeholder.
  • Enemy actions beyond the basic attack: which defend, buff, and debuff actions the engine runs first.
  • The horde hooks: which of area damage, taunt, mitigation, and enemy targeting rules carry the few-versus-many fantasy; the commander unit whose death scrambles a squad; how much of the rank and file may act stupidly; three or four columns by default.
  • Whether a four-face die is too small now that rerolls never repeat a face; d6 may be the floor, or rerolls may scale with die size.
  • Map tokens: 24 px sprites from the blendforge render, or the 3D model drawn small; and whether facing on the map matters enough to keep six of them.
  • A proper iconography source. The line-drawn glyphs are placeholders; a licensed icon set (game-icons.net is CC BY) rendered to textures would replace them in one pass.

Pinned

  • Low-hull berserker or last-stand ability.
  • Command die faces with a second, battlefield meaning. Rejected for now because it doubles the learning load per face.
  • Endless mode after the bounded campaign.
  • Hardwired hardpoints with a paid unlock (section 7); tabled until the economy exists.
