Concept and tone

Three nested loops. The campaign is the roguelike run: a sequence of battles, lost for good if the city gate falls. Each battle is a hex-grid battlefield with the gate on the south edge and a rift on the north edge, won by reaching and killing the boss that powers the rift. Each skirmish is a dice-driven fight triggered when mechs and enemy packs end a movement phase adjacent, and it is where the game's depth lives.

Decided

Doom and Space Marine energy.

Over-the-top badassery, played straight. Less overt irony than first planned: propaganda is fine, manufacturer advertisements and Borderlands-style gags are not, because this is the end of civilization. Never edgy, never grimdark fantasy. The city falling is never funny.

Decided

Mechs are grounded military mecha.

Mechwarrior lineage: rugged, brutal, heavy, built from parts that look serviced rather than cobbled together. Not Evangelion. In-world makers exist as provenance and a recognisable livery, in a maintenance-manual voice, never as marketing.

Decided

The Council for the Ultimate Liberation of Terra, and the world it runs.

Decided 16 and 17 September. Humanity's remaining cities are governed by the Council, formally the Council for the Ultimate Liberation of Terra; its acronym is never written or spoken in the game, however obvious, and the joke belongs to the player who notices. Plainly a martial-law regime pretending it is not, it believes its own cause even as it profits from it, which is how it has kept power: the populace has real believers, and the propaganda is sincere. It rewards innovation that helps the fight, which sets the manufacturers competing for its favour, and it keeps its own private military with the best hardware. The register is a god-emperor's court, not a junta; the tone is not the end of the world but "let them try, nobody stands against the march of human progress", said while everything quietly goes to shit. Closer to Pacific Rim than to BattleTech. Open on purpose, as room to expand: whether this is the last bastion of mankind at all, whether other colonies are doing fine and looking away, and whether the Council had a hand in the event that opened the rifts, its rise to central authority being the point of the play, which may become the campaign's spine. Retired: "houses" for the makers, and MARCH as the council's name. Lore is Ryan's; the fantasy field under design is where he keeps inspiration and homages, never shown.

Decided

The player commands for CARE: the underpaid true believers, not the politicians.

Decided 16 and 17 September. The Council pays for the city's defence through CARE, the Civil Auxiliary for Rift Emergencies: an arms-length body with the feel of an independent agency or a contracted corporation, named by the Council in its own condescension (civil, not military; auxiliary, not principal; the apocalypse filed as an emergency) and deliberately lame beside the aptitude of its people. If Ryan decides they named themselves, Agency replaces Auxiliary and the letters stay. The player came up through its ranks to Battlefield Commander: the general, never a politician. CARE's people are the war's true believers, who know why they fight and it is not the Council's reasons: practical, pragmatic, determined, genuine badasses with a rebellious streak, the lone-gunslinger action-hero register played straight and never winked at. The Council expects results and takes the credit, none of the responsibility. Its own R&D house, the Arsenal, sells CARE a trickle of its technology, the good stuff but never the best, and is the one research path the player cannot force: higher tiers come only as the campaign turns dire enough for the Council to loosen its grip. Holes to fill: what the Council does when the player fails, whether the contract can be lost, how the credit-taking turns into plot.

Decided

Enemies are old Final Fantasy monsters.

Detailed, over-the-top high fantasy, contrasted against the gritty realism of the player units. One genre invading another is the dichotomy the game wants, and it replaces most of the verbal irony.

Decided

Sound: industrial instrumental with house-trap elements, grooving rather than heavy; effects grounded and heavy, never Star Wars.

Ryan, 22 September, the direction for all audio. Music is a heavy industrial core with driving flow and tempo, carried by the beat: not too fast, more grooving than heavy even in the battle themes, musically complex and unique. Inspirations: Tool, Nine Inch Nails, The Omnific, Teho, Hozho. The map and the eventual hangar are obviously more subdued than the skirmish but still groove along with a solid bop. Effects design follows the same rule: grounded and heavy, weight and machinery, nothing that sounds like a space opera. The reason it matters: the more grounded the sound, the more silliness the game can get away with elsewhere, so the sound carries the seriousness the tone rows above ask for. A first set in this register exists (22 September): every effect and a map groove and a skirmish groove, synthesised from written recipes by tools/soundforge the way lowforge builds the models, so the two screens' tempo split and every hook (dice, blows, the contact band, the handover, the gate, the rift) can be heard now and replaced piece by piece when there are real recordings and a real track. Who makes those is in section 12.

The second cut (23 September, after the first was measured and Ryan's notes on it). Built at 48 kHz. Weight lives in the 150 to 800 Hz band a phone speaker can play: a body under every thud, saturation harmonics kept, the sub a bonus for headphones. Mechanical, not industrial revolution: damped knocks that die in tens of milliseconds, servos, hydraulics and relays, never a bell or an anvil left to ring. Every pitched thing is a stack with undertones and overtones, and a small room sits on everything, so nothing reads as chip-tune. The mix lives in the forge as a loudness per sound (the loudest 50 ms); a hook passes an offset only for a relative moment (the enemy's blows, a stack of kills). Big moments (contact, the dive, the cut, the gate, a kill, the outcome) dip the music for a moment and let it climb back. Each loop is sixteen bars in sections, not a two-bar phrase repeated, and a loop resumes where it left when its screen comes back. The pitched sounds play at their written pitch; blows and knocks get a hair of random pitch. The display's scan and idle stay silent: a constant sound on a phone is fatigue, and the music covers it.

Ryan's notes on hearing the map loop (23 September, later), now rules for the music. The chimes over the groove stay and get more room. Never a build, a bass drop or a speed snare: house and trap are influences for the groove, never for the "about to drop" moment, so a section changes by what plays, not by a roll into it. The interlude (the kick out, the chimes carrying it) is the best part and runs eight bars; loops are 32 bars, since long music that does not tire is worth the budget. The crunch bass of the first cut (one saw driven hard and clipped, one fixed low-pass) fit better than the cleaner filtered stack, being deliberately rough, and came back. A little metal is allowed at low gain, inharmonic and gone in a tenth of a second, in a few blows and once every couple of bars; only the cowbell was the problem. The two loops must not read as the same piece: the map stays at 92 (84 was tried the same evening and was too slow; the contrast comes from the instruments, not the tempo) on its own kit (a soft kick, a rim for a snare, brushed hats, the pad and the chimes forward), the skirmish on kick, snare, bright hats, clanks and saw stabs on the chord, with the pad and the bass timbre shared for cohesion. Sound.TEMPO follows the map's tempo, since the rift's vortex steps on the beat.

Open, the same evening: the palette. The map loop's writing is right (the chimes' riffs engage the ear) but its tone and kit read as the Loop Hero soundtrack, which is the pulse-and-saw chiptune-adjacent palette, not the writing; the industrial noises (a machine swell, a ratchet tick, knocks) were too frequent or not the thing, and the swell is gone and the tick keeps to the interlude. The brief is now: keep the driving groove and push musical complexity; the trap-industrial-metal label was the original brief, not a constraint. The proposal is to make the game's dichotomy the mixing rule: the human side is FM keys and bells, plucked and chugged strings, distorted low synth and drums in a room (Descent, MechWarrior 2, progressive metal); the rift side is choir, strings, bells, orchestral hits and fantasy harmony (Final Fantasy, the liturgical 40K register); the map is mostly human-side, the skirmish is the seam where both play at once, and the beat is constant across both. Complexity comes from harmony and counterpoint (chords moving every bar, a second voice answering the chimes, polymeter) rather than fills. Three eight-bar sketches on one riff (forge.py sketch: human, rift, djent) are rendered for Ryan to A/B; the loops are rebuilt in whichever wins. The skirmish tune also wants the map's counterpoint and chord motion, whatever the palette.

Decided from the sketches (Ryan, 23 September, late): the human sketch was plain beside the djent one, no contest. The fight is in the djent palette: the kick slaps and the drums may go harder into metal (a clicky kick, a cracking snare, a crash at every fourth bar, a tom in the turn), the choir a touch up but never so far that it turns gothic metal, the bass a bass guitar rather than the synth crunch (too electronic under the metal), and the loop carries unison riff sections (chug, bass and a lead on one riff in octaves, the drums on its hits, no pad: The Omnific, Dream Theater, not quite a solo) as well as drawn-out ones (open chords at half time, the choir and the bells). The map is in the rift palette, which is the contrast: underwater, the echo kept, and pushed creepier and more otherworldly, more electronic and glitchy against the fight's grounded metal (crushed clicks, stuttering bells, a choir on "oo" pumping on the eighths, a tritone in the interlude's chords, a backwards swell into every section, one orchestral hit at the interlude's door). The bells and the choir play on both sides for cohesion. Which palette goes to which screen is a one-line swap if it ever wants reversing.

The third listen (Ryan, 23 September, late): sections must stay long enough to engage before moving on, so both loops are 48 bars in eight-bar sections. The map's bass is organic, not electronic (pushing it electronic sent it away from Final Fantasy's orchestral epicness): pizzicato contrabass under the groove, bowed cellos under the interlude, and the orchestra's sections trade the tune, with a strings section (the violins carrying it, horns answering, a harp running the chord, timpani) beside the bells, the choir and the strings as pads by section. Exposed chords belong on the map; the fight keeps one four-bar stretch of open chords and something moves on top of it (the lead pulsing sixteenths over a galloping double kick). The fight is riff-heavy with the kick forward: double kicks, kick rolls into the turns (kick rolls are fine; snare rolls are not), syncopated and ghosted snares, the crunch bass only in the lift rather than throughout, and its instruments trade by section too: the chug and the bass guitar, then the crunch synth with electric piano stabs, the unison riff, then a keys-and-guitar section where the lead carries a tune over the piano's comping. The fight's chime is its own instrument, brighter and more metal than the map's bell.

The fourth listen (Ryan, 23 September, night). The map: the backing under the interlude paused between bars (each chord's pad had its own attack and release, and the cello notes stopped short), so the pad now crossfades from chord to chord inside a run and held notes are legato, a continuous drone; the violins' section is right; a discord section is added where every lead plays at once, each in its own count (three against five against seven) over chords with a tritone in each; the pizzicato read electronic and chiptune, so the bass is a bowed contrabass in short bows, with the body's resonance and the bow's grit. The fight: the leads together were harsh enough to hurt after a while, so the guitar lead and the metal chime are softened, the hats and the master's tilt pulled back, while the undercurrent (which was right, industrial) stays; the soft bass section stays; solos are wanted in the Dream Theater way, so a guitar solo section (runs up and down the minor scale over the chug and a busy kick, a tom fill into its turn) and a keyboard solo section (a synth lead's syncopated runs over the piano's comping, the bass guitar answering with runs of its own) replace the open-chord stretch; the drums go wilder still under the solos. A slightly slower map (88) is rendered beside the loop to compare; 84 was too slow.

The effects, two registers (Ryan, 23 September, night). The interface is sci-fi electronic: beeps and boops, StarCraft's menu as the register, pushed a little toward comedy to see where it lands, never a Doom or Quake menu (the relays and plates of the second cut read as one). Combat and the map are over-the-top weighty: lasers, gunfire, explosions and melee of various kinds with Doom levels of punch, for both sides. The sound of a machine's blow follows its weapon: energy (a static face that never crits) is a laser, a kinetic line weapon is gunfire and its crit the cannon, indirect fire is the cannon, melee is a slash and its crit a slam; a foe's blow is a slash, or a slam when it is big (a placeholder threshold); a kill and the gate struck are explosions. The dice sounds belong to the interface (the display computing), Resolve is electronic with the console's latch kept for weight, the contact horn gains a klaxon, the dive counts in with beeps. Every section of both loops is listed with timestamps by forge.py sections, so notes can name a section.

The map's fifth listen (Ryan, 23 September, night). 88 was lazy in the held notes; 90 was rendered beside it and is perfect over 92 (Ryan, 24 September), so the map runs at 90. The urgency at 92 was the kick's voice, not the tempo: the map's drum is now a soft taiko with little front and a long tail in a big room, on one and three only, inevitable rather than pushy. The whole loop is airier and softer: the bells down and longer with a deeper echo, the horn down, the choir's pulse gentler, more room on the master, and the contrabass kept to the lift, the strings section and the climax, with cellos low and legato under the groove where the bows sat oddly. In the interlude the strings change chord late, on the "and" of one, against the cellos on the bar. The strings section is right. The discord was misread: what was wanted is every lead running its own melody at once over the same groove, the discord in how they miss each other, a crescendo to the loop's climax and no change in flow; the count-against-count version is gone and that is what plays now, a horn line, a violin line, the bells' line, the harp's run and the cellos' roots, swelling across the eight bars.

The fight's sixth listen (Ryan, 23 September, night): levelling. The bass buried the other instruments and, in the leads' sections, floundered in the middle, overpowering and boring at once; a bass must take a back seat or kick up a notch. So the chug and the bass guitar play at full only in the riff; the crunch synth alone under the lift (which is right as it is); under the keys a soft sub bass on the roots; under the guitar solo the chug darker and quieter; under the keyboard solo the bass guitar driven and answering with runs. The leads come up and each sits in its own band (the guitar lead, the synth lead and the piano high-passed out of the bass's range), the master cuts a low shelf so they have room, and the mix is checked by measuring each section's low band against its lead band. The drums: the kick is right; the cymbals come out, with a ride under the keys and both solos, a china on the "and" of four before every section and on the solo's accents, and prog displacements on the snare (the unison's snare on the riff's accents, the guitar solo's snare off the beat, the keyboard solo in a half-time feel with the ride's bell).

Two experiments (Ryan, 24 September), rendered as sketches beside the loops, not in the game. Meters: the engine now takes a steps-per-bar per section, so a section can run in 3/4, 5/4, 6/8 or 7/8 and a run can alternate bars of four and three; meter_skirmish plays the riff, then bars of four and three alternating (Ryan's four-three), then a 7/8 section, then the riff; meter_map plays the groove, a 5/4 strings section, a 6/8 interlude, the groove. Realism: a palette built as close to real instruments as the forge can go (a cabinet's resonances on plucked power chords and a lead string with late vibrato, a bass with the finger's thump and the body's peak, bowed ensembles of eight players each with their own vibrato and late start, a horn with a scoop and lip noise, a choir with breath, drums as skin, shell, wires, beater and room, every hit rendered in three variants and timing and velocity humanised); real_map and real_skirmish are the first sixteen bars of each loop on it, for Ryan's theory that a mix of lower- and higher-fidelity voices could be the sound, once he has heard the far end.

Ryan's notes on the map and the experiments (24 September). The map: the new kick is right but a little beatboxy, so it is fuller (the skin's low tone held under it, a knock at 200 Hz) and no further; the horns in the groove were too subtle to notice and come up; the contrabass draws its notes out a little; the interlude's late chord change could not be heard with the cellos on the bar, so the cellos change on the "and" of one and the upper strings on two, wild on purpose; the discord is on track with its levels evened. The realism sketch had lost its kick (a timpani does not read as one; an orchestral bass drum does), its horn was a little loud where the original was too quiet (met in the middle), its lift's solo line was a miss (gone), and sixteen bars were not enough to judge, so the realism sketches are now the whole loops with real voices swapped in (real=True on either loop). The meters: 5/4 does not work by jamming the tune into it and is dropped; the interlude moving on unexpectedly to a bar of 7/8 now and then (not every phrase) and a 6/8 section have merit, and the 6/8's triplets should ride the 4/4 bar's length rather than run at the same step, so the engine takes a step scale per section and the map's meter sketch does both.

The fight's seventh listen and the realism verdict (Ryan, 24 September). The realism run's riff section was really cool: the mechanical sound of the real guitar's backing, the drum voice, and the real bass under the keys (the finger's pluck audible), while the original electric piano was perfect and the realism ride hurt. So the fight is on a hybrid palette now: real drums (the crash and snare shortened, the ride lower and rounder), the real guitar for the chug, the real bass everywhere with more character and longer notes, taking the lift's riff too; the original piano; a distorted real lead for the guitar solo (it needed more gain to carry one), the synth lead for the unison and the keyboard solo (the synth solo voice was chirpy and is gone). The pure-synth fight is kept as a sketch to compare. By section: the kick down, busy but not in the face; the unison's drums a restrained groove, since a kit hitting every note of the riff killed it (a drum solo was the other road); the keys section right, with the ride down and its cadence broken up by little fills and rolls in the Peart way; the guitar solo's kick tamer and its line rewritten as phrases with rests, melodic rather than punchy scales; the keyboard solo at half time with crashes rather than a running ride, its line long and melodic with the bass's own moving line firing at the same time, not answering. The four-three alternation has merit as occasional flair, never a continuous section; 7/8 needs a section written for it rather than the tune jammed in; both wait for a later pass.

The map's realism verdict (Ryan, 24 September): the real version won by a significant margin and is the map. The orchestral bass drum beats the timpani, a little louder, and it had been distorting the whole track: the mix was running into the master's saturation, so every kit sound is normalised and the map is given headroom before that stage. The real cellos, violins and horns are better across the board; the strings' note ends were a touch sudden for the string vibe and are softened. The groove's horns come down a little; in the strings section the horns answer in three notes, the first softer and the next two coming up. The climax is awesome and runs twelve bars. Of the meter experiment, the interlude moving on to a bar of 7/8 works when the melody is written to land on the cut, and the one at the end of the eight-bar phrase worked where the one mid-phrase did not, so each interlude phrase now closes on a 7/8 bar with its lines written for it; the 6/8 triplets work but only as disturbing flair where needed, never a section, and wait for a place. The synth map is kept as a sketch to compare.

A correction on the fight's bass (Ryan, 24 September): the voice he had praised under the keys was the soft sub bass, not the plucked real bass, and the intent was never one bass everywhere. So the soft sub sits under the keys, takes the lift's riff (heard there as he asked), and runs the moving line under the keyboard solo; the driven real bass stays under the riff, the unison and the guitar solo, well under the guitar rather than at the level that had made the groove's bass too loud. When a note can be read two ways, ask before building.

The map's seventh listen (Ryan, 24 September). The bass drum, no longer distorting, came through in headphones but not on speakers, so it is louder with a knock at 200 Hz a speaker can play, and the loops are now levelled by their body (mean level, pinned where each loop was judged) rather than by their loudest moment, which had let a louder drum drag the rest of the track down. The groove's horns a bit quieter, the bells quieter across the board, the interlude's cellos down. The 7/8 feels really good: the interlude now closes each half on one (the fourth bar and the eighth), and two of the turns drop a beat too (into the interlude, into the climax), which is the classic prog move of a turn that moves on early; the turns into the lift and back into the groove stay in four. The strings section runs twelve bars and its horns break in at unpredictable places, three swelling notes each time; the violins are louder where they carry the melody and in the climax, the string drone left as it was. The reprise after the climax read oddly (groove, lift, interlude, groove, turn, lift, interlude round the loop), so it is gone: the loop is groove, lift, interlude, strings, climax, and the climax's turn releases into the groove on the wrap. In the game the map's music had sometimes begun mid-lift: the loop resumes where it left after a fight by design, and that carried across battles too; a new battle now starts it from the top, once the map is built and drawn.

The map's eighth listen (Ryan, 24 September). The climax is incredible and could stay longer, but it just ended, so it has a closer: four bars of a tutti chord held and let go over the timpani, a hit at its front, the bells walking the chord down. The triplet experiment found its place as the reprise after the closer: eight bars in 6/8 at the 4/4 bar's length, the pulse a triplet of the groove's, the groove's material wound down soft, whose turn releases into the groove on the wrap; strings into climax is untouched. The 7/8 turns had the right idea but sounded cut off by accident rather than intent, so the turns are strangely long instead: 9/8 lingering into the interlude, 5/4 lingering into the climax, each with its own bell figure, horn call and bow written to fill the extra beats; the interlude's 7/8 bars stay. The strings section's horns break in six times, three at its phrase ends and three astray. The violins' tails were still cutting hard enough to sound like brass, so their fall is longer and their notes overlap the next as bows do. forge.py audition renders every effect into one file, a second apart, with each one's time, so the set can be heard through in a sitting.

The map's ninth listen (Ryan, 24 September), and a rule: the kick keeps its places for the whole track, so any play with time signatures happens over a bar of the same length, the beat steady under it. That retires the 9/8 turn (not sold on it: right as an entry to the interlude, wrong as a tail to the lift, and it moved the beat) and the 5/4 turn (not the janky transition wanted): the turn into the interlude is plain again, the turn into the climax is a triplet bar (6/8 over the bar's length, the kick on one and three exactly where they always fall), and the interlude's 7/8 closes stay. The complexity was approaching busy rather than wistful, so: the bass drum up again, the bells down again, the lift's horns quieter, and the loop opens on eight bars of the groove bare (cellos, bells, the choir, nothing in front) before the horns come in. The strings section's climb was rewritten on the beat (it had been a janky offbeat) and its horns, which were too loud and awkwardly placed, now break in on beats only and quieter; the contrabass under it had summed into a clipped edge with its notes overlapping, so it is gentler and no longer overlaps. The closer is great. The 6/8 reprise is cool but does not belong in the loop: it is out, and kept as a sketch, since it flows well enough to seed a second track the map could crossfade into instead of looping.

The map's tenth listen (Ryan, 24 September): almost perfect. The bass drum is pulled back a little under the groove and the strings and brought up a little more under the interlude and the climax, and its tail is longer still. The bells' tone had not changed when they came down; only their echo went down with them, and with it the depth, so the dry note stays low and the echo is kept stronger. The lift's bows are extended and legato. The interlude's 7/8 bar had two timpani hits at its end; the second is gone. The strings section is sixteen bars: the violins' line continued through two new phrases, the cellos under it in two-note chords (root and tenth), the harp up, and eleven horn swells that thicken through the second half into a build. The strings section and the interlude are swapped, so the loop runs groove bare, groove, lift, strings, a triplet turn into the interlude, the interlude closing on its 7/8 into the climax, the climax (sixteen bars now, its violins and horns up) and the closer, which releases into the bare groove. The 6/8 reprise sketch stays the starting point for a second map track.

The map's eleventh listen (Ryan, 24 September). The lift's cellos had grown loud and its tune had changed: the bow line written the pass before was forty steps long, and a pattern that does not divide into a bar walks round it by design (the seven-step chug works that way), so the bows had been drifting through the lift; a bar-length line again, quieter, and the bells' echo back where it was. The strings section: the horns a little louder, the drone strings swelling through the second half, the second half's violins on the riff's own long-note pattern rather than off-book, and fuller four-note chords under it. The 7/8 bar's extra hit was the bass drum, not the timpani, and is gone. The triplet turn moves to between the interlude and the climax, and a plain turn takes strings into the interlude. The climax's balance is right and its volume is shaped now, two swells and two falls across the sixteen bars, rising to the closer, which has more horn carrying it.

The map's twelfth listen (Ryan, 24 September: almost done). The lift's five-note line (three, a rest, two faster, echoing the riff) is back exactly; the walk that replaced it was the wrong fit. The strings section: its first half is perfect, so the second half opens the same way and then follows the riff's own long notes over the second half's chord progression, which is right; and the expected horns arrive at every two-bar phrase's end, with the astray ones kept. The strings swell in an almost inspiring, major way, and a turn bar out of nowhere before the discordant interlude was wrong, so a two-bar bridge takes its place: the swell decaying into a low cello, a dissonance creeping in, a timpani roll into the interlude. The climax is so epic that the triplet turn alone was not enough suspense before it, so four quiet bars in 6/8 follow the turn (the choir, a low cello, the bells sparse) until the climax lands with a hit. The climax itself is twenty bars and its instruments rise and fall one at a time, noticeably, each the lead for a moment before fading back (horn, violin, bells, harp, cellos, contrabass in turn), then a tutti rise into the closer, which is good.

Names and two more tracks (Ryan, 24 September). The map loop is the rift theme and the fight loop the human theme; they stay on their screens for now, and the final product may run several tracks a screen, since music is meant to be a big part of this game after what has gone into it. Each theme has a calm sibling now, a subdued background track in the same palette that embraces the house roots more: a steady soft kick, off-beat opens, a sub pulsing under everything, pads pumped or swept. The rift calm grows the 6/8 reprise into a whole track: 6/8 at the 4/4 bar's length throughout, the choir pumping, the bells in threes with their long echo, cellos on the roots, a soft bass drum on one and three, a lift with the violins and the harp, a breakdown with the cellos late and a dissonance, an outro that thins into the intro. The human calm is four on the floor and industrial (Ryan: it fits the background brief): the piano stabbing off the beat, the metal chime as the hook, the guitar muted low as a rhythm, the crunch synth dark under the sub, knocks, ticks, a clank, crushed clicks, a stack cymbal, one section for a synth lead in long notes and one for the crunch synth on the riff, a choir under the breakdown. Both are named and levelled a little under their themes, listed in Sound.NAMES and TEMPO, and not yet hooked to a screen.

The map's music is the two calms, for now (Ryan, 25 September: both very good). A screen asks for a set of loops (Sound.SETS): the map's is rift B then human B, one full loop each, the ending loop crossing into the next over two seconds at its seam, round and round; the skirmish's is human A alone. A set coming back after a fight resumes its current loop where it left; a new battle starts the set from rift B's top. Rift A, rift C and human C sit in the table unhooked. This is to judge the crossfade before deciding whether a set should cycle or pick.

The map's two, brought up to date (Ryan, 25 September: go ahead with the assessment; both at the same volume-ish, the other tracks' levels waiting on where they end up). Design tests

Every mechanic proposed for the skirmish is held to these. They exist because the designer's stated tendency is to overcomplicate for complexity's sake, and the goal is elegant fun.

Decided

A round takes about thirty seconds

of thought on a phone and contains one to three real decisions.

Decided

The build is visible every round.

You watch your components act. They are never just stat bonuses.

Decided

Two sources of randomness, both the player's, both visible.

The die decides what you can do; the damage roll decides how well. Kinetic faces roll a range and may crit; energy faces are static. No hidden hit rolls. Enemies never roll: their intents are telegraphed as exact numbers, as in Griftlands.

Decided

The player always has tools against the roll.

Rerolls before or after attacking, faces that raise the floor or deal the average, execute and finish effects that guarantee lethal, and visible enemy health. Variance is a tactical choice to mitigate, never a punishment.

Decided

Every pack poses a question,

such as finishing the resurrectors or interrupting the big hit, rather than being a damage race with different art.

Decided

Mismatch costs time, never ability.

A wrong loadout for a pack chews longer or eats more damage. It is never unable to act.

Decided

Never helpless, sometimes unlucky.

A bad roll with an interesting answer is where the fun lives. The design removes helplessness, not luck.

Decided

Hard cap on effects in play.

Zero to one chassis passive, where a passive is one idea in at most three rule lines: an upside and its cost count as one passive, two unrelated benefits count as two. Component passives are rare. Every trigger is shown on screen when it fires.

Battlefield layer

The battlefield decides where, when, and with whom you fight. It is deliberately the layer with positioning, so the skirmish can have none.

Map

Decided

Hex grid, gate south, rift north.

Procedurally generated terrain: water, impassable mountains, and more. One mech per hex. One enemy pack per hex. City automated defenses stand on the field near the gate and can be targeted.

Decided

The battlefield may be taller than one screen.

Seven wide stays the rule for thumbs. Height may exceed a screen and scroll vertically, with the camera following the active unit. Pointy-top hexes make defensive lines straight horizontal rows and force advancing enemies to zigzag. With cube coordinates in the logic, orientation stays a rendering choice.

Decided

Multi-hex objects.

A massive boss can occupy three hexes in a triangle. The gate and the rift are wider than one hex. Packs and mechs stay single-hex. Epic scale without player overhead. Simulation cost: units gain a footprint, and blocking, adjacency, and pathing treat every footprint hex as occupied.

Decided

Map presentation rules.

Pack count is one sprite plus a number badge for now, maybe a small, medium, large count later. Player units face the way they move, with three authored directions mirrored to six; battlefield sprites omit visible customization. Mechs get a per-class idle; enemies get no animation except attacks, in the old Final Fantasy way. If overflow hides something that matters, the overflow allowance is too big; partially hidden objects get an outline highlight.

Phase resolution

Decided

Enemy moves are previewed. Player plots routes and commits.

Every unit has a movespeed. Enemy packs usually head for the gate or the city defenses, occasionally for player units. The player sees where each pack will go, then plots each mech's route and commits.

Recommended

Player routes resolve first, then enemies execute their previewed moves and stop at the first blocked hex.

The preview is exact unless a mech ends its move on a pack's path. Body-blocking is the core operational verb, and forming lines is how the gate gets defended. Flyers can engage but cannot block, or blocking breaks. Superseded 22 September by the zone of control row in section 5: a pack stops beside a grounded machine rather than in front of it, packs never swerve for a machine, and flyers control nothing.

Decided

A route is tapped or drawn: the tap takes the cheapest way, the drag says which way round.

Built 25 September, from the rough edge that routes were tap-to-destination only: a tap could never say which side of a pack to walk, and the zone of control makes the way there matter as much as the end. Drag from one of the player's machines and the route follows the finger: each hex it reaches joins the draft along the cheapest continuation that fits the move left (a finger that skips a hex still leaves a walkable route), a hex already on the draft cuts it back to there, back onto the machine clears it, and letting go keeps the route exactly as drawn, with a plaque at its end counting the move spent against the whole. A drag that starts anywhere else still scrolls; a second finger cancels the draft and pinches. A drawn route is judged on the same terms as a tapped one and planned as a re-plot (the machine last): every step adjacent and standable in the planned view, within the move, its end nobody else's destination; refused, it plays the back sound and leaves the machine unplotted. The tap keeps its meaning, so nothing a player already knows changed.

Decided

Adjacency after movement triggers a skirmish.

A player unit adjacent to an enemy pack, plus any units touching those, forms a skirmish. Multiple skirmishes in one phase resolve one at a time. Amended 28 September by the engagement-range row below: touching still seeds the fight, and every grounded machine within two hexes of it joins.

Recommended

A skirmish is the whole connected group of adjacent units.

A clustered wave becomes one dangerous fight, which teaches the player to split blobs and pick off stragglers. Cap participants only if phone readability demands it.

Decided

The boss's contact takes the phase: one fight, every machine in it, no choice.

Ryan, 25 September, built that evening. When any contact after a resolve holds the boss, the phase's other contacts are dropped (packs standing in the boss's own group included), every machine on the field is committed whatever its hex, and the fight opens on its own: no contact panel, no ENGAGE. Positioning has done its work by then; reaching the boss is the battle's end or a withdrawal. On the screen the family's boss theme starts from its top at the moment of contact (section 1 sound row), the map dims and looks at the boss, and a reveal plays over it (the black band with red rules, the boss's picture flying up out of its hex and growing with red afterimages, brackets collapsing onto it as the feed acquires it, its family typing in, its name resolving out of a scramble, the order to engage typing under, the picture tearing while it is held; a tap ends it), then the ordinary handover dives into the skirmish. The withdraw rule is unchanged: a withdrawal returns to the field with the boss still standing. Rift Pressure does not yet feed packs into the boss fight; the plan in section 1 says it should, and it is open. Ryan, 29 September, on the reveal running: it works well enough for now; the dive had aimed at the centre of the map rather than the boss's tile, since the group's centroid is the whole team's, fixed the same day (the boss's hex alone). On the pressure: a valid decision whether bum-rushing the boss should be easier. Feeding the pressure's spawns into the boss fight makes it a race between the temporary power gained on the way and a boss that grows harder and better reinforced the longer it waits, which he finds very interesting and very intimidating to balance, since it hinges on how strong the in-battle temporary upgrades turn out to be (they do not exist yet); it fits the complexity he is after and puts more roguelike into the map phase. Discuss first, then decide, after the upgrades exist.

Pacing: a race to the rift

Decided

No in-field repair. The battle keeps pushing forward.

It must feel like a race to close the rift before the city is overwhelmed, not a back-and-forth war simulation. Slow-and-steady versus blitz is the player's choice, including leaving a defender at the gate.

Decided

Damage sticks. Mitigation is a real concern.

Hull never repairs during a battle. Mitigation comes from recharging shields and from defensive tactics, not from healing.

Decided

A standing shield blocks the blow whole; in a fight, shield comes only from components.

Decided 18 September, after discussion. However small the shield and however big the blow, nothing bleeds through to the hull; the blow still costs the shield its damage. That makes a ten-point shield a real decision every round a big hit is telegraphed, gives glass cannons tension, and leaves no room for an in-combat trickle (dropped the same day: a passive may bring it back for one chassis as a niche). Out of combat, shield returns each battlefield phase at the chassis's field rate; never a free refill between skirmishes, so withdraw-and-re-engage is not a reset. Foes get past a shield by striking twice (double strike) or, for bosses, by breaching: the excess overflows into hull. Rates and sizes are placeholders.

Decided

Armour is a flat number off every bare-hull blow, and zero is fine.

Decided 18 September. A chassis stat (components may add to it later), never a percentage, taken off each blow that lands on hull with no shield standing. A blow smaller than the armour does nothing: a Badger drives its treads over a horde of ghouls laughing, and that is the point. A ghoul that only ever claws for ten deserves to be walled; the answer is monsters that do more than claw (double strike, breach, abilities), not a damage floor. The Raptor has no armour and stays afraid of ghouls. Shield is not armoured: a blow on shield costs the shield its full damage, so shield answers the big blow and armour answers the swarm, and a bruiser and a glass cannon are different builds. The per-round Rift Pressure tick is what keeps a long wall from being free.

Decided

Foes strike in the order they stand, and the heaviest deploy left.

Decided 18 September. Every blow resolves on its own against the shield standing at that moment, so strike order decides what a small shield blocks. No initiative numbers, nothing to micromanage: front rank left to right, then the back rank. At first contact the heaviest hitters (attack times strikes) deploy left, ties in arrival order, so the player sees the big blow coming from the jump. A round cap on skirmishes was rejected: it breaks the flow like an advertisement in a show.

Recommended

One Rift Pressure clock.

A single bar that ticks once per battlefield phase and once per skirmish round, and spawns a fresh pack at the rift when it fills. Dawdling on the map and grinding in a skirmish feed the same meter. It is the race made mechanical, and it replaces any hard limit on skirmish length.

Decided

No persistent movespeed penalties from damage.

Combined with terrain, limping mechs make runs unwinnable with no warning. Hull damage has no secondary penalty in the first prototype.

Open

The battlefield as a landscape: ravines, mesas and gulleys, half chosen and half rift-twisted.

Ryan, 17 September, direction to design against. The ground before the gate is not a field with obstacles on it. It is a landscape the defenders shaped on purpose, ravines and gulleys that split and funnel the horde toward the killing field in front of the gate, and that the rift twists a little more every battle as pieces of the other side bleed through, which is why no two battles share a map. Consequences to work out: the procedural map should group features (contiguous roads, rock ranges, water courses) rather than scatter them; terrain should have high and low, cliff faces and rises that can be leaped and that favour whoever holds the top, alongside plain impassables like mesas and boulders, presented in a way that is not jank; new terrain types bleed in from the rift and grow more frequent near it, bone yards, lava fields, an eldritch nether; and pathfinding should be a decision, not "go around the rock". Built 21 and 22 September: features are laid from noise fields as bodies, clusters and ridges (BattleMap._lay_landscape), roads as a drifting trunk with branches, crags in their ground's idiom; relief exists but is landscape only, never read by a rule (decided 21 September). Still open from this direction: whether height ever becomes a rule (cliffs, rises, who holds the top), and the rift-side terrain types (bone yards, lava, nether) that grow more frequent near the rift.

Recommended

Packs show a likely-loot icon.

Reward types are faction-weighted (section 8). The pack shows the likely type, never the item, so path choice is informed without a table.

Skirmish layer

The skirmish decides how you fight. Touchstone: Slice and Dice. Target prioritization, semi-controlled randomness, and build preparation and synergy. Several variants were considered, including a zoomed-in hex grid; that one was set aside because it repeats the battlefield's decision type, fights the no-micromanagement goal, and is hostile to thumbs in portrait.

Round loop

Recommended

No positioning on the player side.

Mechs have no ranks and never shuffle. Enemies organize around them.

Recommended

Attack, then reroll: one interleaved phase, then the enemy, then straight back.

Every mech's die is rolled. Tap to lock, drag to spend, reroll up to twice at any point, before or after attacking, so a low damage roll is information for the rest of the round. End turn resolves the enemy and starts the next round at once, with no click to begin your turn. This is the loop damage ranges earn, and it is the thing that keeps the game from being Slice and Dice with extra layers. Rerolls are per round, never a pooled stock.

Decided

Damage ranges and crits, by weapon class.

Kinetic faces show a minimum and a maximum, roll between them the moment they land, and may crit for double. Energy faces show one number and never roll or crit: reliable and hot against swingy and cool. Crits are where the exciting passives live. There is no undo; the roll is the commitment. Numbers are in tens.

Decided

Hold a die to see every face.

Deciding whether to reroll needs the whole die, not the face showing. Hold lists every face with the current one marked; double-tap opens the same list as a codex box. Passives are not shown on the die; they belong to the mech.

Decided

No hard round limit.

A cap would punish defensive, value, status, reflect, and mitigation builds in favour of burst. Skirmish length is governed by the Rift Pressure clock, the withdraw option, and tuning (typical skirmish three to five rounds; enemy damage must outpace sustain so stalemates cannot happen).

Decided

Player-initiated withdraw, and its price is one enemy turn.

Decided 19 September. Withdraw is offered at any point of the player's turn, the first round included. Its price is always the same: the enemy takes its turn, then the team flees at the end of the round, so there is no leapfrogging around contact unscathed. Being forced into contact is bad strategy, not bad luck, and gets the same price. Withdrawing ends the skirmish; survivors on both sides carry their damage back to the map and the pack marches on. Replaces the 15 September rule (start of round only, before any face was spent).

Decided

The enemy's turn is seen, blow by blow, before the next round or the flight.

Ryan, 23 September, after a withdrawal put the outcome up over a turn nobody saw. The enemy's turn replays one blow a beat: the foe lunges at its machine, and as the blow lands that machine's shield and hull step down to what it left, a broken shield shattering; the log types the blows in as they play. Only then do the new dice show, or the outcome (a withdrawal included: the parting blows are the price, so they are watched). The player's taps are held off while it plays. The beat is a setting, ENEMY PACE, whose top is the old instant replay (0.12 s between blows) and whose default is 0.45 s.

Decided

Ends on: pool empty (victory), all hulls zero (defeat), or withdraw.

Victory awards resources and a temporary upgrade choice. Defeat turns the mechs to scrap: wrecks to be salvaged after the battle. Surviving enemies continue their assault.

Enemy formation

Decided

The formation is four columns wide.

Ryan, 23 September, after playing both: four plays better than three. Two ranks of four, so eight foes stand at once and a pack of six fills more than a front; a massive foe still spans both ranks of its column. The harness no longer offers a choice; the simulation keeps the width as a parameter for tests of the rank rules.

Decided

A pack is one enemy type. Composition comes from the map.

An enemy hex is one foe type times a strength. What you fight in a skirmish is whatever packs are chained together at contact, so the rift's formation and how the player disrupts it decide the composition. Factions describe the formation they send through the rift together. Bosses are single three-hex units.

Decided

Ranks hold, and reinforcements step in at the end of the enemy turn.

Ryan, 17 September. A front-rank foe only ever stands in the front, a back-rank foe only in the back, even when the other rank has an empty slot: a ghoul in the back row raises "how is that hitting me" and the answer is never worth it. The pool is now called reinforcements, in the interface too. At the first deployment they take slots in the order they arrived, so a formation is predictable; at the end of every enemy turn the reinforcement phase fills each empty slot, left to right and front rank first, with a random waiting foe of the right rank, and the log says how many stepped in. Ryan wants ways for the player to interact with that phase; open. A kill clears its slot at once (Ryan, 22 September): burning a hole through the front line with one die opens the column to line and melee weapons for the rest of the round, a brief window on the back rank before the reinforcement phase plugs it. Earlier that day a killed foe stood until the round's end and still struck, and the reinforcement that took its slot read as a resurrection; now the dead foe's drawing flashes, drops and fades where it stood, and reinforcements visibly step in after the enemy's replay. A decided fight ends at once too: the last foe down, or the last machine burnt out by its own heat, shows the outcome without an End turn (the one shield a player could have gained first is not worth the jank). Facing, also decided: player units face the foes (a rear three-quarter, turned to the left so the claw side shows; Ryan, 18 September, replacing the front-right convention of the day before) and enemies face left; the ghoul already does, and the foeforge prompt now asks for it.

Decided

One fixed semantic palette; the HUD picker changes chrome only.

Decided 18 September from swatch pages Ryan judged by eye (he is protanomalous: warm greens against yellow, cool greens against blue and purple against red are his confusions, red against green is not). Rules: no green or purple ever carries meaning; shield is the only blue (#2f63d8); enemy (#ff1e1e) and heat (#ff6a00) are both warm and a full lightness band apart; hull is bone white; good and selection are white; ink #f2f4ee over muted #7b857c; every state also carries a glyph or a shape. Chrome is one of three HUD colours (green #33ff55, amber #fff21a, cyan #5cd8ff), pushed away from the orange; red was dropped because it is the enemy colour. No coloured text sits on a map tile: numbers go on a dark plaque with a coloured border, a boss is a double outline, an elite tag a double border, and the rift is its own art with no palette role. Gauges show a pending loss dimmed and a pending gain hatched, on all three bars, shield above hull.

Decided

The gate and the walls are Arsenal-built models on the bottom row; the gate carries the first base defences.

Decided 19 September. The bottom row of every map is the gate (three hexes) with indestructible walls across the rest; nobody stands on any of it. Both are lowforge models like the units (gate and wall in tools/lowforge/models_low.py, maker arsenal, rebuilt in the locked register on 21 September; blendforge is gone) and placed on the 3D battlefield as geometry at true scale: metal, heavily armoured, weathered, slightly gothic, built to survive worse than this. Reference: Ryan's 40K fortress images, toned down. Amended 20 September: the first build read as a dinky door in a dinky wall. The gate is there to keep giant demons out and must look it: proportionately far bigger, taller than wide in feel, and its footprint may grow if the height needs it. No crenellations, which make it small (sand-castle squares); fins, blades or other texture as ornament instead. The gate has a pair of guns flanking it on its towers: the first foray into base defences, how they attack and are attacked to be decided; deployable and destructible turrets follow, placed in the hangar phase or as temporary city abilities in the battlefield phase.

Decided

The battlefield is 3D, and the rift's world bleeds into it as flat 2D.

Decided 19 September from the RIFTGATE_MAP3D experiment. The ground, the gate and walls, and the player's mechs are geometry under the map camera. Enemies are never modelled and never have facing: each foe type is a flat token sprite, Final Fantasy style, exaggerated for identification, and on the battlefield it is drawn as a flat image with no 3D cues at all (no shadow, no base, no standee edge). The 2D is not a stylisation of the enemy; it is the other world. Two effects carry that, and they stack: a bleed gradient toward the rift, where terrain height flattens, colour posterizes into the foes' register and the painted terrain marks (as a texture) take over from geometry; and the same flattening as a radius around every pack, moving with it, so where the enemy stands the third dimension itself collapses into a flat painted map. Invaded ground loses height and perspective, not just props. The rift hexes read as the source of it. When a skirmish opens, the reconstruction dives and the ground under the contact goes flat before the cut (the handover, decided 22 September, below): the fight is the seam between the worlds. Terrain marks live in a ground texture, since that texture is what the 3D fades into. Technically: two layers, the 3D viewport and the existing 2D painted map, composited by a mask (north gradient, pack radii, the rift) with a dithered, pixel-stepped edge; tall props collapse toward the mask before the 2D takes over.

Decided

The clash must jar: a gritty, elevated reconstruction against a bright flat overworld.

Ryan, 20 September, after the first compositor read as too gentle. The 3D side is gritty, muted and realistically coloured: a grainy ground texture with cracks, simple lit models, one sun casting shadows, and real elevation, every hex a column with a height by terrain (rough and ruins a step up, mountains tall, water sunk so its far bank shows). The overlays stand on the terrain (a raised hex's routes, plaques and taps lift with it). Elevation is a commitment: once height is visible players expect it to matter, so terrain costs and high ground come due. The other world is an old overworld, ironically bright and saturated, flat, with chunky marks at a coarse scale and no pretence of detail; it need not match the foes' finer sprites. The seam is hard: a dithered band a few cells wide, no soft fade, and the columns collapse just outside it. A camera shift between the two sides was considered and rejected: the compositor depends on the 3D ground projecting exactly onto the 2D grid. Tilt-shift on the 3D side only (a focus band around the middle of the screen, blur above and below, the flat world always sharp) is in as a trial for Ryan to keep or drop. The bright green of the overworld sits beside the green HUD chrome; amber or cyan chrome separates them if it fights.

Decided

Ally art is the mid-nineties 3D register, built by code from a brief.

Decided 21 September, replacing every earlier ally-art direction (the 2D spriter, the pixel and clean toon looks, the hand-polished Badger). Reference: Descent and MechWarrior 2. A unit is about 300 to 900 deliberate flat-shaded triangles as a guideline for a first pass, never a rule (Ryan, 29 September: a machine with four arms earns its facets; components under 150, structures under 1,500) wearing small tiled textures, 64 px, generated by code in its maker's metal and applied by box projection, so nothing is ever unwrapped or hand-painted. One sun, hard shadows, a thin ink line at skirmish scale. Under zoom the texels pixelate and the facets stay crisp, which is the register, not a fault. Both worlds are now honest retro: nineties 3D against sixteen-bit 2D. The metal is the first differentiator between makers: Minerva steel and military greens, matte; Kestrel chrome, silver and blues, glossy; Wainwright & Sutler iron and warm browns; Aphelion whites and reds; the Arsenal dark steel and gunmetal. The build: each unit is one readable function of numbers in tools/lowforge/models_low.py (lofts of cross-sections, tapered booms, six-sided prisms, boxes, wing plates), named by its chassis id; Ryan briefs in words, Claude turns the brief into a first pass, both iterate by render, and Ryan may edit the numbers or drop a hand-made Blender part into the same file. Reducing a detailed model never produces this look; it is built low from the start.

Decided

Each maker has a finish, and Kestrel's is chrome.

Decided 22 September. The metal is the first differentiator and its finish is the second: Kestrel is chrome and shines like it, the signature; Minerva and Wainwright & Sutler are strict matte; Aphelion's white carries a pearl sheen, gloss without metal so the white stays white; the Arsenal is black chrome, a dark mirror. In the engine a finish is roughness, metallic and specular per maker (MechView3D.FINISH), and chrome needs something to mirror, so both 3D scenes carry a sky used for reflections only: the nineties chrome map, pale blue down to a white horizon over cool dark ground, with wide soft gradients, because a flat facet under the orthographic camera takes one reflection sample and a sharp sky made neighbouring facets snap between sky and ground as a model turned. A little roughness on the chrome keeps that continuous. The glow of a lamp or engine is the reconstruction's own light and is not a finish.

Decided

The skirmish is fought on the enemy's ground: the foes stand on the other world, the mechs on the seam.

Ryan, 21 September: a skirmish opens where a pack stands, and where a pack stands the ground has already gone flat, so the fight is never on the reconstruction's terrain. The skirmish screen is the feed the reconstruction hands over to at contact, and its ground says so. Under the foes, the other world's flat bright overworld of the contact hex's terrain, at its two-frame animation. Under the mechs, the reconstruction's tile with the other world eating into it from the top edge through the map's own dithered, pixel-stepped seam: the one place the seam is close enough to read as a texture. The display's grid, the traces on the machines and the scan bar sit over both, the same display on both screens with only the ground changing. Later the seam's depth may follow how far the corruption has come. The map, before a plan is locked, shows the phase exactly as it will resolve (the resolver itself run dry, every unit put back): every contact, every pack a body block will stop, every route that will be rejected. Ryan, 21 September: there is never any ambiguity about what Resolve will do. And 22 September: the player plots in order, each route planned with the machines plotted before it already at their destinations, so a route never conflicts and Resolve never rejects one; re-plotting a machine puts it last in the order (the last thing plotted moves last), and any route the change breaks is dropped and said so. Packs plan the same way, in field order, each around where the packs before it will end, so a pack uses its whole move and only a mech can stop it short. After the movement phase plays, the contacts are offered: how many, who is in each, and the player picks which to fight first and confirms before any skirmish opens. The boss stands on one hex for now and patrols its zone outside the rift (Ryan, 22 September: a wider footprint cannot path, and the gate guard need not march); the multi-hex footprint stays in the engine for later. The movement phase plays: mechs along their routes, then packs along their intents, each cut where it really stopped, and the skirmish opens after.

Decided

Zone of control: a grounded machine controls the ground beside it; a flyer controls nothing.

Ryan, 22 September, settling how a mech stops a pack and how the preview shows it. Every hex beside a grounded mech is under its guns: a pack that steps onto one stops there, and that is the contact. A pack already standing on controlled ground holds where it is; the contact is its phase. Packs plan blind to the machines, so a plot never makes a pack swerve (a first cut that routed them around control read as a bug: the enemy dodged every contact the player set up); the shown path is the pack's own, cut at the first controlled hex. The preview plans the packs after the machines have moved, so plotting a route that cuts a pack off changes that pack's drawn path before Resolve, and each grounded machine's zone is drawn as one tinted flower round its destination: there is no hidden rule. A flyer's edge is speed and its cost is that it cannot stop anyone; it engages by ending beside where a pack will be. A body block (a pack walking into a machine's hex) can no longer happen against a grounded machine; it remains for packs against packs. Contact is loud: a CONTACT band across the map, black with red rules, beating twice over the dimmed field, then the panel with portraits of everyone in the fight (the machine bare, no outline; the foe as its battle sprite), paged when there are several, the map zoomed to the one being looked at with its hexes lit and the others dimmed. While contacts wait, the map freezes on them: nothing of the next phase (no intents, no zones, no new contacts) shows until every pending fight is done. The codex opens on the map as it does in the fight: double-tap a unit; a pack's entry is its foe type's, headed by the pack and its strength. The handover into the skirmish (decided 22 September, from three options: a cut with a flash, a zoom that flattens, a full feed switch): ENGAGE dives the reconstruction onto the contact, the other world's bleed grows out of the pack until the ground there has gone flat and bright while the machines still stand in it, the display cuts to black, and the skirmish's feed fades up. It says what the skirmish is, the feed the reconstruction hands over to, with the bleed the map already has. The ground a contact is fought on (decided 22 September): the pack's hex, since the fight opens where the pack stands and its ground has already gone flat; with several packs, the first in field order, the one the contact names first. One rule, used by the skirmish's terrain, the handover's spread and every label (the contact plaque, the panel before ENGAGE, the skirmish's header), so the ground is never ambiguous and the player reads it before committing. Ryan's caveat holds: a pack standing on good ground gets it for free, so terrain effects should be things the machines feel, not bonuses the foes get. A route may end on a hex an earlier-plotted machine is leaving, and a tap there routes rather than selects. The destination effect is one component in the HUD's own weight: the tinted hex and its thin outline drawn under the line, three bright broken rings turning against each other around a dot (the display's target lock) well inside the outline, and the intent line arriving at the outer ring; nothing translucent, nothing abutting; the rings' colour meets the ground (bright on dark, deep on pale) with a slow breath between (Ryan, 22 September); the contact effect (a heavier pulsing tint with rings bursting out of it) never shares a hex with it; contact wins; a machine's own destination marker sits on two faint breathing rings in the display's light; the other world's dither never covers a machine (the machines are drawn again over both worlds, from their own layer); the enemy's marks sit on the ground's own height like every other mark (a trial at the hex's base, 22 September, was worse and was reverted the same day); the enemy's line, outline and rings breathe together between red and a bright salmon; the tints are heavy on purpose, the zone breathing, because the UX matters more than the ground under a mark; every hex tint and the zone's outline lie on the ground under the machines, never over them, hidden by whatever stands on them as the grid is; a machine keeps its outline where the wall hides part of it, and the gate and walls wear no outline; the gate is selectable, with its integrity as a true bar on the wall's face and a codex entry of its own; the rift carries a bar of its own above it, in the scenery rows where the boss standing in front never covers it, counting down to the next spawn; the top bar says only what is not on the map, in plain words (the round, and how many rounds until the rift spawns); the HUD's own marks (the frame, the bars, their text) stay crisp and thin under zoom, drawn in screen space, because a HUD is drawn on the glass, not on the ground. The map tuner is a development tool now (Ryan, 22 September: happy with the look); the player's knobs live in a pause menu on both screens (MENU: resume, settings, and from 23 September quit, which asks first and leaves for the harness with nothing kept; settings: the display colour, brightness, move pace, enemy pace, and glitch and scan bar toggles, then the music and effects knobs; saturation and bloom were cut the same day as knobs no player would want), saved between sessions; a colour change rebuilds the screen behind a mask with the menu still up (Ryan, 23 September: it had closed the menu, and the rebuild is not instant); every gauge on the skirmish sits on a dark ground of its own chamfered shape rather than a plate under the stack, and stands a little off its drawing (the same day); the scan bar off means the lights off, every trace resting as ink; red outlines alone never read on this ground, so every one of these tints its hex and moves. The movement phase plays at a pace by movespeed, the quickest unit at the fastest pace the display will ever show. The pulse trace is the default look: everything rests as ink and the scan bar lights it as it passes, with a long tail; the skirmish's machines carry the same silhouette; only the codex is ever pure black ink with no highlight.

Decided

Engagement range: a contact seeds where units touch, and every grounded machine within two hexes of it joins; tethers show who is in.

Built 28 September. A fight still seeds only where a machine and a pack are adjacent, so the zone-of-control contract holds: a pack stops beside a grounded machine, and a machine two hexes from a lone pack is no fight. Once a contact exists, every grounded machine within Battlefield.ENGAGE_RANGE (2) of any member joins, the nearest group first, and the widening repeats until nothing more joins, so a machine two hexes behind a joined one is in through it. A machine is never in two fights; at a tie it takes the first in field order. Static defences still join by touching alone. A stealth machine controls no ground and is never pulled in by range; its join rule (beside an ally, or when it stopped a pack in its path) waits on the blocked list and lands with the rest of the trait. On the map, every machine in a previewed contact, and in one waiting to be fought, carries a tether: a dashed line to the nearest pack it will fight, breathing with the contact's rings, its dashes crawling toward the fight, dimmed when another contact is the one being looked at. Ryan's read in play decides whether two is the number and whether formations are still wanted.

Recommended

Engagement by the pack's capture zone: a pack fights everything within two hexes of where it stops.

Proposed 29 September, for Ryan to decide in a session of his own. The history: the 22 September rule (the fight is the units touching) could not get a squad of four beside a moving pack with move 2 or 3; the 28 September engagement range fixed the placement but measures the range from the group, so joins repeat machine to machine and chain across the map, and nothing shows until the dry run finds a contact. Explaining it to a new player was a nightmare. The proposal turns the rule round: keep the zone of control as the stop (a pack that steps beside a grounded machine stops there), and give the pack a capture zone, radius two, around where it ends; every grounded machine inside it fights. Measured from the pack, nothing grows: a machine inside two packs' zones merges those two fights, which is bounded and visible. The zones can be drawn all the time, red flowers at each pack's predicted end, lit when a machine stands in one, so who joins is seen while planning; the tethers become redundant. One sentence for a new player: "your machines stop a pack that steps beside them, and every machine within two hexes of it joins the fight." Ryan's variants, weighed: his variant 1 (entering the pack's zone starts the fight) is this if the stop stays the zone of control, and a new stop rule if not; variant 2 (an engagement zone and a larger capture zone) is exactly this with engagement equal to adjacency; variant 3 (both sides carry zones, overlap fights) is really a range of four and busier to read, and what it is after, ranged machines joining from farther, fits inside this rule later as a machine stat, reach, default two, drawn as the machine's own ring only when selected. Two holes: radius two from where the pack stops means a column of four still does not all fight (the pack stops at the front machine, the rear ones are three away), so the decision becomes spread against depth, the placement puzzle milder; and the radius is the one knob (Battlefield.ENGAGE_RANGE can carry it). A flyer joins only by ending next to the pack, as now.

Decided

The battlefield screen is CARE's tactical reconstruction of the field.

Decided 20 September. Ryan's framing was a war table in-world: an approximated satellite view with the HUD, grid, mechs and labels projected over it, the only explanation for seeing the corrupted 2D landscape at all; he took Claude's refinement as the same idea with more to hang on it. Sensor language follows and is wanted for its strategic depth: a pack not yet seen is a contact, the edge tags are sensor returns, the intent lines are projections; the reconstruction's quality is a possible research path later. Scale is a feel question, not a plausibility one: the mechs need not fit through the gate, but the gate must look like it keeps behemoths out.

Decided

The city is under a field, and the gate is its projector.

The field is cast from the gate outward and over the city, and the projector stands in its own shadow: the mouth is the one place the field cannot cover, which is why every pack heads for it and why the gate falling loses the campaign (the projector goes, the field goes, the city is open). Decided

Front and back ranks, enemy side only.

Simple two-rank structure. It adds a second axis to target choice: a front rank of bodies and a back rank of problems.

A pool with ranks, in columns.

Range archetypes

Decided

No damage falloff at range.

Futuristic weapons hitting for less at range is counterintuitive. Range is expressed as archetypes with soft penalties, never smaller numbers.

Decided

Three reaches plus keywords.

Decided 18 September with Ryan's vocabulary audit. Melee hits the front rank, and the back rank only when the whole front is empty, so a melee build can be stranded by a held back rank and that is its cost. Line of sight hits the front rank freely and a back enemy only where its column is exposed. Indirect hits any foe in either rank; on the front rank it pays splash, half the damage off your own shield. Keywords ride on top: spread for neighbours, penetrate for the column. A passive may change a component's reach while a condition holds (the Badger's cracked weapons go melee). Melee is authored on a component too since 26 September (the Badger's claws); until then it only ever came from a passive. No delayed shells: delay adds cross-round state for no gain.

Shields, heat, injuries

Recommended

Shields are a timer.

Shield faces add shield. The shield buffer gives a few rounds to solve the pack before hull is at risk. Enemy damage should threaten to break a shield within two or three rounds against a typical pack, so shield faces buy time rather than immunity.

Recommended

Heat is the second resource.

Strong faces add heat. A vent face clears it. Running hot risks internal damage. Passives and support faces (vent an ally's heat) make heat a team problem, not a solo one.

Decided

Every chassis cools passively at the end of the round.

Ryan, 22 September. A chassis carries a cooling rating (content; 0 when absent) and sheds that much heat at the start of every turn, before the dice roll. Venting stays a tactic; a round spent on ballistics or shields is now also a way of waiting to cool down. Cool at the start, heat at the end (Ryan, same day): the Overheating status ticks at the end of the round, after the enemy has struck, so a scorch lands on the round's peak heat before the next turn's cooling relieves it; the first cut had it the other way round and cooling softened every scorch. Every overheat burns at the end of the round (Ryan, 22 September, third pass): heat climbs past the maximum during the round with no spike when a component is spent, the Overheating tick lands on top, and whatever stands over the cap then burns hull one for one as the gauge settles at the cap. So a machine run hot can still be vented before it pays, and the space is there for other ways to intervene. The old "heat max" chip is now the burn warning: the hull the round's end will take unless something changes. The forecast's heat counts the stacks carried plus one per scorching blow coming; it counts the die's own heat only while that die is being dragged, since shown unasked it read as something done to the mech rather than by the player. Cooling is a stat like the others (a passive may raise it). Tuning, not design: cooling must stay well under a typical weapon's heat, and a vent component must be worth a whole turn, so the heat sink wants a bigger number. Numbers are placeholders.

Decided

Injuries are one threshold and one roll of the die, on bare hull only.

When a blow lands on bare hull (no shield standing) and what gets past armour is at least a quarter of maximum hull, the mech's die is rolled and the component it lands on is injured. An intact component cracks: it loses its keywords, crit chance and unique effect but still does its basic action. A cracked component hit again is destroyed and goes blank. A destroyed component or an empty hardpoint takes the blow and nothing happens, so a badly broken mech shrugs off more of them. A passive may hold a cracked component short of destruction (the Badger's). The counterplay is shielding before a big telegraphed hit. The one-in-two coin on top of the threshold was cut on 17 September as randomness with no decision behind it; per-faction signature injuries were dropped earlier as an unnecessary layer. The threshold is a placeholder.

Decided

A damaged face is a damaged component, and it stays damaged.

Cracked and destroyed faces persist across skirmishes and battles until repaired in the hangar. This is the component damage system: there is no separate one. It makes a lost skirmish cost something into the next battle and gives the meta layer's repair and salvage real work to do.

Pinned

Low-hull berserker or last-stand ability.

A mech at critical hull unlocking an overdrive face is Doom energy and an anti-spiral. Parked, not rejected.

Recommended

Terrain is a skirmish-wide condition.

The hexes involved set a modifier for the whole fight, so choosing where to engage still matters without positioning inside the skirmish. Water dampens burn, ruins give the pack cover, and so on.

Die faces and iconography

Decided

Primary icon, a number, small secondary icons, one catch-all star.

The primary icon is the face's type: the reach archetype for attacks, a shield for shield faces, and so on. The number is its power. Small superscript or subscript icons mark common attached effects. One catch-all icon marks a rare, unique effect (legendary faces). Long-press a face to inspect secondary numbers, unique effects, and temporary boosts. An icon cheat sheet is available from the pause menu.

Recommended

Every entry on the die is a component.

There is no generic equipment and nothing fills a slot for you: an entry is a component, or an empty hardpoint that does nothing when it comes up, or a destroyed component. A pristine, fully fitted war machine is reliable; a light or injured one is not. Decided 18 September, replacing "every face does something".

Recommended

A keyword vocabulary of about ten.

Variety comes from combining number, reach, keywords, and heat cost with passives, not from more rules. Slice and Dice gets hundreds of distinct faces from a vocabulary this size.

Decided

Keywords are a list, not a single slot.

A face may carry several keywords, so faces can be upgraded during a battle or in the meta. The content validator caps authored faces at three keywords; upgrades may push past that at runtime. Iconography stays readable because the primary icon and the number are fixed, and extra keywords show as small secondary icons.

Decided

Minimal chrome in the skirmish.

No cards, no boxes. The log stays in the centre for now because it is needed for testing and the space is free; it can hide behind a button later. Health and status are small floating slider bars beside the sprite. Mechs face north-east and enemies south-west; mechs get a per-class idle, enemies only attack animations. A massive elite may occupy both rank slots of a column. Tap, double-tap, or hold shows full numbers and details. Passives are simple enough not to be displayed at all times. Enemy health stays visible (the JRPG-style hidden-health option was dropped with the Griftlands damage model).

Decided

The vector HUD is the UI.

Adopted 14 September from the layout canvas. Everything that is not a sprite is a flat vector overlay: slanted gauges, rounded-square dice with corner brackets when locked, bracketed [ATK 10] readouts, a terminal-style log. The HUD colour (green by default, amber, red, blue) is a player setting saved to the user settings file. Decided

Decided

Three rerolls, and a reroll always shows a different face.

Decided 15 September. Two rerolls did not feel like enough even before consumables and commander powers; the base is three. A reroll never lands on the face showing nor on one equivalent to it, as a known global rule: these are not really dice, and a reroll that changes nothing reads as a bug rather than as bad luck. A die whose faces are all alike keeps its face. The count shows on the Reroll button only, not in the header. This is a rule the player must be told: the Reroll button's tooltip and the die codex say it now, and the gesture tutorial should too. Knock-on to watch: on a four-face die three never-repeating rerolls almost always find the wanted face, so d4 may be too small a size to exist (see open questions).

Decided

Intents show the action, not the target.

Each foe shows what it will do and for how much. Holding a foe or a mech draws the arrows for that unit; an overlay button draws every arrow. This is Slice and Dice's model.

Decided

Few machines against a horde is the fantasy, and the hooks come from it.

Decided 15 September. With every foe on the field striking each round, incoming damage on the lowest shield is the pressure the whole skirmish is built around, and the answer is design, not a cap: area damage that earns its keep against a full formation, taunt and mitigation that shape who gets hit, and enemy behaviour that is not always optimal. The horde is an invasion, not an army: most rank and file may target badly or sometimes not attack at all, while the higher tiers of the demonic and eldritch factions execute plans that look thought through. A direction to build: packs that move with or natively include a commander unit; kill it and the pack's targeting scrambles, even mid-turn. Formation width was a setting (Skirmish.columns, three by default, four for the horde feel) until 23 September, when four was locked in from play (section 5); the parameter stays for tests. The current foes and pack compositions are proof-of-concept content and will be replaced.

Decided

A foe type is not limited to one action.

Each foe in content/foes.json carries an actions roster: attack, defend, buff, or debuff, each with a player-facing line and a status. Sometimes a foe attacks, sometimes it shields, sometimes it buffs the horde or weakens a mech. The engine still runs only the basic attack, so the validator insists on exactly one implemented attack per foe and refuses anything else marked implemented; planned actions live in the file and stay out of the codex until coded.

Decided

Tap a die to lock it, hold it for details.

No separate Keep button. A locked die is highlighted and skipped by reroll.

Decided

Double-tap a unit for its codex entry.

Hold is the quick read: numbers and arrows while the finger is down. Double-tap opens a box that stays: the codex text, stats, actions, faces shown visually in a column with the current face highlighted, the passive with its rules, and last the flavour line as an italic quote. The head-on sprite sits top-right of the box across from the name. Every foe has its own flavour line, not the faction's. The design question a unit poses is never shown; fantasy and question are internal design fields, and any hint the player needs goes into the codex text. A tap outside closes it.

Decided

Numbers in the magnitude of tens.

Hull, shield, damage, and heat live in tens, not ones, so upgrades can be incremental and big numbers feel big; a 15 or a 33 is fine, and the validator only refuses negatives (clarified 18 September). Burn is one stack per twenty damage dealt at five per stack, splash is half the damage rolled, heat overflows one for one.

Mechs and the builder

Mechwarrior's build depth, simplified and dialled back. The feel to keep is that a loadout is a real identity. The habits to drop are strict weight classes and gated equipment.

Decided

Three to four mechs per battlefield.

Enough for a real decision between splitting up and staying together for skirmish strength, without tedious planning. Recommended: design for four, start a campaign with three, unlock the fourth slot early.

Decided

Chassis are not classes and not only bipeds.

Tanks, hovercraft, quadrupeds, and flyers are all candidates. No light, medium, heavy labels. Chassis diversity comes from base stats: hull, shield, movespeed, hardpoints, and similar simple numbers that do not need prominent display.

Decided

The die is the equipment: fixed components plus hardpoints, one entry each.

Decided 18 September. A chassis has fixed components (built in, never removed or sold, no hardpoint used: the Badger's claw and plating) and hardpoints (free slots, any component in any one). Die size is fixed count plus hardpoints; the Badger rolls a d6 from two fixed and four hardpoints, and its model carries four pins. Every component is exactly one entry: multi-slot components were considered and scrapped the same day as invisible complexity (where would it render, and would anyone pay for the fragility). The player never sees a polyhedron, so shown text says component, never face; Face stays the engine's word for one entry.

Earlier, recommended: hardpoints set die size.

Each component contributes one face; the chassis fills any remainder. A chassis with four hardpoints rolls a d4 and is consistent and focused. One with eight rolls a d8 and carries more but sees each face less. That is the deckbuilder's thin-deck tension as a chassis stat. First pass: d4, d6, d8. The builder shows the odds of each face.

Decided

Zero to one chassis passive, and it may be a skirmish passive.

Two flavours. A double-edged niche definer: a skirmisher aircraft with big shield, high movespeed, low hull, few faces, and "double damage, double heat," positioned by its manufacturer as a hit-and-run assassin, that becomes a carry beside an ally who vents its heat. Or a simple multiplier fantasy: "doubles all burn," where the player stacks burn and burns everything like a maniac.

A passive is one idea. Two complementary rules, such as "when I gain shield, one random ally gains the same amount" and "my shield does not recharge outside combat," are one passive with an upside and a cost, not two. The test is whether it can be named in a phrase and understood as one thing. Cap: three rule lines.

Recommended

A passive multiplies or redirects what faces already do.

It never introduces a verb of its own. If it needs a new mechanic to explain, it is a component, not a chassis.

Decided

Components are free.

Untyped hardpoints, any component in any slot. A niche you can leave is an invitation; the player steps outside it for experimentation or because resources force them to make it work.

Recommended

Silhouettes become terrain traits.

Hovercraft cross water, quadrupeds ignore rough ground, flyers pass over mountains but cannot body-block, tanks move faster on roads. Team composition becomes a decision about the map as well as the fight.

Decided

The passive and the fixed components are the design; the stock loadout is the maker's generic kit.

Ryan, 26 September, on the Mandrill's first fill. A chassis's flavour and its design live in its passive and its fixed (locked) components, which the player cannot swap; everything in the free hardpoints is stock that the player will replace, so it is never factored into the design of the unit. The stock kit will most likely be generic and shared between a maker's machines, with unique components unlocked through play (drops and blueprints, section 10). Two consequences: a passive must work with generic kit, never depend on one unique component (a mech must not feel terrible to run because the right blueprint has not dropped); and every mech wants enough depth that there is no straight-line optimisation and no single build path. For the Mandrill this means its identity has to come from its passive and from something fixed; its shoulder cannons or its backpack pods are the candidates for fixed slots, and the rest of its filled kit is in flux. Commander and city

The mechs are the team; the player is the commander. Commander tools do what mechs cannot: manipulation, mitigation, information, and movement. They are never a fifth gun.

In the skirmish

Recommended

One command die, rolled with the mech dice.

Its faces are rechargeable abilities, so recharge is the roll and nobody hoards a die result. Examples: smoke that blanks the back rank's intents this round, a drone screen that absorbs the next hit, focus fire that adds one to every face on a single target, a scan that reveals the pack's next two rounds. Die size (d4, d6, d8) is a research path with stronger faces and a backfire face or two on the larger dice.

Decided

Reusable commander abilities never deal direct damage.

One-shots may. Damage one-shots belong at the battlefield layer (soften a pack before contact) so mech dice stay the killing power inside a skirmish.

Decided

Command faces come from the hangar, never from skirmish rewards.

Thematically wrong as loot, and they would dominate the reward pick because they affect every skirmish equally.

Open

Is the command die needed at all?

The alternative is one-shots only, with meta upgrades to inventory size and effectiveness, and reroll bundles among the rewards. Claude's view: keep the die, because a rolled face is inherently non-hoardable and the one-shots-only model pushes everything into the inventory the designer already knows they would hoard.

One-shots and the hoarding problem

Decided

Spending a consumable must not feel bad.

The designer is in the camp that hoards potions for a rainy day that never comes. If one-shots are core, the design has to make spending the default.

Recommended

Three fixes.

One-shots picked up in a battle expire when the rift closes, so there is nothing to save them for beyond this battle. The pouch holds three, so a fourth pickup forces use-or-discard on the spot. A starting stock chosen in the hangar restocks every battle; research raises capacity and quality. Doom Eternal solved the same problem the same way: resources regenerate on a short loop, so spending is the default and saving is the anomaly. Saving a full pouch for the boss remains a legitimate blitz strategy with a real cost in missed upgrades.

Skirmish rewards

Recommended

Pick one of three.

A modifier on a mech face you already carry (lasts the battle), a one-shot for the pouch, or a battle-wide boon such as an extra reroll per round. Temporary upgrades amplify the build you brought and never replace it, so the pull is toward synergy.

Decided

Reward types are faction-weighted, never guaranteed.

Undead might give a greater chance of multiple or rarer one-shots, for example. Flavour to be determined per faction.

On the battlefield: city abilities

Decided

Battlefield-level abilities belong to the city.

Sprint, airlift, teleport, tractor beam, orbital strike, and any number of silly ideas. They are upgraded and managed as city systems, and no command die is rolled on the battlefield.

Decided

Cooldowns in battlefield phases are acceptable here.

Each movement phase is a turn, so a cooldown is legible and cannot be hoarded. City research shortens cooldowns, adds charges, and unlocks abilities. Cooldowns still do not exist inside the skirmish, where the die is the recharge.

Recommended

Some city abilities are limited by distance from the gate.

Artillery reaches the southern half, airlift only lifts from the gate, the tractor beam has a range. The push north leaves city support behind, which is a real cost to blitzing and comes out of flavour rather than a rule.

Each faction has a behavioral signature that demands a different tactical answer, with internal diversity and general paradigms to guide decisions rather than strict rules. Elements are flavour and the reward currency, never a damage-type triangle. Decided

Every foe's second action runs: a defend, buff or debuff it does instead of a blow, picked by weight and announced like one.

Built 30 September from the ten actions written on 14 September, each an effect the engine runs rather than a rule keyed to a foe's id (Foe.ACTION_EFFECTS), so Ryan can move them between foes or write new ones: defend folds against damage (half taken until the foe's next action, the Spawn) or regrows hit points (the Colossus); buff readies a foe to get back up (it returns to the pool once, whole, after dying, unless an executing blow touched it in the fight: the Ghoul), calls a foe of a named type up into the pool for the round's end (the Necromancer's ghoul, made by the battle's content), or rallies every other foe on the field to hit harder next round (the Pit Lord); debuff costs the team rerolls next round (the Brute), stokes a machine's heat (the Hellcaster), scrambles a machine's next reroll so it lands anywhere, the same entry included (the Whisperer; a cleanse clears it), or cracks the component a machine's die is showing with no hull hit (the Maw). A foe doing one of these lands no blow that turn: the forecast, the intent chip (its kind's glyph), the hold and the bot all read it that way, and a stoke's heat shows in the forecast. The statuses these leave (Folded, Kindled, Getting Back Up, Scrambled) are chips on the unit. Weights are placeholders: every attack now carries weight 3 against its special's 1, so a foe does the special about one turn in four; Ryan moves them per foe. "Foe roles" in the to-do had no definition anywhere in content or here, so nothing was built for it: a rank is the only role a foe has today, and a role system is Ryan's to propose. The runner's reading: the whole matrix eased, since a foe spending a quarter of its turns on its special lands a quarter fewer blows: the Brute x4 wall fell for most machines (the Clydesdale from 0 to 39 under greedy, the Nemesis from 7 to 56), the Pit Lord cells rose everywhere (its Kindle turn is a turn without the furnace blow), and ghoul fights run twice as long because the ghouls get back up (the Clydesdale's from seven rounds to fourteen, with random play winning it less and greedy still winning). None of that is the specials being weak or strong; it is the weight of a special against a blow, which is Ryan's to set per foe, and the first thing to move if the fights read too soft.

Meta and resources

Decided

Two kinds of gain.

Persistent resources, spent between battles. Temporary upgrades, lasting the current battle only. Skirmish victory yields both.

Decided

Wrecks are recoverable.

Defeated mechs become scrap, salvaged, repaired, or broken down into components between battles. Losses hurt but stay recoverable, which is what prevents a campaign from being lost three battles before it ends.

Decided

Research trees.

Chassis and components, city defenses and city abilities, commander dice and one-shot capacity. Manufacturers give the chassis tree its structure.

Technical plan

Recommended

Godot 4 with GDScript.

Chosen on the project's merits, independent of any other codebase. A 2D-first renderer with pixel-art settings built in, hex tile layers, a strong UI toolkit for a game that is mostly panels and dice, Android export out of the box, no build step, and a permissive license with no runtime fee. Unity wins only if a deep ads and in-app-purchase SDK ecosystem is a day-one priority.

Recommended

Keep the simulation pure.

Hex math, movement, skirmish resolution, and map generation are plain scripts with no scene dependencies, driven by a seeded random generator. That makes them testable headless and gives reproducible runs for replays and daily seeds.

Decided

Balance is measured by a ladder of bots, and read as bands, not targets.

Ryan, 25 September: one person can play-test feel and micro balance, never the scale macro balance needs. So the pure simulation gets a player: sim/policy.gd drives a skirmish headless on a ladder of skill. Random never rerolls and spends every die on any legal target: the floor, and a pack it beats is too easy, no argument. Greedy reads the same forecast the screen shows the player and takes the best one-round trade, rerolling dice that come up under their die's average, holding a die whose every spend would cost more hull than it earns; it cannot see anything the player cannot. Personas are habits on top (withdraw under a hull line). A rollout rung (replay a few rounds ahead from the seed) waits until greedy stops being enough. tools/balance.gd plays every chassis alone and the starting team against every pack a faction sends, hundreds of seeds a cell, into docs/balance.md: win rate per rung, rounds and hull spent, and pick share per component, the signal for equipment (a component nobody spends is dead; one spent every time is the whole die). A cell where the rungs land together holds no decision, which is the Slice and Dice smell caught by machine. The runner says which matchups are off by a lot; feel decides the rest, and no simulated win rate is chased to a decimal. Battle and meta simulation stack on this in order, each level's bot using the one below as its auto-resolve, and neither is built before its loop exists.

Recommended

Save at every phase boundary.

Android can kill a backgrounded app at any moment. If the whole state serializes at the end of each battlefield phase and each skirmish round, resuming is free rather than a retrofit.

Recommended

Cube coordinates for hexes.

Pointy-top rendering with cube or axial coordinates in the logic, so distance, neighbours, and lines are trivial and orientation can change later.

Recommended

Prototype the skirmish first.

It is the riskiest unknown and it defines what a component is, which defines the builder and the meta. It can be played with physical dice and index cards before any code exists, which is also the fastest way to catch overcomplication.

Getting the game onto a phone: two paths, and what each can tell you.

Decided 23 September, from the first phone session. The quick look is a Web build served over the LAN: tools/phone.sh exports and serves it, prints an https:// address, and the phone opens it in its browser on the same Wi-Fi (accept the certificate warning once; the first button press goes full screen). It shows sizes and touch at the phone's own pixels and nothing else: the frame rate is the browser's, the load is slow, pinch and hex outlines may misbehave. Ryan's verdict: good for layout, dismiss the rest. The real thing is a debug APK over USB: once, on the phone, tap Build number seven times under About, then turn on USB debugging under Developer options, plug in and accept the computer's key; then tools/android.sh exports, installs and launches it, and tools/android.sh log tails the game's output from the phone. tools/android.sh install reinstalls the last export without exporting. The phone is the reference glass: what the build does differently on a device, from the first native days.

The map's second phone pass: what a tap shows, what the board says, and the log off the screen.

Ryan, 29 September, from play on the phone. The board says what a player wants at a glance and nothing else: every machine's shield and hull as gauges, its order as the display's hex (column.row, for flavour; the map is where you look), what it will meet in the enemy's red, the enemy on the field as packs and foes, and whether the plan makes contact at all. Decided

The phone's third pass: the fine map, no anti-aliasing on Android, text sampled linear, and every colour checked by simulation because Ryan is colourblind.

Ryan, 29 September, from a day on the device. The fine map: the Compatibility renderer has no anti-aliasing to offer (MSAA leaks, measured: about 170 MB every twenty seconds until the driver aborts at 1.8 GB; screen-space AA is refused outright), so the machines' edges come from resolution: the map's scene and compositor, and every machine's view, render at the glass's density rather than the design's and the window's stretch lands one to one. Decided

A battle is saved after every change and resumes where it stood, straight into the fight if one was on.

Built 30 September, first of the "before a phone leaves the desk" items: a phone puts an app down without asking. sim/save.gd writes the whole battle as plain data and reads it back: the map by its seed (regenerated, never stored), every unit with its numbers, base stats, die entries with their damage state and component, the damaged originals the hangar repairs from, every live modifier with its scope, stacks and grant order, the routes being plotted, the contacts waiting, the boss, the field's gate, pressure, spawn table and log, and the skirmish under way with its dice, heat, rerolls, every foe on the field or waiting by rank and column, each foe's target and chosen action, and both random streams, so the resumed fight plays on blow for blow as the original would have (the tests play both copies with the same bot and compare the logs). The file is JSON under user://battle.json; a random stream's state is a 64-bit integer JSON cannot hold, so it travels as a string, and a file from another version loads nothing rather than something wrong. The battle screen saves after every phase step and after every change in the skirmish (the skirmish screen's refresh calls back), and clears the save when the battle is decided; the harness shows Resume battle while a save exists and opens it where it was, with no handover for a fight already on (a resume is not a contact). Saving is on only for a battle the harness starts or resumes: standalone scene runs and the tests never write it, so a screenshot pose can never overwrite Ryan's real battle. The pause menu's QUIT now says a battle is kept where it stands. Not saved: a phase's move animation, the replay of an enemy turn, the boss reveal; a resume shows the state after them.

Content pipeline

Concepts are authored fantasy-first in docs/concepts/ using the template there, in batches by category.
