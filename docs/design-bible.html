Decided

How to read a row.

A Decided row is one of three things: a design test (what the player feels, section 2), a rule the engine runs, or a content call for a named piece. A maker's or a side's theme, word or keyword is a Lean, never a Decided rule. A "never" carries its reason and the condition that would reopen it; a "never" without either records the current state, not a law. A row that retires a mechanism names the form that failed and the test it failed, so a new form that passes the test is free to be proposed. Every row is open to debate (section 2) and a passive may break any rule as a one-off (section 7): a proposal argues for the change in the open rather than designing around the row, and its bench shows the ideas a row killed, so Ryan can overrule the row and not only the idea. A number a row quotes from a quick run is a direction, not a finding (section 11); the runs live in docs/measurements.md. Decided 13 October, after the review of that day.

1

Concept and tone

This document records what is decided and why. The rules as they stand are in docs/rules.md, how things stand today in docs/status.md, and what comes next in docs/todo.md. Each row says what holds now; history lives in git.

The game

Decided

Three nested loops, and the depth lives in the skirmish.

The campaign is the roguelike run. A battle is a hex field, the gate at the bottom and the rift at the top, won by killing the boss that powers the rift. A skirmish is the dice fight opened on one or more packs at a time.

Decided

A few machines against a horde.

The player fields a few machines, none of them disposable, through many battles, with no unit micromanagement. An enemy hex is a pack, so a skirmish is a few machines against many foes.

Decided

Learnable in play.

Tooltips and an in-game cheat sheet, never a wiki, a build planner or damage-per-second maths.

Open

The game's name.

"Riftgate" is a placeholder.

Tone

Decided

Doom and Space Marine: over the top, played straight.

Propaganda is in; manufacturer adverts and Borderlands-style gags are out, because this is the end of civilisation. Never edgy, and a city falling is never funny. Grimdark is how this game plays it straight: the world is grim and nobody in it winks, though a player may take it as dark humour.

Decided

Grounded military mecha.

The machines are in the MechWarrior and StarCraft Terran line, never Evangelion's demon-mecha: rugged, brutal, heavy, serviced rather than cobbled together.

Decided

The Arsenal is twisted by the occult, never merged with it.

Each of its machines is a battlemech with a sealed occult power source where a fusion reactor would be, drawn by an engineer who has had things whispering in his ear and twisting his thoughts. Mechanical only: no flesh and no gore. One may borrow another line's frame, twisted (a corrupted Aile Strike, an Evangelion frame), and it still moves with the game's weight (section 7); none has a mind of its own. CARE pilots fly them, and CARE would never let anything else into its hangars.

Decided

The makers are provenance, a livery and a voice, never advertising.

In-your-face advertising turns the tone to black comedy. A maker calling the commander its best customer is fine.

The world

Decided

The Council rules the cities.

The Council for the Ultimate Liberation of Terra; its acronym is never written or spoken. It is a martial-law regime pretending otherwise, believing in its cause while profiting from it: a god-emperor's court closer to Pacific Rim than to BattleTech. It rewards innovation that helps the fight, so the makers compete for its favour, and it keeps the best hardware for its own military.

Decided

The player is CARE's Battlefield Commander.

CARE, the Civil Auxiliary for Rift Emergencies: the Council chose the name in condescension. The commander is a general, never a politician. CARE's people are the true believers, pragmatic and rebellious, played straight; the Council takes the credit and none of the responsibility.

Decided

The Arsenal is the Council's own maker.

It sells CARE a trickle and never its best. It is the one research path the player cannot force: its tiers open only as the campaign turns dire.

Decided

What the Council names a machine and what it manifests as can differ.

Ryan, 11 October: the Council, and by extension the Arsenal, are self-righteous seekers of knowledge and power who think of themselves as the saviours of humanity, so the names they give their machines and what those machines turn out to be can be incongruent.

Decided

The occult is the Council's, and the Arsenal says it in liturgy.

The domains moved the invasion to the genre's high fantasy, so among the makers the demonic and the eldritch are the Council's and its Arsenal's: the one place the player meets the dark on our side. The invasion meets its own dark at depth (section 9: the Convergence's last tier wears the final dungeon's cosmic register), which is the enemy's and never a maker's. The Arsenal is demonic in look and effect and liturgical in what the player reads: its manual never says demon; it says devotion, penance, judgement. That is the incongruence above made a rule: names in the register of justice and vengeance (section 7), machines that manifest as Hell, and a player who looks a name up finds both. Machines that named their demons would make the Council's nature the premise instead of a reveal, and that stays open (below).

Decided

Lore is Ryan's.

design.fantasy in content holds his homages and is never shown.

Open

The lore holes.

Is CARE an Auxiliary or an Agency? What does the Council do when the player fails, can CARE's contract be lost, and how does the Council's credit-taking become plot?

Pinned

Room to expand, left open on purpose.

Whether this is mankind's last bastion, whether other colonies look away, whether the Council had a hand in the rifts (its rise may become the campaign's spine).

The invasion's register

Decided

The JRPG genre invades, played dead straight.

Final Fantasy is the spine; homage spans the genre (the Gel is Dragon Quest's slime, the Needler the Cactuar made sinister). Every foe is drawn in one style of our own, never in a series' design language. Homage is by archetype (Ryan, 15 October): a series' own coinages take names of ours, as the Cactuar became the Needler, a homage's look is never the series' (the Exor homage need not even be a sword), and a mechanic its publisher holds a patent on stays out (Nintendo and The Pokémon Company sued over Palworld on patents, 2024, not on looks). Detailed high fantasy against gritty realism is the game's dichotomy, and it replaces most of the verbal irony (section 9).

Decided

Our side is 3D geometry; theirs is painted 2D.

The genre mash is literal: everything human is geometry, the foes are flat sixteen-bit sprites. The map is the overworld and the skirmish is the battle screen. On the map a machine is its skirmish model and a pack is a flat token; in the skirmish everything is full size.

Art direction

Decided

Ally art is mid-nineties 3D, built by code.

tools/lowforge builds each machine from a written brief: a few hundred flat-shaded facets wearing small generated tiles in its maker's metal, no hand modelling, no unwrapping, and no image model touches a machine (section 7 has the rules for models and motion).

Decided

Each maker has its metal.

Minerva matte steel and military green; Kestrel chrome, silver and blue; Wainwright & Sutler matte iron and warm brown; Aphelion pearl white and red; the Arsenal black chrome and red glass.

Decided

Foes are painterly sixteen-bit battle sprites in ornate high fantasy.

The anchor is the SNES Final Fantasy IV to VI enemies: realistic proportions with a small, low-set head, a frozen mid-menace pose, eyes without pupils, a weapon only where the brief names one. A brief names the genre's tradition, never a series' design, and a domain's look is a mood, never a palette. Heroes are drawn in the elaborate boss register, and the party is as varied as people are: genders, skin tones, ages, builds and postures differ across the eight (Ryan's pass, 13 October, on the picker); their costumes are to differ too (Ryan, 19 October: the mages all wear the same thing, because the hero style prompt asks every hero for robes with gold trim and flowing cloth; a later pass gives each hero a prompt naming what it wears, as the Augur's does: the to-do). Each caster's power is its own shape and colour, never shared: the Cleric's crystal, the Augur's dark omen-smoke, a green so deep it is nearly black with pale edges (Ryan, 19 October: dark, not light; the Necromancer's ghost-fire held the green until it was dumped, 16 October), the Black Mage's flame, the Summoner's deep violet rune circles (Ryan, 13 October: the Golem belongs to the lava field, not to the Summoner, so it shows no fire and no rock; deep violet because mid violet is the map's rift-intent field under a deutan simulation).

Decided

Foe art comes from an image model on the development Mac, picked by Ryan.

tools/foeforge runs FLUX.2 Klein through mflux: four candidates per foe from its one-line brief (design.sprite), its codex and its design takes (battle poses for variety); Ryan picks on a page, at the desk or on the phone. A flaw his notes name is tried by the model before any hand, and the route depends on the flaw (Ryan, 18 October: image to image is the better outcome most of the time): a flaw of structure, a limb, a hand, a grip, a weapon, goes first through image to image from the picked render at strength 0.6, which kept the Treant's, the Zombie Knight's and the Reaper's compositions and made the change where the edit mode had failed twice each time (the Reaper's edits took the blade off with the extra hand, then left the hand); a flaw of colour alone, a moss, a glow, a drip, goes through the edit mode on the picked source, which leaves the drawing untouched; a change of costume is a fresh render from a rewritten take, since image to image keeps the costume with the composition (the Augur's hooded coat survived every seed). Image to image drifts, redrawing the whole figure a little each pass (the Treant's twig claws became hands over two), so it chains from the pick Ryan liked, never from the last pass. The model's licence is checked before shipping.

Decided

Foes are cut small and sharp, and readability is the cut's job.

72x96 (96x144 when massive, 36x48 for a hero's chibi), gently sharpened, sixteen colours with dithered shading and a one-pixel outline, shown at whole device pixels, never smoothed and never lit by the scene: the clash is painted pixel art against flat-shaded geometry. Each pick's source render is kept with its prompt and cut options (tools/foeforge/picks), since AI art wants fixes on the source before a recut.

Decided

The battlefield is CARE's holographic reconstruction.

Gritty, sunlit 3D ground, gate, walls and machines against the rift's bright, flat 2D world bleeding in through a hard dithered seam. Height is landscape only. The skirmish is fought on the enemy's ground: machines face the foes in a rear three-quarter view turned left, foes face left. The rift vortex is four frames of dithered pixel art, never smoothed or lit.

Decided

The gate and walls are huge armoured Arsenal models.

They fill the bottom of the field with tower guns, and the gate projects the field over the city, its mouth the one gap every pack heads for.

Open

The gate's look.

A more sci-fi gate than the gothic one, how the projected field shows, the walls as emitters.

Sound and music

Decided

Industrial, grooving and grounded.

Music is a heavy industrial core carried by the beat, never fast (Tool, Nine Inch Nails, The Omnific, Teho, Hozho); effects are weight and machinery, never space opera. The grounded sound carries the seriousness that lets the game be silly elsewhere. Everything is synthesised from recipes in tools/soundforge/forge.py.

Decided

Two palettes, mixed on every screen.

Human: drums, chugged guitar, driven bass, electric piano, synth lead, metal chime, and a held saw drone in every track. Rift: orchestra, choir, long-echo bells, backwards swells and written dissonance. Bells and choir play in both.

Decided

The music never builds, drops the bass or plays a speed snare.

A section changes by what plays, lasts eight bars or more, and adds instruments, not loudness. Complexity comes from harmony and counterpoint, never fills; the kick never moves, so a change of metre sits over a bar of the same length. Boss themes are epic, not intense.

A domain may have a boss theme.

It starts from its top when the boss's fight opens; a domain without one takes the skirmish's pick.

Open

Boss themes for the Meadow, the Waste, the Peak and the finale.

Decided

One master, judged through the phone.

A gentle tilt, a presence lift at 1 to 2 kHz, no hard clip; the weight sits in the 150 to 800 Hz band a phone can play, the sub a bonus for headphones. The mix is measured through the phone model (a 220 Hz high-pass, a 9 kHz roll-off).

Open

Level the other five tracks against Hold The Line, through the phone model.

Decided

Sound lands by A/B on Ryan's phone.

A sketch never overwrites a shipped file; a pick ships from the function that rendered it, at the level he heard (the forge's SHIP table); melodies come through the Melody Desk.

Decided

Nothing glides in pitch.

Filters and loudness may move, so every effect is an impact, a mechanism or a burst. The exceptions are Aphelion's falling death whine and a short glide into a console note.

Decided

Combat has Doom's punch for both sides, never comical.

Each spend is deliberate, so a blow is drawn out, with a boom tail and weight in the 250 to 2000 Hz band. The scan and the idle are silent.

Decided

Effects fall into four registers.

The console; the iron (a blow by its weapon, then the hit, block or shield in the struck machine's metal, and footfalls by way of moving); the rift (each sound family's blow, heavy blow and arrival, and the boss reveal); the red (contact, the cut, the gate and the outcome, tuned to E).

Decided

A blow sounds by its component's shape and entry.

Content names a sound word only where those cannot (barrel, twin_barrel, rack, blade, blunt, laser, plasma, deflector, coil). The blunt weapons share one blow: a car hitting a wall.

Decided

Deaths are short for foes and personal for machines.

A foe dies in one short blast. A machine dies at the end of the enemy's turn, one at a time, in its maker's own explosion with a gap left for Jenny: Minerva two booms, Wainwright & Sutler winding down, Kestrel crash and fire, Aphelion discharge and beeps, the Arsenal flames over a ghost. No knocks; a shield breaks in a discharge, without shards.

Decided

The console is a nineties war room.

StarCraft, MechWarrior 2, Descent: retro-future and weighty, never arcade, chirpy or fast. Bowed glass and plate that groan, a boop in the register of StarCraft's nuke warning, a pulse, a soft click. Each kind of event has its own figure, since shared pitches read as confirmation; heat signals never fall (cancel) and never rise (confirm).

Recommended

Calls made while landing the effects stand until overturned.

A hovercraft steps like a flyer, random pitch goes only on footfalls and struck plates, and there is no route-drawing tick.

Pinned

The weapons' blows are acceptable as they are.

A burn or shock modification adding its own layer is a stretch goal; each family's own foe death comes later.

Jenny

Decided

The console speaks as Jenny.

The Jenny dataset (Dioco), rendered offline by Piper through the mid chain, slowed by a third, plosives tamed, on her own bus about ten decibels over the music, credited "Voice: Jenny (Dioco)".

Decided

Terse and military, and always on information.

She speaks only sometimes, but every time on information: a machine lost, a component damaged, heat past three quarters, an overheat, the gate breached. A rare personality layer (slang, references, Irish turns of phrase) is the only break in the fourth wall. She comments on the machine from outside it, censors herself under a tone, and calls the flattening corruption.

Decided

A line Ryan has heard stays unless he cuts it.

An unheard line comes in only when he adds it, every pool is rendered in full so each line can be judged, and the buff pool stays generic until the vocabulary is locked.

Pinned

The idle nag ("awaiting orders"); the loss pools stay thin.

Design tests

Every mechanic is held to these tests. Slice and Dice is the touchstone: target priority, semi-controlled randomness, build preparation and synergy.

Decided

Elegant fun, never complexity for its own sake.

Simple is fine: if every ability is a wall of text, the game is not playable on a phone.

Decided

A round is about thirty seconds of thought, with one to three real decisions.

Decided

The build is visible every round.

Components act on the die, never as mere stat bonuses.

Decided

The randomness is the player's, and it shows.

The die decides what a machine can do; a rolled weapon's damage decides how well. No hidden hit rolls and no crits. Enemies never roll damage: they draw their move and target from the seeded stream as the round opens and show both, exact, before the player acts (Griftlands); the one chance on their side, Numbed's resist, shows on its chip. One source of randomness means one the player can see and shift: the die, a rolled range, a rider's chance.

Decided

The game does the arithmetic.

A flat weapon never rolls, however many things feed its number, and the die shows the number its blow will deal, for the foe it is dragged over.

Decided

Chances live only on riders, and riders are additive.

Never a chance on the damage (a rolled range is variance the player sees and can reroll, not a chance) and never a binary win or lose. A chance the player can shift by their own choices is a decision; one they cannot touch is noise. Ryan, 16 October: random effects create high moments if they can be controlled; random that takes away feels bad (a miss, a failed dodge, a foe randomly picking the weak machine), random that adds feels good or fine (a chain into the low-hull backliner feels good; one into a front-rank goblin instead still did damage).

Decided

A damage rider on an action the player takes anyway needs a limit.

It costs no tempo, so nothing else holds it back (Ryan, 13 October, of the Wainwright & Sutler boiler, the Whip Scorpion since 20 October, whose vents blast a random foe for the heat they shed: the chance is its limit, and the player times it, so it stays a decision).

Decided

The player always has tools against the roll.

Rerolls before or after attacking, flat weapons, modules that raise a floor, visible enemy health: variance is a choice to mitigate, never a punishment.

Decided

Avoid binary play where possible.

A soft counter, taking back part of what the other side gains, beats a hard one wherever it makes sense: burn never stops healing, so a burning healer is a race, not a wall. Hard counters are not banned; the game has a stun.

Decided

Asymmetry over parity.

Pieces need no numeric parity with each other; asymmetric balance is harder, and it is where the interesting choices are. The guard is against must-picks (pick share in the runner, section 11), not against difference.

Decided

Builds grow from soft synergies, never a lynchpin.

The player crafts what works from what they find. A piece without which a build does not work (an ARPG's chase item) makes the player farm for it; this game avoids that (section 7, tags).

Decided

Every pack poses a question.

Finish the resurrectors, interrupt the big hit: never a damage race in new art.

Decided

Mismatch costs time, never ability.

A wrong loadout fights longer or takes more damage, but always acts.

Decided

Never helpless, sometimes unlucky.

The design removes helplessness, not luck.

Decided

Rule text is a budget, not a cap.

About three rule texts on a chassis's description, its passive and its modules; a component's rule shows in its own popup and is not counted (Ryan, 12 October). Each is one idea in three lines at most (an upside and its cost are one idea). Common components carry none; a rare one carries a unique rule (the chase: the player may break the game now and then); a module is a small number and a stat line. Judged running on a phone with every trigger firing; the Clydesdale overruns it on purpose.

Decided

A map action that buys a fight buff is not a condition.

Moving or standing still, joining a fight, being caught: the player does these anyway, so the buff they switch on is a boost for a good part of the fight, and completely free.

Decided

A one-off exception for balance is a symptom: find the root cause first.

Ryan, 18 October: "If a situation occurs where it seems reasonable to propose a one-off exception for balance reasons, look into the root causes as to why that seems like a reasonable solution." The case in point was the revolver: "Rapid once a round on it" looked reasonable because a Rapid chain on a stepping die is free, and the root was that Rapid pieces pay no heat, the stock pass's matter and no rule on one chassis. A passive may still break a rule as a one-off for its design (section 7); this row is about exceptions reached for to fix a number.

Decided

Every rule and guideline is open to debate.

A good reason moves any of them: the tests in this section, a maker's profile, a settled row. That is how the game iterates and improves, so a proposal argues for changing a rule in the open rather than designing around it.

3

Campaign loop

The campaign is the run ("run" never means a battle), and battles are its floors. The rules are in docs/rules.md, the campaign part.

Decided

Losing the capital loses the run.

Everything in the run goes with it; only the pool, the codex, difficulty tiers, a Sponsorship piece and livery carry over (section 10).

Decided

Five cities.

The capital at the centre (the Council and the Arsenal) and four around it, each hosting a maker. A city's gate is its only health number and carries between battles.

Decided

The mission map is a choice of front, never city management.

A front's card names its domain and guest, its ground, the Convergence's tier and how long its gate holds if ignored; its formation is fuzzy until scouted.

Decided

The whole roster goes to one front; the fronts left alone bleed.

Ryan, 13 October: the intent, which the records had let blur, is to bring every machine to one front and fight it out while the other open fronts weaken their cities by attrition; the fronts keep moving and new ones open, so every front is tackled in time. Splitting the squad across two fronts was never the intention. A hangar larger than the squad is the answer to a battle that goes badly: spare chassis let the next front be fought without sitting the whole team out. The rules already say one battle a turn with up to four ready machines, and the campaign runner sends every ready machine to one front; what thins its team today is wrecks sitting out with no spares, which the hangar and the economy answer, and fights inside a battle that one machine fights alone (section 4's pacing row).

Decided

The capital's gate is the run's life bar.

Into the Breach's grid power: fallen cities drain it, drawn as hordes marching on the centre, and the finale is fought on what is left. Its repair is the one priced repair, so it weighs survival against the team.

Decided

A city falls when its gate reaches zero, fought or not.

It costs that battle's salvage and its maker's future blueprints, research and stock, never the player's kit; its rift stays open and its horde drains the capital.

Decided

No forced last stand, and a won battle restores the gate free.

The map keeps its one choice and no city runs on a one-way clock. The restore is free of salvage, not of Favour: the win's fee is docked a point per gate point lost, which is what pushers let through cost (section 3's tally row). The free restore is a lever for higher difficulty tiers.

Decided

A front is a rift whose domain is rolled as it opens.

Never fixed to a city: a fixed pairing would solve the map.

Decided

The Convergence makes the finale inevitable; finals are a choice.

Three bosses of one domain beaten draw its hero into a final, whose win thins that domain from the rifts and the finale (section 9). Four finals are impossible by design, so which domains to chase is always a choice: one or two the goal, three a feat.

Decided

The Rupture meter and Anathema fill by the threat-weighted tally.

Ryan, 13 October: what a foe is worth by its threat powers both the battle's meter and each domain's bar, because a count of fights or kills would make picking off small packs with flyers the optimal play and leave a strong foe worth seeking only for its drop. The gate is the other price on that play: a won battle restores the gate free, but the win's Favour is docked a point per gate point lost, so pushers let through cost the roster's growth; whether that bites is the dock rate and Favour's worth, the economy pass's numbers. Threat-weighted, a detachment's kills add little meter, so its worth is defence and tempo (section 4). The meter counts live during the battle; salvage still tallies at its end. Today a count of three bosses stands in for Anathema. Not built.

Decided

Difficulty grows by smarter foes and nastier compositions, never by inflating the rank and file.

Aims, conditions, behaviours, back ranks, elites and pack count, so upgrades buy adaptability against questions, not damage against hull. A boss tier and a promoted hero carry numbers of their own (section 9: the promoted form's size, armour and extra move), which is a new foe, never an old one with a bigger number. A battle has one lead domain and at most one guest until the last tier, whose signature is anything from the roster. Section 9's recolour tier (the same foe repainted with a bigger number) is open against this; section 12.

Decided

The hangar has no chores.

Repair is free and refit instant; resources go only to upgrades, builds and the capital's gate, and a rushed wreck is the one priced exception. The wreck's time in the shop is why a roster outgrows the squad. A machine reads in two words: in the shop or ready, dressed or bare.

Decided

A team wipe is a graded loss.

The horde strikes the gate unopposed and the city falls only if the gate gives out, so the gate a city carries in buys its survival.

Recommended

A passed turn is a turn.

Waiting for the shop never stops the clock. Built.

Decided

The starting draft is a tender.

One tier-one machine from each city maker, so every maker gets a hearing; the player takes three. The Arsenal never offers.

Recommended

Twelve to sixteen battles, four to six hours with saves.

Ryan, 13 October: a longer campaign is fine, so the roster grows and is outfitted, which is the mech fantasy, as long as a battle stays short; the length to find is where racing the game's power feels right and a new chassis is worth trying rather than the starters being all one can afford. The design already removes the sunk cost: kit moves freely, refits are instant, repair is free, no chassis is strictly better, the fourth slot is empty from the start and wrecks force rotation. The levers are the chassis offer rate, one every two or three battles, and the battle count; the test is how many different chassis a run fields, which the runner reads once offers exist. The acts are the Convergence's tiers.

Open

Sensor contacts, at the campaign layer only.

Formations fuzzy until scouted, scouting a research path. In battle a pack may show as a cosmetic blip, since hiding its type would break the exact preview.

Pinned

An endless mode, after the bounded campaign.

4

Battlefield layer

The battlefield is the layer with positioning, so the skirmish has none. Its rules are in docs/rules.md, the battlefield part.

The field

Decided

A pointy-top hex grid nine wide, gate south, rift north.

Taller than a screen and scrolled, one unit to a hex. Nine is the thumb's limit, holds two fights apart and gives a detachment room; the two-and-two split it was sized for is an emergency now (the four-machines row), and nine stays until phone reads say the hexes are too small (Ryan, 13 October); pointy-top makes defensive lines straight rows; a hex is at least 48 px wide on screen.

Decided

Every map guarantees the horde a crossing at least three hexes wide, and one real water feature.

Holding every pack takes three machines. The water is a river with fords or a lake band. Height is landscape; no rule reads it.

Decided

Five map types, and the name is the intel.

The player reads a word, never a share. Mixed Ground (the base generator); Open Steppe (mostly open, a lake: wheels and the horde fly); Rubble Field (a ruins belt, the road its one lane: the walker's map); Badlands (crag ridges, tight crossings: the flyer's map); Flooded (a river, a lake, puddles: hover and tracks ford). Each passes "mismatch costs time, never ability". The type shows on the front card beside the domain and tier (not built: campaign battles all use Mixed Ground; the harness picks any).

Decided

Each way of moving calls each ground fast, normal or slow, and a slow hex ends the move.

No move buys through a stop, so every way keeps a good and a bad ground and the player never weighs two numbers. Moves follow size and role; the content check flags, never refuses, a ground chassis outpacing another way everywhere (flyers exempt).

Decided

Foes move binary.

The Final Fantasy overworld rule: broken ground delays only machines, and terrain is never a bonus to foes.

Decided

The player never does arithmetic on the map.

The map draws reach; a slow end hex is dashed (structure, not hue); move is a pip gauge with a slow last step hatched; the codex gives move in open-ground hexes; a terrain tap gives each way's word. Move rewards come in tens.

Decided

A pack is one sprite and a number badge.

Weapons

Decided

A weapon is rolled or flat by its niche.

Flat is reliable (a flat 15 kills a foe on 15 every time, a 10 to 20 half the time) and the finisher; rolled is not, and is the opener and the boss chipper. Ryan, 13 October: heat is a resource and a balancing attribute beyond damage, the secondary cost beside a cooldown (section 8), and like reach it is set by the component's niche. Rolled or flat and hot or cold are four corners, never two pairs: it was never the rule that flat weapons make heat and rolled ones do not, though the content still carries that split from the old kinetic and energy classes (every rolled weapon cold, every flat one hot: 6.4 against 14.3 heat a spend in the 13 October report), and the stock pass re-prices heat by niche. Where a flat weapon's average matches a rolled one's, the reliability is paid for somewhere, heat the usual place, or flat becomes the default; where it does not, heat is free to say something else about the piece.

Decided

No weapon classes, no crits, no Execute, no Steady.

Each failed a test of section 2, and the test is the rule: crits put a chance on the damage; Execute was a binary kill; Steady was an average, a number dressed as a rule; classes asked for parity. A new idea that is none of those is free to be proposed. Chances live only on riders. Tags carry no rule of their own (section 7). Damage that grows with the target's missing hull is a unique rule on one piece today (the Pinion Driver); a second carrier would make it a keyword, as with the Boar's spill. A foe that gets back up is answered by Suppress, Disrupt and kill order.

Decided

Two reaches, and no falloff.

Direct reaches the back only through a hole; Indirect reaches anyone. A third, Close, was a worse Direct whose price existed only against a back rank: two archetypes are plenty. Melee is a tag. The old close weapons come down to Direct's band in the stock pass.

Decided

Reach is priced.

At equal heat Direct hits harder than Indirect, unless the runner shows heat is the better price for Indirect. Measured 11 October: Indirect lands about 12 hull a spend for 8 heat against Direct's 18 for 9, and danger close moves no row's win rate by more than three points, so the numbers carry the price and the splash only steers Indirect toward the back.

Decided

Indirect is reach only, and area belongs to a keyword.

Ryan, 11 October, agreeing the line-up's proposal: danger close goes, so a beam or a phased emitter can be Indirect without exploding, and an explosive says so with an area keyword on any reach. Danger close priced nothing and only steered Indirect toward the back (above). Not built: it waits on the area keywords (below) and on a new rule for the Artillery Rangefinder, whose rule was sparing it.

Control and statuses

Decided

Burn never stops healing.

Damage over time soft-counters healing, taking back some or all of what is healed.

Decided

The Pocket Paradox is a meter.

It counts applications of other statuses, never stacks, so tripping it takes several components or a hit carrying several statuses, and it tunes by its threshold. A chance to suppress was binary; a debuff that zeroes damage was toxic against fodder and bosses alike. A trip that Numbed resists still spends the meter, so it never sits primed to try again, and so does a trip on a foe already suppressed (Ryan, 17 October: "Suppressed is suppressed - they lose their next action. It does not stack, it does not carry. That way lies madness"; the carry the engine ran from 10 October, a trip on a suppressed foe taking its next action, was never his call and is gone). Its existence never dictates another design: a status that feeds it counts, and the count is tuned for the final ecosystem (Ryan, 17 October).

Decided

Numbed is a buff nothing removes, and nothing touches it.

A buff with a cannot-be-removed line, not a kind of its own (Ryan, 18 October: its own kind was one type too many, "no reason to have a billion types of objects"), which nothing reads, deepens, spreads, cleanses or purges: a stun resistance that effects can change is where other games' combat loops broke. It decays, so rotating stuns is a slope, not a wall, and its slope says spread the stuns. No foe resists by nature: the chip shows who resists and for how long, and a phone has no room for an innate figure. If a sure first stun on a boss proves too strong, this is revisited. A suppress on a foe already suppressed for that action does nothing and numbs it no further (Ryan, 17 October: Suppressed never stacks and never carries, a Paradox's trip included). Built 17 October.

Decided

A status stacks or renews, as its definition says.

Ryan, 17 October: renewal without stacking must be supported, "a pretty common thing for a powerful or non-number-related buff to do" (the shield-spender's regen, docs/briefs/kestrel_shield_spender.md, is the first to want it). The engine reads the policy off the status: add, the default, a stack a grant, a timed grant keeping its own end (Ryan, 16 October: "Timed stacks should decay based on when they started. Not stack and refresh."); renew, one grant whose end is the later and whose count the larger (Suppressed, Retaliate; and Shocked, Getting Back Up, Guarded and Scrambled, untimed one-stack statuses on which a renewal changes nothing: never stacking and renewing are one state, parted only by a timer, Ryan, 17 October). Section 6's table checks its stacking word against the policy, so it never says what the code does not do. Built 17 October. A status either runs down to zero on its own clock or is timed, never both (Ryan, 18 October: "A halving rule is a halving rule, it doesn't have a duration. In the same way that a decrementing rule doesn't have a duration, just lowers by one ... Things either decrease to zero, or are timed, not both"): Overcharged halves and takes no rounds from the component that grants it, Armoured, Primed and Retaliate end with their timer, and the validator refuses rounds on a halving status (built 18 October).

Decided

Armoured and Overcharged count in points, and Overcharged halves.

Ryan, 17 and 18 October, closing section 12's question on the stacks of 5. Both sides say Armoured (Braced until 18 October; the engine id stays) and a stack is an armour point, so the chip's count is the armour ("Easy to read at one glance"); its duration stands (the row below). Overcharged is a damage point a stack and halves at the end of each round, rounded down, whatever granted it, until it is gone: a stack fading a round let a source feeding two a round climb without limit, where a halving one settles near twice what it is fed a round ("to prevent stacking to the moon and fueling massive ramp strategies"), and Ryan would grant it to more things on that clock. Rounded down because a status decays to zero or is timed, never both (the row above): rounded up, one stack would halve to one and never end. The Stingray's Exothermic Suite now adds a point per 20 heat to either where it added five; Ryan expects, and hopes, that it is the more balanced for it (section 11's scales row: the numbers are pushed down). The Ammit's Quintessence Tap keeps every Overcharged on it for the battle whatever granted it (Ryan, 18 October: "that's the rules text, it doesn't care where it came from"; the engine kept only the talons' until then), for the battle only, never between battles, everything resetting at the hangar (Ryan, 18 October; the engine builds each battle's machines afresh from the hangar's records), its kill reward two stacks; the release valve, should it need one, is an enemy silence or purge (Ryan: ok; the Silence and Purge row below), and nothing else is considered unless the Ammit "proves stupid overpowered": the simple version is measured first. Re-numbered with it: the Shoring Jack Armoured 10, the Breaching Plow 10, the Phase Conjugator 3 a shield, the foes' brace its value in stacks, the Overcharge Coil 2 (Ryan: +10 for a round was "silly high" for a support action spent the same turn as a combo), the Grievance Reliquary a stack a blow, the Soulcutter Talons two a kill. Built 18 October (483 tests): two values in the status table, a halving decay beside the four, a buff component's grant of a halving status carrying no rounds, the keep applied to every grant, the brace validator asking a positive number. Reopens if the count moving by halves reads badly on the chip in play, or if a buff wants a run-down the halving cannot give.

Decided

Burn is a numbered keyword, and Burning a damage point a stack.

Ryan, 18 October: "Is burn still tied to the 10 damage threshold as specified in the codex? It should be fixed to a numbered keyword, like a weapon applies 'Burn 2' for 2 stacks, irregardless of damage. Also, in the spirit of flattening numbers, burn should only do 1 damage per stack." So a Burn weapon says its stacks (Burn 3: three stacks of Burning on each foe hit, whatever the blow dealt; a stack per 10 of the blow, rounded up, since 2 October until then), the first numbered keyword, carried on the component's face beside the keyword and asked for by the validator; a foe's Burning loses a damage point a stack at its action (5 before), so Burn 3 is 3, 2, 1 over three actions. The machine side (5 heat a stack) stands until heat is re-priced (section 12). First fills, Ryan's to move: Burn 3 on the 10-damage pieces (the Blazethrower, the Solar Flare Array, the Photosphere Lance, the Incendiary Shells), Burn 5 on the 15s (the Meteor Artillery, the Gravity Knuckles), which keep each weapon's burn near what it dealt before (6 and 15 over the ticks against 5 and 15); the Stingray's Suite adds a stack, a point, per 20 heat. Built 18 October. Reopens with the hull scale: when foe hull comes down, the numbers come down with it.

Decided

Stun is the best mechanic in any game, so adding hard control is always weighed first.

Ryan, 13 October: Suppress is prevalent and needs dialling back (the 13 October report: the Umbra Inducer 58% and the Megataser Harpoon 64% of their machines' spends, Numbed the only brake, three more sources among the proposals). The drift behind it: proposed designs kept reaching for a stun or a burn in place of build space. The roster analysis (14 October) measured the roster against itself and proposed caps (no hard-control piece below tier two, at most one locked on a die, one hard-control passive a maker). Ryan, 15 October, struck the caps as artificially limiting and set a rule in their place: adding hard control is always carefully considered, and other options explored, before it is suggested or committed to. Ryan, 17 October, on its price: a cooldown is no penalty for a Suppress, since Numbed is in effect a cooldown (each landed suppress adds three stacks to a massive foe and two to a small one, one fades a turn, so on one foe the landed rate settles at about one stun in three rounds however often it is tried; in a formation with fresh targets it never engages); and the more mitigation the game adds, the more Suppress must be priced so the other options stay attractive, since scarcity would make its carriers (the funnel-web) must-picks. Also his, the same day: without hard control a tank can become required, so damage must stay a viable way to win, Suppress may be allowed more widely if gated by a condition worked for or a price paid, and off-tank mitigation (a Barrier slot-in) is part of the answer. Ryan, 18 October, on what prices it: not the damage it deals, which is incidental, since a stun's worth is a turn where the party is not under threat and the other machines fire their best without fear (Darkest Dungeon's lesson, taken less far than it took it: its stuns needed a two-turn setup, too awkward to use reactively, so they rewarded planning ahead and sacrificing chip damage on a quiet turn); so a Suppress is gated by a condition, a setup, a self-sacrifice or its timing, and the player decides whether to build a team that makes it worthwhile. The Paradox is the model: good design, a balanceable mechanic the player interacts with, its numbers to tune. Claude's no-damage proposal retired for the reason the cooldown was: it priced the stun where it is weakest (fodder) and not where it is strongest (a boss). Today's stuns by gate: the funnel-web's web by timing (the foe acts once before the burst), the Paradox by setup, the Suppression Battery by self-sacrifice (its 20 heat, a price once heat bites), the Umbra Inducer ungated and the Baboon's session's. A gate is a rule, so gated Suppress pieces sit above the basics by the rule-text row (section 2), scarcity by reason rather than cap. Claude's leans for the stock pass: the Battery as counter-battery, Suppressing a back-rank foe only, so a massive boss is beyond it; the Inducer Suppressing a foe carrying a debuff, the Tensor's read. The slot-in question is the stock pass's (docs/todo.md).

Decided

Another look: own rerolls as a number (the engine runs it: the Clydesdale's Battery Director, a static effect a module may carry today), on the machines whose identity reads its die, priced so no module is the universal pick. A second action: the team's order before self-sequencing (an ally's action as the pair: the Dingo's Shock to the Sparrowhawk's cash); a die mechanic where the sequence is the identity (the Cassowary's stepping); and where one machine must act twice, a price in a banked resource (the Nemesis's rolls again as a family, one resource a maker: rage, the hopper, Spun Up, shells, Overcharged, Kestrel's heat), a cooldown burst (two actions' worth, then dark, once the cooldown property lands) or a delayed action (the bomber's mines, the web, a charge that arms now and fires later). The cantrip: combined entries, two effects on one spend (a lure that regens itself, a vent that shields, a gun that vents on fire), with nothing to chain and nothing to limit. Tempo lent as a status is Rapid by another name, ranked last. Beyond the dice, commander actions on map-turn cooldowns (section 8: movement and manipulation, never a fifth gun): a sortie, a scramble, a field refit; action economy nobody can stack. Three rules of thumb for every design, rework and rebalance: a design that wants many blows is pointed at strikes; one that wants order is given a die mechanic; one that wants a second action pays in a banked resource, a cooldown or an ally's turn; Rapid stays for the cantrip, gated (if it kills, on a cold spend, once a round, by a cylinder). For the stock pass: the four ungated carriers re-priced (heat on the Vacillator and the Flakshot, the Deflector a tier-two blueprint under Ryan's rule of 20 October, a Rapid shield never below tier two and a Rapid vent never below tier three; the Diffuser settled that day, a vent that shields, no Rapid), every new Rapid gated, cantrips as combined entries before Rapid ones, own-reroll modules priced, multi-strike pieces as the blow currency: Claude's leans, Ryan's numbers. Thorns, damage paid at the blow, scale with enemy blows, strong against a horde and useless against a boss, and on a tank they stack into a wall: dangerous rather than forbidden, a thorn is balanced only by a drawback it carries and never rides a megatank (Ryan, 12 October). Retribution is the Arsenal's strongest suit (a lean, section 7); a retaliation elsewhere needs a fantasy of its own, which is the Armadillo's open question. Shocked doubles the team's next blow, so the tank sets up kills, and does nothing else: the 10 a Shocked striker once paid on every blow was thorns left in, on a tank, and it went. The condition that reopens a reward for being struck (Ryan, 20 October, the Whip Scorpion's Heliographic Siphon): the machine spends its own turn to be struck, deals nothing that turn, and the reward comes later and at random (heat its vents blast next round), so it is paid for in tempo and is not the old discharge, which rode free on a turn the tank took anyway.

Decided

Each foe picks its own target, by its aim, as the round opens.

Weighted, shown and committed. There is no taunt stat: redirection is a spend that shows on the intent (Lure), the codex says what each foe goes for (so a domain teaches how to be hit on purpose), and there is never a dodge chance.

Decided

A foe's blow may land on every machine.

Ryan, 15 October: no foe struck more than one machine, the storms' three blows included (each lands on the one machine picked), a gap the foe kit refresh fills; the engine piece is built first. A move tagged all (a working name; "Every Machine" in the codex) lands each of its strikes on every live machine in turn, left to right, with its riders on each, so no aim and no Lure moves it. Its arrows reach every machine, the forecast counts it on each, and the plaque names it once. Its number is what each machine takes, so a move with it is priced well under a single blow from the same foe. A shield saves only the machine it stands on; the answers that stop the whole blow are at the striker: kill it first, Suppress it, Disrupt it. The storms carry it since the foe kit refresh (Ryan, 15 October: Root Storm, Quake, Bone Storm and Cleave, each one blow on every machine at the factor its three used to share).

Decided

Every threat shape has two answers or more, and none has all.

Lure, Suppress and Disrupt touch a telegraphed move; shield and armour take it; Breach beats the buffer by design, and a blow at every machine beats Lure. Cleanse stays while riders persist.

Decided

Damage redirection is common, never a tank's lock.

Lure today. A draw by an ally (an interpose) comes back only on a locked piece, never as a core mechanic (Ryan, 12 October): Guard was built and taken out because a standing draw made one machine the target of everything, which is the lock; a conditional draw on a locked piece passes (the Onryō's Pall is the proposal, the Warrior's Cover its mirror on the foes' side).

Pinned

A deterministic reflect as a boss effect.

Decided

Phased: cannot act, take damage or be targeted, and only before it acts.

Ryan, 20 October, in the helicarrier's session, on Claude's ruling: "cannot act, take damage, or be targeted" is the cleaner wording, and it holds until the round's end; granted only to a unit that has not acted this round, so the status always costs the action it protects (his first form, invulnerable now and the next action lost, offset the two halves, as he saw). Not targetable means allies cannot vent or shield it either and a foe's intent on it re-picks, which moves the intent lines as a dock should without a lure; it still cools by its own cooling, so an overheated machine phases with its problem intact for next turn, and ticks and overheats still land. The one rule on the status gates every grant of it: the helicarrier's dock (every ally that has not acted), and Aphelion's planned pieces. Section 12's question on Phased (a design line before code) is answered by it.

Mitigation, heat and injury

Decided

A shield absorbs its number, and the excess passes to the hull.

Ryan, 14 October. Under the old rule (a standing shield blocked a whole blow, however small) a 5-shield entry was secretly "negate one blow", worth the biggest blow it met rather than its number, and the content kept rediscovering it: the Spall Liner's and the Boundary-layer Bleed's small stand-up shields, a screen of 10 to every ally (four blows negated for one spend), a tiny bait for Misharu. Measured 12 October (greedy, 30 seeds a cell, a row mean over twenty packs; docs/measurements.md): letting every blow's excess past a standing shield moved the machines' mean win rate by a point, because the bot never fielded a tiny shield; the rule bit only at the margin every new shield design was heading for. Now a shield is an honest number, a breach ignores the shield outright (the shield stands, so the Waste keeps its signature and gains two dear answers, the Barrier and the Ward, below), and the one-blow block becomes a priced, single-target buff of its own. What softens: shield answers the big blow by its size, not by standing; the Peak's telegraph wants a shield sized to it or a Barrier; the Goshawk's regen and the Puffin's overshield become hit points; shields may want raising in the stock pass. Shield still returns only at the field rate out of fights, so withdrawing is no reset. Not built.

Decided

Barrier and Ward: the block is a buff, one machine at a time.

Ryan, 14 October, the upside of the honest shield. A Barrier on a machine means the next blow a foe lands on it deals no damage, so it never injures, until the round's end; its riders land. A Ward means every blow a foe lands on it that round leaves no rider, breach included, so the shield absorbs the needle; the damage lands. Both up is the old whole block at twice its price. The shapes they answer part by domain, the Barrier the Peak's telegraph and a boss's blow, the Ward the Waste's breach and the Peak's scorch, so a team carries what its fronts ask. A Barrier is never granted to every ally at once: four blows negated for one spend is the exploit the shield rule removed. It is Shocked's mirror on our side (the next blow at zero), so the engine's shape exists. The Ward lasts the round because riders come in volume where the big blow comes alone (Ryan), and its worth is thin while only two domains lay riders on machines, which the foes' fresh start may change. Carriers and numbers are the stock pass's; the Blast Door's fantasy is a Barrier already. Ward is the buff's word, so the Oblation Ward is the Oblation Mantle (in content since 15 October, its id kept) and the Votive Ward, still a brief's, the Votive Screen (Ryan, 15 October). Ryan, 17 October: Barrier and Ward together want the same care as Suppress, and if a Barrier grows common the foes need more damaging riders to carry past it (damage over time, on-hit effects, a countdown's blow), which is the fresh start's riders-in-every-domain line; a countdown's blow is a Barrier's intended prey, its chip naming the round. The two buffs are not built.

Decided

Armour is a flat number off every blow that reaches the hull, and zero is fine.

Shield answers the big blow, armour the swarm. A breach's overflow loses armour too: a breach beats the shield, never the plate under it, so a small breach does nothing to a heavy machine and a big one still lands. A walled foe is answered by foes that do more than claw, never by a damage floor.

Decided

The Boar keeps its spill, written out; Breach is the foes' word.

Ryan, 12 October: the spill matches the Boar's brief and serves "a passive opens design space": it gives flexibility, which the runner cannot measure (it reads about a point of win rate, since a foe behind with blow to spare is rare); if it proves weak it is buffed. Its passive says it in full. Ryan, later on 12 October: no keyword the player learns, being unconditional and the Boar's today (if the Boar, then this), though a keyword in one place costs the player no memory either; a second source granting it would make it one. The engine runs it as a keyword the passive grants to every weapon the Boar fires (the vocabulary's Spillover, the rules' component rules), so today every weapon entry on its die wears a Spillover chip, since the die shows each entry as its passives make it; proposed for the stock pass: the star chip in its place (section 6), the hold reading the passive's line. Ryan, 15 October: "All its damage or subsequent strikes spill over": the killing strike's excess and every strike after it, each whole, carry into the foe behind (before, a weapon of several strikes lost its later strikes on the fallen foe). A carry past a kill stays the Boar's: a multi-hit weapon strikes its one target, because that is intuitive (Ryan, the same day, in Kestrel's session). Built that day. Breach stays the foes' word, and since 14 October it ignores the shield outright (the shield row above); no machine's blow meets a shield, since foes carry none.

Recommended

Shields are a buffer, and heat is the second resource.

A typical pack wears a shield down in two or three rounds; the Barrier is the timer now, one blow and one turn; heat is a team problem.

Decided

Machines cool at the start of the round and overheat at the end of the player's turn.

So a hot machine can vent first, and an overheat is the machine's own doing on its own turn: with volatile, the one way to die on your own turn. A withdrawal ends the turn too, so running hot and leaving is no way round the burn. A foe adds no heat outright: its Burning lands at the end of the player's turn, before the overheat, and its Scorched heats only the weapons fired (below). An overheat is hull damage for every passive. Cooling stays well under a weapon's heat.

Decided

Heat bites where it is carried; a cold build pays elsewhere.

Ryan, 13 October. Heat is the one cost that competes with damage on the same die (a cooldown is the other, printed on the pieces that carry one: section 8), and what makes rolled against flat a choice (the rolled-or-flat row); priced per piece but never felt, it is decoration. Today it is barely felt outside the Peak: vents sit at zero to six percent of spends and cooling and heat maximum price at nothing. The test, Claude's placeholders until the stock pass and play: a build that runs hot overheats by its third round of firing without a vent, so the hot builds decide every fight with a vent in it; a cold build is a healthy build and pays somewhere else, in a rolled range, a short reach, a lower number or fewer strikes, never for free; and the Peak's Burning and Scorched press a resource the hot builds already feel rather than being the only heat in the game. Ryan's caveat: heat is not judged from a lack of content. Eleven weapons carry no heat today (the Chainsaw Claw, the Twin Autoguns, the Ankle Mortar, the Howitzer, the Incendiary Shells, the Pinion Driver, the Proximity Klaxon, the Breaching Plow, the Flakshot Repeater, the Afterburner Cannon's base, the Rivet Gun), most of them rolled kinetics with low numbers, which is a cold build paying in reliability as meant; what each pays is the stock pass's question piece by piece, and whether heat bites is judged once the roster has hot builds to feel it. A cool weapon is also soft cooling (Ryan, 13 October): spending it instead of a hotter, harder one is a cooldown turn. So heat builds are a spectrum between two extremes, a kit tuned to its passive cooling that never vents and a hot kit that buys its heat back with vent turns, and the middle is how many vents and how many cool weapons a build runs. Heat is a budget (the maximum plus cooling times the rounds) against a demand (the heat the damage-optimal spends would add); the efficient build spends all of it and ends near the maximum, which is good play, not the problem. The worst case for balance is slack: a kit with the hot build's damage that ends the fights that matter with budget unused never paid for its damage, and everything converges on it. The test: demand passes budget by about the third round of a hot kit's firing, so a formation fight forces a cooling turn or two, by vent or by cool weapon, and a short fight forces none; a cold build pays its lower numbers and reach in every fight and collects in the long ones (endurance), the hot build is burst, the mixed build is the flexible one. What the runner reads, per row, spikes rather than actions: peak heat as a share of the maximum and the rounds above four fifths of it; overheats and the hull they burned; forced choices, the rounds where the best-damage entry showing would have crossed the maximum and a vent, a cooler weapon or nothing was spent instead; the damage forgone to heat over those rounds, the one number that says what heat charged (near zero on a hot kit is the worst case); and the slack left at the fight's end against the fight's length. The same chassis fielded three ways reads side by side. The levers are the maxima, cooling, the weapons' heat and the vents' numbers.

Decided

Foes heat machines only through debuffs: Burning and Scorched.

Heat applied straight is so polarising that it needs several avenues of counterplay, or it spirals. Ryan, 12 and 13 October: direct heat goes, and two debuffs carry it, named Burn and Scorch, the player's keyword reused and acting differently on a machine. Burning, the harsher, adds 5 heat a stack at the end of the player's turn, before the overheat, then loses a stack, so the player can vent the heat already there or cleanse the debuff: two answers. Burning already means a tick to the player, and metal does not burn, it heats: one word with two effects by body, so the hold says which. It is also the first debuff that means something on either side (a heat tick on a machine, a hull tick on a foe), which an Arsenal cleanse that moves debuffs onto a foe would need (section 7). Scorched, the softer, makes every weapon the machine fires run 5 hotter a stack until the end of the player's turn, when every stack goes, so spending equipment or a cool weapon plays round it; the die shows the hotter heat. Both cleanse. One new word, where two fire words (scorch, sear, singe) would never say which is which, and "Overheating" is gone, since the real overheat had the word. Every move that heated a machine took one by how threatening it must be, on the rule of thumb that a move on every machine takes the softer, since four machines ticking at once is the spiral direct heat had (Claude's fill, Ryan's to move): the Drake's Stoke sets the hottest machine burning, two stacks; the Magma Golem's Eruption, the Black Mage's Fira and the Summoner's Pyroclasm scorch every machine, two; the four scorching blows (the Bomb's Flare Up, the Salamander's Bite, the Drake's Breath, the Black Mage's Fire) leave Scorched. A foe's blow may carry burn too; none does yet. Burning on a machine has a glyph of its own (Ryan, 13 October): Burn's flame filled solid with the heat glyph's thermometer cut out of it, apart from the overheat's bare thermometer and from Primed's hollow flame, which a hot machine may wear beside it; Scorched has a placeholder of its own, heat rising off a barrel. Heat reads adds before multiplies, as a blow's damage does, so the Sparrowhawk's double doubles Scorched's extra too (Ryan asked, 13 October): a stack makes its 10-heat weapon run 30, and its die says so. Built 13 October. Measured the same day (30 seeds a cell, a direction; docs/measurements.md): the teams do not move, the Peak's formation opens to the Wainwright & Sutler team now that Fira and Hellfire heat nobody outright, machines overheat far less and fire hotter instead, and the Goshawk, whose passive turns heat into shield, is the one that loses, since the old tick paid its shield every round. Scorched losing a stack a turn was tried first and piled up under several scorchers; every stack going at the turn's end is what is built. Exit: the Goshawk's loss and the Peak's numbers are Ryan's play to judge.

Decided

An injury is one threshold and one die roll on bare hull, and it lasts the battle.

The one permanent cost of a bad fight, answered by shielding before a big telegraphed blow. The threshold is a share of maximum hull on purpose (Ryan, 11 October): asymmetric balancing, so the tank and the glass cannon both have a place, and full greedy aggression risks something against its reward. The threshold may move and the rule be reworked; it goes only if it stops serving that purpose, never because a fight's numbers barely feel it.

Pinned

A low-hull berserker or last-stand entry, an anti-spiral.

Recommended

Never a bonus to foes. Nothing acts on it yet.

The die

Decided

A die entry reads at a glance from fixed places.

The primary glyph top centre (what it does when spent: Direct crosshair, Indirect arc, shield, vent, buff, blank), the number beneath, heat bottom left, its place on the die top left, a chip per keyword down the right edge, a star chip for a unique effect. The place (Ryan, 15 October: “Roman numerals in the corner? I don't think it's the size that makes it subtle, so much as the font and color choices”) is a grey Roman numeral in the quotes' condensed italic, so it never reads as a stat; it counts the die in its order, fixed components first, then the hardpoints, and the hold and the codex number their lists the same way. It shows which hardpoint holds what, a reroll that lands on a copy, and a stepping die's next entry (the revolver). A shield that also buffs is the shield glyph plus a buff chip. The glyphs are placeholders. One star stands for any number of rules, so stars never need merging (Ryan, 12 October): the hold lists every rule an entry carries on its line and every passive's change in its notes.

Decided

The die shows the number one blow will deal.

A range if rolled, one number if flat. Dragged over a foe it shows that foe's number. The game does the arithmetic; the player reads it. Its heat is what the spend will add, every modifier in (13 October, so a Scorched machine's die shows its weapons running hotter, and the Sparrowhawk's its double).

Decided

Every entry is a component, a blank or a destroyed component.

Nothing fills an empty hardpoint, so a full, undamaged machine is reliable. Shown text never says "face" (the player sees no polyhedron); Face is the engine's word for one entry. Whether it says "die" is open (Ryan, 13 October; section 12).

Decided

Keywords are a list, at most three on an authored entry.

Upgrades may add more.

Decided

Ryan, 16 October: the keyword budget goes, since it got in the way of good ideas: "We need to build good gameplay, and then if our vocabulary is too big, trim the fat. Decided

Three rerolls a round, never onto the entry showing; a copy of it may come up.

These are not really dice, and a reroll that lands where it was reads as a bug, so the entry showing is skipped. Its copies are not (Ryan, 15 October): skipping them too was abusable, since a die of one key entry and copies of one filler sent every reroll from a filler onto the key, so a copy comes up at the odds its copies give it, and the entry's place on the die (its numeral) shows that the die moved. One exception, a design one-off (section 7): the Cassowary's Spin-Up spins its whole cylinder, itself included (Ryan, 19 October), since a revolver's spin may stop where it was. The player must be told; die size is a balance lever.

The screen

Decided

Minimal chrome.

Flat vector overlays in a HUD colour the player picks (green, amber, blue; never red, the enemy's). No unit names, rank labels, passives or help text on screen; enemy health shows.

Decided

One chip row under each unit.

A foe's intent (action and number, not target: Slice and Dice's model) and its statuses; a machine's statuses (every one, however long it lasts, so the Ammit's battle-long Overcharged reads there too, and in its codex with its count, kept for the battle: Ryan, 16 October; built that day) and the forecast's warnings, recomputed after every action, its gauges pulsing on one clock where they will change. Holding a unit draws its arrows; the eye button draws them all.

Decided

Numbed's chip has bracket corners, the mark of what nothing removes; a status chip has a box.

A Paradox chip shows its count.

Decided

Tap, hold, drag.

Tap a die to lock or unlock it, hold it for every entry and what applies now, drag it to spend. Hold a unit for its numbers and arrows; double-tap it for its codex, the base state between fights (the fight's effects live on the hold). A hold's readout opens above the thing held, clear of the thumb.

Decided

Every special, a boss's blow and a blow at every machine are announced on a plaque.

A JRPG message window in the foes' sixteen-bit register over their rows: the invasion at the interface layer, never HUD chrome. The replay holds for it (a second, a placeholder), once per move, so a storm of three strikes says it once. Plain blows and the player's components get none. A blow at every machine earns one (Claude's fill, 15 October, Ryan's to move): it reaches everyone at once, so it is named before it lands.

Decided

The HUD is a feed being analysed, not a print.

Rare, varied one-shot events (scans, telemetry, tears, signal drops, a die recomputing), never a rhythm and never competing with the pending-gauge pulse. Nothing else moves while the player thinks.

Decided

What the player sees of content.

The codex, the flavour quote, a passive's name and description, a module's description, a foe action's text. Internal text lives under design; planned drafts never show; tags show in the codex only. content/vocabulary.json defines every term and generates docs/vocabulary.md.

Decided

Few machines against a horde is the fantasy.

The answer to every foe striking each round is design (area damage, lure, mitigation, foes that misplay), never a cap.

Open

A gesture tutorial and an icon cheat sheet in the pause menu, the reroll rule first; a licensed icon set (game-icons.net, CC BY) for the glyphs; terrain in the skirmish's ground bands, if at all.

Mechs and the builder

A loadout is a real identity: MechWarrior's build depth, simplified, with no weight classes and no gated equipment. The words: a component is one piece of equipment and exactly one entry on the die; a hardpoint is a free slot; a chassis's fixed components are built in and use no hardpoint; a module sits inside the machine and is never spent.

Machines and the die

Decided

Chassis come in any shape and differ by plain numbers.

The silhouette is a terrain trait (section 4), so a team is picked for the map as well as the fight.

Decided

Four squad slots; a run starts with three machines and buys the fourth.

A real choice between splitting up and staying together.

Decided

The die is the equipment.

Fixed components plus hardpoints, one entry each. A small die is consistent; a big one carries more but shows each entry less often (a thin deck as a chassis stat). Fixed components are never removed or bought open, because restriction makes builds fun.

Decided

Hardpoints are untyped.

The die is already the slot limit (a shield entry costs a gun entry on the same roll), and a niche the player can leave is an invitation.

Pinned

What a hand holds: locked, or a hand that takes more.

Ryan, 12 October: a gauntlet on the Nemesis's hip cannon loses too much immersion; tabled, the fists back as the Nemesis's locked piece. The shape he meant, if it returns: a hand as a superset, never a restriction. A hardpoint on a hand takes anything a mount takes and also what only a hand can hold (a gauntlet, a sword, a handheld rifle, a shield on the arm); a mount takes no hand-held piece; nobody loses a build and a chassis with hands gains the fantasy. It pays only with enough chassis with hands: almost every Arsenal chassis, many of Aphelion's, few of Minerva's and Wainwright & Sutler's, none of Kestrel's. It also raised a word: most players read Melee as a sword (the tags row).

Decided

A chassis's design is its passive, its numbers and its fixed components.

The stock in its hardpoints is generic kit the player replaces, so a passive must work with generic kit, and no machine has one straight-line build.

Decided

Toughness is not slots.

A tank is tough through its numbers and passive, never through a locked component that is only a stat line (plating by another name); its locked component is what only it can do, and a locked shield that does something passes (the Armadillo's Lockdown Shutters, the Puffin's Backwash Ionizer). If a machine proves both unkillable and hard-hitting, the fix is its hull, not its slots.

Decided

One passive, one idea.

Nameable in a phrase (an upside and its cost count as one), at most three rule lines: a double-edged niche definer (the Sparrowhawk) or a plain multiplier fantasy.

Decided

A chassis's identity must open build space.

Its passive, numbers and locked components together, never a convenient hole filled or a nice-to-have. A passive that only feeds its own fixed components adds nothing: it might as well sit on the components. The interesting part is how the player gets there: the Nemesis banks rage on any hull damage, so overheating on purpose, or anything that hurts it, becomes a build. A map rule has its place as a passive, but on its own it is a weak identity. Ryan, 15 October, keeping two of the roster analysis's tests: a new chassis earns its place by a new read (a state nobody reads), a new product (a state nobody produces), a new price, or a change to what a kind of piece does, since niches can and should overlap across a larger roster and the test reads the mechanics (a mechanic is its pattern in its context, never one trait it shares, such as a random target: Ryan, 16 October, "if you say 'can't do this thing because that already kind of does it,' then you quickly run out of things"); and a passive aims to be fed by, and to feed, other makers' pieces, ideally two routes in and two payoffs, an aspiration and never a pass or fail.

Recommended

A passive multiplies or redirects what components already do.

A verb several components share belongs on them as a keyword (so overshield is one).

Decided

A passive may break a rule, as a one-off.

That is the point of a passive: the rule bends for one machine and holds for every other, so the player learns the rule once and the exception where it lives.

Decided

A weapon is spent on a foe; equipment on a machine.

A weapon need not deal damage. Equipment (a shield, a vent, a buff) says whom it reaches. A buff grants one of the machines' own buffs; a debuff on a foe stays a weapon.

Decided

A component is intact, cracked or destroyed, nothing finer, for the whole battle.

Decided

The codex shows a machine between fights; the hold shows the fight.

The codex has base numbers and battle-long rewards, never a combat effect.

Decided

Content stays a draft until Ryan signs it final.

design.stage says so; none is final yet.

One tag makes a state another tag reads: "beam weapons deal extra to burning foes" beside "missile weapons add a stack of burn" pulls toward mixing, on one die or across the team. A count every piece of the tag feeds, where nothing a tag grants feeds that tag (the gunship: each weapon fitted raises a number by its tag, Ryan's, section 7), is no lynchpin and passes.

Motion

Decided

Every machine has its own idle acts and its own movement, on both screens.

Four acts each, five for the Baboon and the Sparrowhawk. Foes stay painted pictures that breathe and lunge.

Decided

Machines are piloted, not animals.

An act is what a pilot does: looks round through the head, turrets or sensors, flexes limbs and weapons, test-charges a weapon, shuffles impatiently. Cockpits are fixed, except the Clydesdale's head; the Ammit's head is still by design.

Decided

Heavy and mechanical, with inertia.

MechWarrior or Star Wars, not Gundam or Evangelion. Every part chases its act on a servo slightly under critical damping, timed by the machine's heft; a blow is keyed as a slam that accelerates into the hit and stops dead.

Decided

Acts are loud and never in step.

Each moves several joints and the body at once, with glows hot enough to read across the map on a phone. A machine picks acts at random, never the same twice running, with long rests between.

Decided

A grounded machine stands still between acts.

Only a flyer rides a slow swell; a hovercraft balances like a platform on water, tipping in gusts, catching itself and settling with a heave.

Decided

Turrets turn slowly, not far, and pause before turning back.

At most 70 degrees from the bow, 35 degrees a second, and a hold of 0.6 seconds before reversing; lowforge refuses an act that breaks this. Under way a turret turns with the hull.

Decided

Walkers stomp and plod.

A planted foot never slides; the body vaults over near-straight legs and nods onto each foot, never rolling side to side; a walker turns at the waist and steps round to turn in place.

Decided

A move on the map is one smooth curve with real acceleration.

The machine turns onto its way, accelerates at its own rate, holds full speed through corners and eases to a stop; pace follows the move value, so a longer route takes longer. Drifters (the Dingo wildly, the Puffin tamer) nose in and step their tails out; the flyers and the Stingray bank; every other machine follows its heading. Packs keep quick straight steps.

Decided

An act may name the places it plays.

Travel that leaves the hex (the Sparrowhawk's loop, circle and figure eight, the Dingo's strafe) plays on the map only.

Balance

Decided

Balance is measured by a ladder of bots and read as bands.

Random play (never rerolls, spends anywhere legal) beating a pack means too easy; greedy play (the best one-round trade on the player's own forecast) losing means a wall; the rungs landing together mark a cell with no decision; personas sit on top. tools/balance.gd runs every chassis alone, the starting team and each maker's first three against every pack, boss and domain into docs/balance.md. Pick share is the component signal. Play tests decide feel; no win rate is chased to a decimal. What a run can say (13 October): one cell near 50% carries a band of about 18 points at 30 seeds, 14 at 50 and 7 at 200, and a row mean over twenty packs about 4, so a cell is read at 200 seeds or as a band, a quick run is a direction and a row that cites one says so, and every run a decision cites goes in docs/measurements.md, dated with its seeds, where the row keeps the reason and the exit. The greedy bot values only what it knows: a verdict on a mechanic it cannot value (a sentence, a doubling, a turn of the ranks, a wound) is a verdict on the bot, and says so.

Decided

A component is balanced against the components of its tier; a chassis alone is a diagnostic.

Ryan, 12 October: chassis serve different roles, so a solo win rate is no main indicator, and a weapon is never balanced because a lone Prairie Dog or Sparrowhawk loses four on one, a fight it should lose or win only at a real cost in hull. The measures that matter: a component against its tier's others in the same hardpoint of the same machines (its value and its pick share), the team rows, and whole runs. One machine that warps its kit never dictates the whole roster of components (Ryan, 13 October, of the gunship).

The roster is widened so compositions can be read.

If every machine is a solo carry, a battle with four is trivial, so the roster needs machines that are only good together.

Decided
