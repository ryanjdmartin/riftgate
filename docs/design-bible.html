1

Concept and tone

Three nested loops. The campaign is the roguelike run: a sequence of battles, lost for good if the city gate falls. Each battle is a hex-grid battlefield with the gate on the south edge and a rift on the north edge, won by reaching and killing the boss that powers the rift. Each skirmish is a dice-driven fight triggered when mechs and enemy packs end a movement phase adjacent, and it is where the game's depth lives.

Decided

Doom and Space Marine energy.

Over-the-top badassery, played straight. Less overt irony than first planned: propaganda is fine, manufacturer advertisements and Borderlands-style gags are not, because this is the end of civilization. Never edgy, never grimdark fantasy. The city falling is never funny.

Decided

Mechs are grounded military mecha.

Mechwarrior lineage: rugged, brutal, heavy, built from parts that look serviced rather than cobbled together. Not Evangelion. In-world makers exist as provenance and a recognisable livery, each with a voice of its own (the voice field in content), never as in-your-face advertising: that tips the tone from grimdark into black comedy (Ryan, 5 October, loosening the earlier "maintenance-manual voice" wording, which was too specific; a maker telling the commander they are its best customer is fine, and simpler reads better).

Decided

The Council for the Ultimate Liberation of Terra, and the world it runs.

Decided 16 and 17 September. Humanity's remaining cities are governed by the Council, formally the Council for the Ultimate Liberation of Terra; its acronym is never written or spoken in the game, however obvious, and the joke belongs to the player who notices. Plainly a martial-law regime pretending it is not, it believes its own cause even as it profits from it, which is how it has kept power: the populace has real believers, and the propaganda is sincere. It rewards innovation that helps the fight, which sets the manufacturers competing for its favour, and it keeps its own private military with the best hardware. The register is a god-emperor's court, not a junta; the tone is not the end of the world but "let them try, nobody stands against the march of human progress", said while everything quietly goes to shit. Closer to Pacific Rim than to BattleTech. Open on purpose, as room to expand: whether this is the last bastion of mankind at all, whether other colonies are doing fine and looking away, and whether the Council had a hand in the event that opened the rifts, its rise to central authority being the point of the play, which may become the campaign's spine. Retired: "houses" for the makers, and MARCH as the council's name. Lore is Ryan's; the fantasy field under design is where he keeps inspiration and homages, never shown.

Decided

The player commands for CARE: the underpaid true believers, not the politicians.

Decided 16 and 17 September. The Council pays for the city's defence through CARE, the Civil Auxiliary for Rift Emergencies: an arms-length body with the feel of an independent agency or a contracted corporation, named by the Council in its own condescension (civil, not military; auxiliary, not principal; the apocalypse filed as an emergency) and deliberately lame beside the aptitude of its people. If Ryan decides they named themselves, Agency replaces Auxiliary and the letters stay. The player came up through its ranks to Battlefield Commander: the general, never a politician. CARE's people are the war's true believers, who know why they fight and it is not the Council's reasons: practical, pragmatic, determined, genuine badasses with a rebellious streak, the lone-gunslinger action-hero register played straight and never winked at. The Council expects results and takes the credit, none of the responsibility. Its own R&D house, the Arsenal, sells CARE a trickle of its technology, the good stuff but never the best, and is the one research path the player cannot force: higher tiers come only as the campaign turns dire enough for the Council to loosen its grip. Holes to fill: what the Council does when the player fails, whether the contract can be lost, how the credit-taking turns into plot.

Decided

Enemies are old Final Fantasy monsters.

Detailed, over-the-top high fantasy, contrasted against the gritty realism of the player units. One genre invading another is the dichotomy the game wants, and it replaces most of the verbal irony.

Decided

Sound: industrial, grooving, grounded; two palettes, one for each world; the rules the listens left.

Ryan, 22 September, the direction for all audio. Music is a heavy industrial core with driving flow and tempo, carried by the beat: not too fast, more grooving than heavy even in the battle themes, musically complex and unique. Inspirations: Tool, Nine Inch Nails, The Omnific, Teho, Hozho. The map and the eventual hangar are more subdued than the skirmish but still groove. Effects follow the same rule: grounded and heavy, weight and machinery, nothing that sounds like a space opera. The reason it matters: the more grounded the sound, the more silliness the game can get away with elsewhere, so the sound carries the seriousness the tone rows above ask for. Everything is synthesised from written recipes by tools/soundforge/forge.py, the way lowforge builds the models; the forge on this computer is the instrument, and there are no real recordings to commission (closed 29 September). The recipes are the record of what each track is; this row keeps the rules.

Two palettes (decided 23 and 24 September from A/B sketches). The human side, the fight's: real-ish drums, a chugged guitar, a driven bass, electric piano, a synth lead, a metal chime, and the drone Ryan singled out as an element for every human track (the sweep pad's saws an octave down, held on one chord, the filter three quarters open and nearly still). The rift side, the map's: an orchestra (bowed contrabass and cellos, violins, horns, a harp, timpani, an orchestral bass drum), a choir on "oo", bells with a long echo, backwards swells, and dissonance written in (tritones, sevenths and ninths, chords changing at different times). The bells and the choir play on both sides for cohesion. The fight is the seam where both play at once; which palette goes to which screen is a one-line swap.

Rules for the music. Never a build, a bass drop or a speed snare: a section changes by what plays, not by a roll into it. Sections stay long enough to engage (eight bars; a track runs 70 to 144 bars) and complexity comes from harmony and counterpoint, never fills. The kick keeps its places for the whole track, so any play with time signatures (the 7/8 closes, a 6/8 triplet bar, a 3/4 stretch) happens over a bar of the same length with the beat steady under it; kick rolls are fine, snare rolls are not. Solos in the Dream Theater way; drums slap wherever they are a big part of a human track; a bass takes a back seat or kicks up a notch, never the middle. Bosses are epic, not intense, so a quiet build is a feature. A pattern whose length does not divide the bar walks round it by design (the seven-step chug), so lines are bar-length unless the walk is wanted; a stab's length is in steps, not seconds. Every drum part and voice draws its own seeded stream, so an unedited section never changes; crashes are placed by hand at section doors, never at the top. Tracks are levelled by body (mean RMS, pinned where each was judged), the mix sits under the saturator's knee with headroom (the human tracks' crackle was the master flattening a busier section, 30 September), and a section adds instruments rather than loudness: a volume knob is what a device is for. A loop resumes where it left when its screen comes back; a new battle starts the map's set from the top. When a note can be read two ways, ask before building.

The tracks (titles in the next row; Sound.TRACKS, SETS, LOOP_FROM). Hold The Line (human A, 104): the riff in three stages, lifts, the seven, the keys with Ryan's own bridge melody, a drum solo, a three-set climax at one loudness, two breaths; wraps to bar nine; locked. Night Shift (human B, 94): the human calm, four on the floor and industrial, the piano break its identity. Full Bore (human C): the dense prog-metal track, paired basses an octave apart in the Omnific construction, a section in seven. The Far Bank (rift A, 88): the rift theme, groove, lift, strings, triplet turn, interlude with 7/8 closes, a thirty-two-bar climax of rotating swells, a closer; the third act's boss theme for The Chorus, wrapping to bar nine; locked. Low Ground (rift B, 90): the rift calm, a ground bass descending A G F E under six variations, 6/8 at the 4/4 bar's length. The Crossing (rift C, 126): E Phrygian, a plucked ostinato in three-three-two over timpani, Ryan's kick-chug-snare engine as one section, a 3/4 stretch, extended to a second pass through its shape. The map's set cycles Low Ground then Night Shift, each loop crossing into the next over two seconds (equal-power on linear gain; a decibel fade gapped); the skirmish's set picks one of Hold The Line, The Crossing and Full Bore per visit and never crossfades mid-fight (the player's skip is exempt). Sound.TEMPO follows the map's track, since the rift's vortex steps on the beat.

Boss themes (25 September): one per family in the acts' order, since a boss skirmish is a long fight and an act's identity. The theme starts from its top the moment the boss is contacted on the battlefield, so its intro plays under the reveal and the fight arrives in the groove; a family without one takes the skirmish's pick. The Risen's would grow from Low Ground's ground bass (a dirge that returns after every breakdown), The Infernal Horde's from The Crossing's brass and timpani (telegraphed stabs with a bar of air before each), melodies proposed in chat first. Open: whether the final battle's source rift has a boss of its own with The Far Bank as its track, leaving The Chorus a theme from a seed (Ryan's lean, 29 September: probably).

The phone (26 to 30 September, from A/B sketches on a listening page; the phone-speaker model is high-passed at 220 Hz with a presence bump, rolled off at 9 kHz). One master for both palettes: a gentle tilt (1.5) with a narrow presence lift at 1 to 2 kHz, no hard clip anywhere. The driven bass is two asymmetric saturation stages whose drive follows the string, through a bass cabinet (voice 12, "hands down"); the backing guitar lost its clip; the kick is a metal kick at the middle size; the toms keep their body and are hit hard (tightening them made them hollow); the crash is soft and closed above 8 kHz; the soft sub carries a speaker shadow. The rift tracks sit half their phone gap above where they were (The Far Bank -17.9 dB, The Crossing -17.7; a quarter was wrong in the game). Weight lives in the 150 to 800 Hz band a speaker can play, the sub a bonus for headphones. The track levels stay until Ryan says otherwise. A speakers/headphones setting (shipped levels for headphones, an offset per track and a bus EQ on the speaker profile) was the planned answer to what one file cannot do for both; dropped 2 October (Ryan): the one master balances for both for now. Ryan judges music on his phone, so a change is heard as an A/B before it lands; a sketch never overwrites a shipped track, a pick lands in the forge, then build and one --import. His melodies come through the Melody Desk (30 September onward): the session interprets what he sends, it is not drop-in.

Rules for the effects. Nothing sweeps in pitch, anywhere: a glide reads as the arcade (the laser's "pew", the shield's rise, the kill's wind-down were all glides and all went), so every sound is an impact, a mechanism or a burst. Damped knocks that die in tens of milliseconds, never a bell or an anvil left to ring; a little inharmonic metal at low gain is allowed. The interface is a 90s computer game's cockpit (Ryan, 2 October: StarCraft 1, MechWarrior 2, Descent; retro-future, not retro; the 24 September "future-digital, not retro" line is retired), deliberate and with intent, not understated: grounded computer gibberish, irony not parody, weighty. That era was sampled synths rather than chips, so a short glide into a note is allowed in the console alone, and only there; the dice belong to the interface, and the seated die has a voice (a low E3) since the console is deliberate now. Combat and the map are over-the-top weighty, Doom's punch, for both sides: a machine's blow follows its weapon (energy a flat laser, kinetic gunfire with the cannon on a crit, indirect the cannon, melee a slash with a slam on a crit), a foe's blow a slash or a slam, a kill and the gate struck explosions. The mix is a loudness per sound in the forge; big moments (contact, the dive, the cut, the gate, a kill, the outcome) dip the music for a moment. The display's scan and idle stay silent: a constant sound on a phone is fatigue.

The effects' pass (30 September onward, on the listening page; nothing shipped; each pick lands as a recipe in forge.py with a small hook the page names). A new set from the UI, the lore, the music and both sides' unit design, sorted into the screen's registers: the console (green), the iron (a blow by the weapon's shape, a hit, a block that glances, a hit on a standing shield, the shield breaking, a kill, each in the struck machine's metal, footfalls by silhouette), the rift (violet: each family its own blow, heavy blow and arrival, a boss reveal), the red (contact, the gate, victory and defeat tuned to E). The third cut, more oomph (Ryan, 1 October, night; built 2 October): a lot of the set did not read on the phone, since a speaker plays nothing under about 200 Hz, so a blow's weight is its own mids saturated and added back in the 250 to 2000 Hz band (heft), a boom tail behind every blast, and length: each die spent is deliberate and no rapid fire comes in a row, so every blow is drawn out (the barrel a report with the breech after, the rack's salvo in flight most of a second, the blade over a second with three bites, the laser charging then held, the plasma winding up, the deflector ringing a second and a half, the coil holding its charge as a hum, the kill near three seconds with three blasts) and should feel rewarding and powerful; shields and buffs the same; over-the-top Doom energy, not comical irony. Every cut is measured through the phone model against the shipped sound, not guessed. The Pac-Man ruling stands for every blow: nothing glides in pitch; a filter and a loudness may move. The sketches are written over the shipped levels (console +4 dB, blows +2, shields and buffs +2, kills +1), said on the page for Ryan to pull back. Ryan, 2 October: the weapons are acceptable for now and parked. The blunt impact (decided 2 October): one sound for the Breaching Plow and the Gravity Knuckles alike, since these are multi-ton war machines and a blunt blow is a car hitting a wall (the mass coming, the crumple on the thud, glass, the boom, a rattle settling, a little of the wet for what it hit). Content carries a label for the effects (decided 2 October): a sound word on a component, from a vocabulary the validator checks (barrel, twin_barrel, rack, blade, blunt, laser, plasma, deflector, coil), named only where shape and face cannot derive it, so the plow says blunt and nothing else says anything. The label takes a list, and a list means layers: the first word is the blow, every word after it rides on the landing, built in the forge as one recipe timed to the blow; the Gravity Knuckles say ["blunt", "plasma"]. The list represents game effects, never gameplay properties: it is not tied to keywords or tags and does not feed them, so a filing tag can never change a sound. A stretch goal, not now: a modification that adds burn or shock to a component adding its layer. The voice (2 October): a formant synthesiser (tools/soundforge/speech.py, crude by design) says eight console lines as a Betty and as a VEGA (Ryan's references: MechWarrior 2's Bitching Betty, StarCraft's Adjutant, Doom's VEGA), so whether CARE's console should speak at all, and as whom, is heard before the words are made good; Ryan's first listen found them too faint under noise, cracks and pops to judge: measured, the glottal source was an impulse train (every period a click) and the vowels sat 10 to 17 dB under the consonant bursts; both are fixed (a smooth flow pulse, the voiced part levelled) and the lines play at the contact's level rather than a console tone's, so the question stays open and judgeable. The console derived again (Ryan, 2 October: the rounds since 30 September were incremental adjustments, not a measurable shift in direction; derive it from the UI design, the music's atmosphere and the direction notes, without the existing sounds or the existing choice of which events sound; on the listening page, proposed, in its second cut). What the UI is: a feed being analysed whose motion already has four verbs on screen (brackets collapse onto a thing over a third of a second; digits scramble at 45 ms a frame and settle; text types at 12 ms a character; things land in 0.35 s); every button plays a tap on press, and MENU, END TURN, RESOLVE and ENGAGE are blanked in code because the action plays its own sound (the bible's "buttons never take an effect" is the feed row's visual rule, that the glitch one-shots never land on buttons, and says nothing about sound; a first cut misread it, Ryan caught it). Ryan's notes on the first cut, each a rule: the feed is digital, so no static anywhere (it quantises, holds, drops frames and resolves; it never hisses); no work verb of clicky ticks with no story; no knock, since the human register has a digital softness beside its heavy gain and only the weapons hit hard, the rest of the audio wants nuance, complexity and depth; and the console wants an instrument of its own that sits in the music's atmosphere without drawing on its instruments, since the skirmish rotates three tracks and the map two more and the effects suite must fit all of them. So the console is an FM terminal: that era's cockpits and consoles were FM synths and sample cards, retro-future digital, and FM's glassy, faintly metallic tone is a timbre nothing in the music uses, so the separation is in kind rather than level. Its motions are digital: acquire is a tone that starts coarse (a high modulation index, three bits of depth) and resolves to clean as the brackets land, the pitch fixed from the first sample; a thing let go is the same run backward; confirm is a soft bloom (a low body with a brief FM burst on its front, no hit) under a dyad sustained and closing under a low-pass, small for a lock, medium for a die spent, large as three stepped notes E, B, E for RESOLVE and END TURN; refuse is F falling to E, dry; touch is a soft FM tick on every button; and the scramble is digits flipping as pitch, quantised notes stepping at the display's own frame rate and landing on the note. The round opening, every intent acquired at once, is the console's signature line, once a round. Pitches E and B, which sit under all five tracks, at E5 and B5 for the speaker with an E4 or E3 body for headphones; every file levelled by what the phone model plays of it; the music under the sequences 4 dB down (Ryan: too loud against the UI). The 25 September note keeping the pitches at G3 to E4 is set aside on purpose, since those pitches do not exist on the speaker; slow fronts and the resolving motion, rather than beeps, are the guard against the arcade. Gaps the code has, which a pick adds a hook for: no seated-over-a-target state in a drag, nothing animates when a round deals its dice, an invalid drop and a disabled press are silent, and the codex, confirm panel, hold readout and menu appear with no transition. The third cut, the operations channel (Ryan, 2 October, on the FM cut: the direction holds and it reads well over the music, but it is an arcade cabinet, not the grimdark future; his thought experiment, a war-game RTS, and his release of this cut from the no-static note, which had been a preference between two cuts rather than a direction). An RTS interface is an operations channel, not a cockpit: its sounds are traffic, not feedback; an order goes out and the unit answers, and the field reports back. So the FM terminal stays the instrument and runs through a digital comm channel (a hard band from 300 Hz to 4 kHz, compression, codec grain, a squelch as each transmission opens and closes; the squelch is a transmission's edge, not a hiss under anything). The key is the channel opening and says nothing; a dismiss is it closing. A confirm is an order sent and, a beat later, the machine's acknowledgement in its maker's colour (MAKER_ACK: Minerva plain, Kestrel high and quick, Wainwright low and a touch sour, Aphelion glassy, the Arsenal not quite in tune), which puts the voice question where it belongs, the line answering rather than a narrator, and gives the makers a second place to differ. The commit sends E, B, E as one transmission and every machine answers in turn (all units committed); the round opening is the field reporting; pitch is spent on those two lines a round and nearly nowhere else. The acknowledgement's beat after the order is a timing hook the game does not have. Ryan on the channel: war room is the right call and the digital fracturing works, but the execution is arcade and early Flash, too fast and chirpy, the high-gain sounds piercing on the speaker; humanity's technology is brutal, military, utilitarian; push it grimdark with a fresh pass, not an iteration on the FM set. The fourth cut, the war room (tools/soundforge/warroom_sketches.py): nothing is a chirp and nothing is fast. The console is a military data system heard through the war room's monitors (a steep roll-off above 3 kHz, a floor at 180 Hz, saturation): a low carrier at the bottom of what a speaker plays with a slow warble on its loudness and a formant moving over it, a machine's hum that almost talks, the pitch fixed and the mouth moving; a vocoded voice, a buzz through two moving formants, one to three syllables and no words, so humanity's machines answer in grunts and each maker has a vowel (MAKER_VOWEL); and the breakup, buffer stutters, dropouts and the bit depth collapsing and recovering, the only quick thing in the set. Piercing is measured, not guessed: the 2 to 8 kHz band against the 300 Hz to 2 kHz body, held at least ten decibels under (the FM and channel cuts had sat 8 to 10 under; the war room sits 12 to 22). The grammar stays the war room's: a dull switch, the carrier coming up out of breakup, a blurt sent and the grunt a beat after, the commit as a thrum building through stutter into a three-syllable statement with every machine answering, the round opening as breakup resolving into a held two-tone. All four cuts sit on the page, the war room first. Rendered by tools/soundforge/fx_sketches.py beside the shipped files, as AAC by afconvert on the Mac or ffmpeg at 256 kbps elsewhere (its encoder smears a transient's front at 128).

Ryan, 25 September: give the tracks proper names, then in-game music controls: a ticker on the top bar, and music controls in the pause menu, on the page itself rather than a settings item, showing the current track with next and previous. Rift A still has no screen, so the controls never reach it. The skirmish's rule that a fight never crossfades mid-fight is the game's rule for itself; the player's hand is exempt.

Decided

Map tokens are chibi; combat units are full size.

Chibi 24x28 map tokens that make no attempt at scale, and full-size units in the skirmish, in the old Final Fantasy overworld-versus-battle split. Heavies, elites and bosses overflow their hex on the map, anchored at the hex base, drawn south over north, with the hex as the tap target. Resolved 21 September: the map draws the same models as the skirmish, and the chibi read is now the enemy's alone, since a pack on the map is a flat picture of the other world.

Decided

Few, non-disposable war machines.

Each player unit is an apocalyptic war machine that takes on many foes over many battles. No unit micromanagement. Enemy hexes are packs, so every skirmish is a few machines against a horde.

Decided

Learnable in play.

The game is not simple by nature, but a player must never need a wiki, a build planner, or DPS math. Tooltips and an in-game cheat sheet are fine. External tools are a design failure.

Decided 16 September from the art lab (sections 8 to 10). The human side is real geometry in the engine. Target quality: an early-to-mid-2000s RTS model, not a painting. Everything human follows: gate, defences, the hangar. What it buys: every facing free, animation, livery swaps, damage states as hidden parts, no image model near a mech. Every mechanic proposed for the skirmish is held to these. They exist because the designer's stated tendency is to overcomplicate for complexity's sake, and the goal is elegant fun.

Decided

A round takes about thirty seconds

Decided

The build is visible every round.

You watch your components act. They are never just stat bonuses.

Decided

Two sources of randomness, both the player's, both visible.

The die decides what you can do; the damage roll decides how well. Kinetic faces roll a range and may crit; energy faces are static. No hidden hit rolls. Enemies never roll: their intents are telegraphed as exact numbers, as in Griftlands.

Decided

Rerolls before or after attacking, faces that raise the floor or deal the average, execute and finish effects that guarantee lethal, and visible enemy health. Variance is a tactical choice to mitigate, never a punishment.

Decided

Every pack poses a question,

such as finishing the resurrectors or interrupting the big hit, rather than being a damage race with different art.

Decided

Decided

A bad roll with an interesting answer is where the fun lives. The design removes helplessness, not luck.

Decided

Revised 5 October (it was "zero to one chassis passive"; retired when chassis modules took rule text, section 10). Campaign loop

Decided

The campaign is the run.

Battles are its floors; a battle ends when its rift is closed on the boss that powers it, so every battle has a boss fight. Permadeath applies to the campaign: when the capital's gate falls the run is over and everything in it (salvage, blueprints, modules, the roster) is lost. Between campaigns only the pool, the codex, the difficulty tiers and a sponsorship carry (section 10). "Run" is the campaign; a battle is a battle.

Decided

Five cities, five lives: gate integrity is the campaign meter.

Ryan, 5 October. The capital sits at the centre of the mission map with the four cities around it; each hosts a maker, the capital the Council and the Arsenal, and each sits under its own field. A city's gate carries its integrity between battles. The mission select is a choice of front, never city management: each front shows its faction, its ground type and its tier of the Convergence as words ("the Risen, rubble field, tier II"), its formation fuzzy until scouted, and how much its gate will bleed if it is ignored this turn. A gate at zero falls unfought; the forced last stand of 5 October is retired (6 October, the warning row below). A city falls only by losing a battle there (Ryan's rule), and it costs three things, none of them the player's kit: that battle's salvage, its maker's new blueprints and research (refit and repair stay with CARE, who maintain what the player already owns), and its rift stays open. The capital cannot be attacked before the finale and its gate never bleeds on its own.

Decided

The capital's gate is the life bar, and the loss is real at every battle.

Ryan, 5 October, after Claude's Into the Breach reading (grid power: one bar across the islands, drained by every loss, the run over at zero). Every fallen city's horde is loose and drains the capital's gate every turn from then on, drawn on the mission map as a march on the centre, structure and motion rather than a colour. At zero the Council's field fails and the run is lost, mid-campaign if the player let it happen. The finale is fought at the capital on whatever gate it has left, so the one priced repair in the game, the capital's gate, is a decision with two faces: survive now and start the finale stronger, or spend on the team instead. Retired: "if the gate falls, everything is lost" as a per-battle rule; a lost battle loses a city, not the run.

Decided

Track B: the finale is inevitable; faction finals are prizes.

Ryan, 5 October, over the Mega Man alternative (beat every faction's final to draw out the true boss; too much control, and it needs no clock). Every turn raises the Convergence (named by Ryan, 7 October; it was "the rift threat"); at its threshold the true final boss arrives at the capital, sooner if cities have fallen. Defeating enough of a faction's bosses draws out that faction's final; beating it thins that faction from the spawns and from the finale, so each one won narrows what the finale can throw. The final is a tougher fight against a tougher boss, the faction's leader (Ryan, 7 October; each leader is content, proposed in chat first). What draws it out is a bar per faction on the mission map named Anathema (Ryan, 7 October): kill enough of a faction and it names the commander anathema, and its leader comes; what fills it is open, and the recommendation (7 October, after Ryan pointed back at section 4's pacing row) is that row's own calculation: every kill of that faction feeds it by how dangerous the foe was, a ghoul pack a sliver and a boss a large share, the same weighted tally as the Rupture meter, with farming answered the way that row answers it (escalation makes a lot of farming deadly, the backstop ends it), never by a cap. The first pass counts three of the faction's bosses beaten as a placeholder. The campaign's length must leave room for one or two finals as the realistic goal and all three as a feat. This is the in-battle pacing row (section 4) at campaign scale: battles raise the Convergence as kills fill the bar, the finals are the Fractures past the Rupture, and the finale is the backstop.

Decided

Difficulty by smarter foes and nastier compositions, never by stats.

Ryan, 5 October. As the player's options grow the enemy answers with aims, conditions, behaviours, formations with a back rank, elites and pack count, not bigger numbers, so the game is never a numbers race: upgrades buy adaptability against questions, not damage against hull. Each battle has one lead faction and at most one guest faction, so the pre-battle build question stays legible, until the last tier, whose signature is anything from the roster. Packs grow in count, not in per-foe stats.

Decided

Between battles: the hangar, where nothing is a chore.

Ryan, 5 October, replacing "repair, salvage, research". Hull repairs free; cracked components repair free; a destroyed hardpoint component is gone (section 7); a destroyed fixed component is rebuilt free, being part of the chassis and so of its blueprint; a wrecked machine comes back whole in the shop, out for the next battle, its cracked and destroyed components lost and its intact ones recovered; refit is instant, because a refit timer would tax the one action that is the fun. Resources go on upgrades, builds and the capital's gate, never on routine repair; the one exception is rushing a wreck out of the shop for salvage (Ryan, 6 October: the rush was always a choice under discussion, never a hard rule); repair as a priced chore and the "fighting shape" question are retired with it. A machine is in the shop or ready, dressed or bare: two words, no numbers. The shop timer is what makes a roster larger than the squad worth having.

Decided

A front is a rift, and its faction is rolled when the rift opens.

Ryan, 6 October, refusing a faction fixed to a city: a favourable pairing of faction and maker would solve the map, and a city whose faction had been thinned by its final would become immune. Rifts open at the standing cities up to a count that rises with the Convergence's tier (placeholders: two at the start, one more a tier); each rift rolls its lead from the factions not yet thinned and, from the guest tier on, a guest from any other faction, since a beaten faction leads no new rift but may still guest. A rift stays until a battle there closes it, so a besieged city keeps the same horde turn to turn and a city with no rift does not bleed. A city whose rift was just closed opens no new one for a turn, so a won city is not besieged again at once. Built the same day in sim/campaign.gd; the guest joins the rift's spawn cycle, alternating with the lead's, while the formation on the field at the start is the lead's alone.

Decided

No forced last stand: the front card warns, and a won battle lifts the siege.

Ryan, 6 October, asking whether forcing the last stand added anything; it did not: an ignored front is the hardest front at a quarter gate, forcing it takes away the one choice the mission map offers, and it contradicted "a city falls only by losing a battle there". Every open rift the player did not fight at bleeds its city's gate each turn; the front card shows the turns left (Campaign.turns_to_fall); a gate at zero falls unfought, and a battle fought there before then is fought on what the gate has left. A won battle restores the city's gate to full, free: the siege is lifted, a reward of the win and never a purchase, so the city gates are never a tax and the capital's gate stays the one priced repair (the life-bar row above). Without it every city would be on a one-way clock and twelve battles would be triage. Gate integrity is the only number that tracks a city's health (Ryan). Full restore confirmed (Ryan, 7 October), and kept as a lever for the higher difficulty tiers: a tier may restore only part of the gate, or by the margin of the win.

Decided

A team wipe is a graded loss, not a fallen city.

Ryan, 6 October: the risk of being wiped in battle one is a difficulty-curve question, a roguelike's bar test, and stays; what had to be decided is what a wipe costs, since the choices after it hang on it. When every machine is wrecked the horde walks on unopposed and strikes the gate for a few phases (placeholder six). At zero the city falls like any lost battle; otherwise it stands wounded, its rift still open and the same horde there next turn. The salvage from the skirmishes won was in the city: kept when it holds, lost when it falls. No research token, no offer, and every machine in the shop. The player then chooses between passing a turn for the shop, rushing a wreck out for salvage, and building from a held blueprint once building exists. So the gate a city carries into a battle is what buys its survival after a wipe, which is what the meter is for.

Recommended

A passed turn is a turn; the finale, once due, is the only front.

Claude, 6 October, built in the first pass. The Convergence rises on every turn, a battle or a pass, so waiting for the shop never stops the clock. Once the finale is due the capital is the only front and the other open rifts go on bleeding their cities; a finale wiped with the capital still standing is fought again next turn. The starting team stays open: a draft is wanted (Ryan, 6 October) and nothing about it is designed, so a run starts from a team handed to it, the content's starting team for now, with a little salvage (placeholder: one rush).

Recommended

Twelve battles as the working length.

Claude, 5 October, so the other numbers have something to be measured against: at ten to twelve minutes a battle (nothing has timed one yet) that is two to two and a half hours of fighting before hangar time, which Ryan wants long enough for the salvage, build and tune loop to breathe (this is a mech game; the draw is the loadout). A one-battle shop timer is then a twelfth of the run. Three acts, one a faction, are retired: acts are tiers of the Convergence and factions are fronts. An endless mode can come later.

Nothing of the in-battle version was ever built, and it cannot be: a pack's zone and behaviour come from its type, so hiding the type either leaks it through the drawn ring or breaks the exact preview, which is the game's spine. Battlefield layer

The battlefield decides where, when, and with whom you fight. It is deliberately the layer with positioning, so the skirmish can have none.

Map

Decided

Hex grid, gate south, rift north.

Procedurally generated terrain: water, impassable mountains, and more. One mech per hex. One enemy pack per hex. City automated defenses stand on the field near the gate and can be targeted.

Decided

The battlefield may be taller than one screen.

Seven wide stays the rule for thumbs. Height may exceed a screen and scroll vertically, with the camera following the active unit. Pointy-top hexes make defensive lines straight horizontal rows and force advancing enemies to zigzag. With cube coordinates in the logic, orientation stays a rendering choice.

Decided

Multi-hex objects.

A massive boss can occupy three hexes in a triangle. The gate and the rift are wider than one hex. Packs and mechs stay single-hex. Epic scale without player overhead. Simulation cost: units gain a footprint, and blocking, adjacency, and pathing treat every footprint hex as occupied.

Decided

Maps come in named types, and the type's name is the intel.

Ryan, 4 October: what is the optimal tile distribution, and could the player see a map's composition before choosing units? Measured first on 200 generated maps (tools/map_survey.gd): the generator made one map with cosmetic variation (open 48%, rough 22%, ruins 11%, water 6%, crags 13%, the ranges narrow), and that composition bit nobody on the trip north: a walker reached the rift in six phases on every map, tracks and a flyer the same, and moving the ruins share from 6% to 17% cost wheels half a phase. Terrain showed only in reach, the share of the field a machine can stand on after two phases (wheels from 17% to 89% by map; crags the one ground that bites every ground machine), and in where the horde's crossing fell. So the mean does not matter, the spread does: intel is worth reading only if maps differ enough that the right team changes, and "ruins 17%" would be arithmetic the player has to interpret. Built the same day on Ryan's go, every number a placeholder (BattleMap.KINDS): five map types as presets of the generator's knobs, each still a seeded noise map so no two are alike. Mixed ground is the generator as it was. Open steppe: open 73%, little of anything else, a lake; wheels and the horde fly, blocking is hard, fights come to the gate. Rubble field: a belt of ruins two or three rows deep across the whole field, since a patch leaves a lane (measured: clusters at 27% of the field slowed nobody), with the road the one open lane through it; off the road, tracks, wheels and hover stop at it, the walker's map, and the horde does not care. Badlands: crags 19% in ridges; tight crossings, blocking easy, the flyer's map, and the guarantee opens about four hexes a map to keep the horde's three-wide crossing. Flooded: a thick river and a lake with puddles, water 15%; walkers and wheels stop, hover and tracks ford, the horde funnels to the fords. The type's name is what the player reads ("RUBBLE FIELD"; the map's header carries it, the terrain codex says each ground's word by way of moving), never a share. Where the intel sits: beside the faction at the campaign's battle choice, which does not exist yet; faction already makes the path choice (section 9), and ground is the second axis of the same decision. The survey compares the types side by side; playable is guaranteed by construction and tested (the crossing three wide, every way of moving with a path to the rift), while difficult and fun need the map played: Ryan's play per type first, a map rung on the policy ladder once the pacing pieces exist. Every type must pass "mismatch costs time, never ability": a flooded map with an all-wheeled team is winnable, just slower. Measured after the build (100 seeds a type): the types tell apart by composition (open 48, 73, 53, 49 and 55 percent; ruins 19% on the rubble field, crags 19% in the badlands, water 15% flooded) and every map keeps the three-wide crossing, but the trip north by its best lane hardly moves for anyone (a walker six phases on every type; wheels 3.0 on the steppe, 3.4 flooded, 3.7 to 3.9 elsewhere), and reach after two phases moves only for wheels (90% on the steppe, 60% in the badlands) and hover (52% flooded, 37% badlands). So the types are legible before they are decisive, and the decisive part is tactical. The type list and the shares are Claude's, Ryan's to move; which types the campaign offers, and how often, is a design line.

Decided

Decided

Each way of moving calls each ground fast, normal or slow, and a slow hex ends the move.

Ryan, 2 October, in the discussion that replaced the day's price table. The aim: nothing the player weighs as two numbers at once, and every way of moving with a ground it is good on and a ground it is bad on. The price table could not do that. Hexes a phase are move divided by price, so a bigger move buys back any penalty up to the same ratio: under the old table a wheeled 60 reached every hex a walker 30 reached from every start on 200 generated maps (Ryan's hole: a Dingo at 60 crosses as much rough as a walker at 30 and still rips through plains). The one rule no move can buy through is a stop, so slow ground is one hex a phase whatever the move.

The words. Moves stay in tens. Fast costs 5 a hex (twice the ground), normal costs 10, slow can always be entered with any move left, even 5, and entering it ends the move, even with 40 left. Order matters, and that is accepted: a slow step last costs nothing, a slow step first costs the phase. Leftover move is stranded otherwise (5 left buys no normal hex, but it buys a slow one).

The grounds are four: open (plain and road, the same ground; road is art only, Ryan, 2 October, and the art may lean on that), rough (forest, scrub), ruins (rubble) and water; mountains and crags are walls to all but flyers. Pacing: a race to the rift

Decided

No in-field repair. The battle keeps pushing forward.

It must feel like a race to close the rift before the city is overwhelmed, not a back-and-forth war simulation. Slow-and-steady versus blitz is the player's choice, including leaving a defender at the gate.

Decided

Damage sticks. Mitigation is a real concern.

Hull never repairs during a battle. Mitigation comes from recharging shields and from defensive tactics, not from healing.

Decided

A standing shield blocks the blow whole; in a fight, shield comes only from components.

Decided 18 September, after discussion. However small the shield and however big the blow, nothing bleeds through to the hull; the blow still costs the shield its damage. That makes a ten-point shield a real decision every round a big hit is telegraphed, gives glass cannons tension, and leaves no room for an in-combat trickle (dropped the same day: a passive may bring it back for one chassis as a niche). Out of combat, shield returns each battlefield phase at the chassis's field rate; never a free refill between skirmishes, so withdraw-and-re-engage is not a reset. Foes get past a shield by striking twice (double strike) or, for bosses, by breaching: the excess overflows into hull. Rates and sizes are placeholders.

Decided

Armour is a flat number off every bare-hull blow, and zero is fine.

Decided 18 September. A chassis stat (components may add to it later), never a percentage, taken off each blow that lands on hull with no shield standing. A blow smaller than the armour does nothing: a Badger drives its treads over a horde of ghouls laughing, and that is the point. A ghoul that only ever claws for ten deserves to be walled; the answer is monsters that do more than claw (double strike, breach, abilities), not a damage floor. The Sparrowhawk has no armour and stays afraid of ghouls. Shield is not armoured: a blow on shield costs the shield its full damage, so shield answers the big blow and armour answers the swarm, and a bruiser and a glass cannon are different builds. The per-round Rift Pressure tick is what keeps a long wall from being free.

Decided

Foes strike in the order they stand, and the heaviest deploy left.

Decided 18 September. Every blow resolves on its own against the shield standing at that moment, so strike order decides what a small shield blocks. No initiative numbers, nothing to micromanage: front rank left to right, then the back rank. At first contact the heaviest hitters (attack times strikes) deploy left, ties in arrival order, so the player sees the big blow coming from the jump. A round cap on skirmishes was rejected: it breaks the flow like an advertisement in a show.

Recommended

One Rift Pressure clock.

A single bar that ticks once per battlefield phase and once per skirmish round, and spawns a fresh pack at the rift when it fills. Dawdling on the map and grinding in a skirmish feed the same meter. It is the race made mechanical, and it replaces any hard limit on skirmish length.

Open

Pacing: a battle is a short run of fights toward a boss the player earns.

Ryan, 2 October. The battle was meant to play like a small roguelike run: several skirmishes for upgrades while pushing through the waves toward the boss; at least three, about five on average. Measured that day with a headless probe (the greedy bot, the default team): a rush wins in 3 to 9 phases, the Sparrowhawk usually starting the boss fight by phase 3, because the boss's contact pulls in every machine wherever it stands; an undefended gate falls in 12 to 76 phases; a team that never moves held the undead for 300 phases with the gate untouched, since Rift Pressure spawns one pack of fixed strength each time it fills and never escalates. A flyer skipping everything to solo the boss is the thing to kill, and a bigger map or a slower flyer would add phases but not fights. The direction, agreed in discussion, each piece for Ryan's design session before code:

One bar, filled by threat. Every kill feeds it by how dangerous the foe was (a ghoul pack a sliver, an elite a large share), never one notch a skirmish (Ryan: killing six ghouls should not break a seal). Each mark on it is a prize, the marks spaced further apart like experience; one mark (the third, as a placeholder) also unseals the boss (the Rupture), and from then on when to go is the player's call. Marks past it are optional extra prizes, a risk for a player who feels behind at the meta level. Named 7 October (Ryan): the Rupture meter. Killing foes feeds their essence back into the rift until it overloads into a Fracture; each Fracture is a burst of feedback that grants the team a temporary boost (section 8's pick, which no longer follows a won skirmish); enough Fractures and the rift Ruptures, breaking the barrier that protects the boss powering it. Fractures go on past the Rupture, each harder to reach than the last, so a player who feels behind can keep gaining power at a growing cost. The words are capitalised as names. The name was chosen to read as forcing our way through, never as serving the rift ("Tithe" was refused for that). Elites: a tier between the rank and file and the boss does not exist yet (in code, elite means the boss). The Sparrowhawk's codex has it built to eliminate priority targets and disengage before it is overwhelmed, and hunting easy fights is its niche, so elites should be costly for it to stay on, not unreachable; the same codex says the horde is dumb without a guiding intelligence, a hook for what elites are.

Turtling is its own punishment (Ryan: defensive play is valid, farming without end is not). The soft pressure is escalation: Rift Pressure spawns faster and sends worse packs the longer the battle runs, so whether the team will make it to the boss is uncertain because of the fights, not hidden rules, and a little farming pays while a lot gets the team killed. The hard backstop is the flattening reaching the gate's approach (the rift-converted ground starting farther in and spreading in rings as the face of Rift Pressure, one clock with two faces; it advances only between phases with its next ring drawn on the plan, so the preview stays exact; it is never slow ground for machines, or late pushes die; it needs a look of its own apart from the flattened discs painted round every pack). A Loop Hero forced boss is the alternative backstop; one, not both. With the boss sealed, flyer rules need no change for pacing.

Decided

Combined with terrain, limping mechs make runs unwinnable with no warning. Hull damage has no secondary penalty in the first prototype.

Skirmish layer

The skirmish decides how you fight. Touchstone: Slice and Dice. Target prioritization, semi-controlled randomness, and build preparation and synergy. Several variants were considered, including a zoomed-in hex grid; that one was set aside because it repeats the battlefield's decision type, fights the no-micromanagement goal, and is hostile to thumbs in portrait.

Round loop

Recommended

No positioning on the player side.

Mechs have no ranks and never shuffle. Enemies organize around them.

Recommended

Attack, then reroll: one interleaved phase, then the enemy, then straight back.

Every mech's die is rolled. Tap to lock, drag to spend, reroll up to three times a round (section 6) at any point, before or after attacking, so a low damage roll is information for the rest of the round. End turn resolves the enemy and starts the next round at once, with no click to begin your turn. This is the loop damage ranges earn, and it is the thing that keeps the game from being Slice and Dice with extra layers. Rerolls are per round, never a pooled stock.

Decided

Damage ranges and crits, by weapon class.

Kinetic faces show a minimum and a maximum, roll between them the moment they land, and may crit for double. Energy faces show one number and never roll or crit: reliable and hot against swingy and cool. Crits are where the exciting passives live. There is no undo; the roll is the commitment. Numbers are in tens.

Decided

Hold a die to see every face.

Deciding whether to reroll needs the whole die, not the face showing. Hold lists every face with the current one marked; double-tap opens the same list as a codex box. Passives are not shown on the die; they belong to the mech.

Decided

No hard round limit.

A cap would punish defensive, value, status, reflect, and mitigation builds in favour of burst. Skirmish length is governed by the Rift Pressure clock, the withdraw option, and tuning (typical skirmish three to five rounds; enemy damage must outpace sustain so stalemates cannot happen).

Decided

Player-initiated withdraw, and its price is one enemy turn.

Decided 19 September. Withdraw is offered at any point of the player's turn, the first round included. Its price is always the same: the enemy takes its turn, then the team flees at the end of the round, so there is no leapfrogging around contact unscathed. Being forced into contact is bad strategy, not bad luck, and gets the same price. The enemy's turn is guaranteed (Ryan, 7 October): spending dice before withdrawing is fine because nothing may skip that turn, so no passive or component lets a machine out before the enemy strikes or makes its blows miss as the team leaves; the Prairie Dog's screen did both and gets a new passive. Every kill made before withdrawing is kept for the salvage. Withdrawing ends the skirmish; survivors on both sides carry their damage back to the map and the pack marches on. Replaces the 15 September rule (start of round only, before any face was spent).

Decided

The enemy's turn is seen, blow by blow, before the next round or the flight.

Ryan, 23 September, after a withdrawal put the outcome up over a turn nobody saw. The enemy's turn replays one blow a beat: the foe lunges at its machine, and as the blow lands that machine's shield and hull step down to what it left, a broken shield shattering; the log types the blows in as they play. Only then do the new dice show, or the outcome (a withdrawal included: the parting blows are the price, so they are watched). The player's taps are held off while it plays. The beat is a setting, ENEMY PACE, whose top is the old instant replay (0.12 s between blows) and whose default is 0.45 s.

Decided

Ends on: pool empty (victory), all hulls zero (defeat), or withdraw.

Every kill counts toward the salvage and the Rupture meter whatever the fight ends in, and the temporary upgrades come from the meter's Fractures, not from a won fight (7 October; it read "victory awards resources and a temporary upgrade choice"). Defeat wrecks the machines (the hangar row, section 3). Surviving enemies continue their assault.

Decided

Decided 19 September from the RIFTGATE_MAP3D experiment. The ground, the gate and walls, and the player's mechs are geometry under the map camera. Superseded 1 October by the engagement-zone row below: the range was measured from the fight and chained machine to machine across the map, nothing showed who joined until a contact existed, and it could not be explained in a sentence (Ryan, 29 September, from play). The tethers went with it. Built 28 September. A fight still seeds only where a machine and a pack are adjacent, so the zone-of-control contract holds: a pack stops beside a grounded machine, and a machine two hexes from a lone pack is no fight. Decided

Engagement zones: every pack has a zone, a machine standing inside it where the pack stops fights it, and a machine inside two zones joins the two fights into one. The enemy commits its routes first; aggressive packs and hunters react, and the map says beforehand where.

Ryan, 1 October, decided in discussion over the written-up history (the touching rule of 22 September, the engagement range of 28 September and its three faults: it chained machine to machine, nothing showed who joined until a contact existed, and it could not be said in a sentence) and an interactive sandbox that drew each option on the game's field. Built the same day. The fight. A pack's engagement zone is a radius in hexes around where it ends its move, from its foe type (zone in content/foes.json, 1 to 4; placeholders: front-rank 1, back-rank 3, bosses 2). Every machine inside it fights that pack. A machine inside two packs' zones puts both packs in one fight, and that is the only way fights join: packs never join through each other and machines never through each other, so nothing chains, and the zones drawn on the map are the whole rule. For a new player: stand inside a pack's zone and you fight it; everything your machines connect is one fight. The stop. No ground is controlled: a pack stops only where a body stands in its path (a machine, a pack, the gate), and a machine beside its path is walked past unless the zone reaches it where the pack stops. A flyer fights by the zone like anyone and is still passed through. A stealth machine is never pulled in by a zone alone: it fights when it stopped one of the fight's packs, or when another machine is in the fight. A static defence inside a zone is in. The enemy commits first (Ryan: a pack whose route changes with the plan is a hidden rule, however deterministic the engine). When the round opens every pack commits a route, and the route the player reads is the route; the plan only cuts it where a body stands. Three behaviours on the foe type (behaviour): a pusher walks its committed route to the gate and never reacts, the pressure; an aggressive pack does the same unless a machine ends inside its reach, its move plus its zone, drawn as a dashed ring at the round's start, in which case it goes for the hex within its move whose zone holds the most machines, ties toward the gate; a hunter locks one machine when it spawns, by its caveat (hunts: nearest, weakest, strongest, slowest or fastest; weak and strong read hull plus shield, slow and fast read move, stealth machines are never picked), wears the display's target lock on it, and chases wherever that machine ends until one of them is dead, picking again by the same caveat if its prey is wrecked. The ring and the lock answer "will it come for me" before the plan is touched; the live preview then shows exactly whom it catches. A ranged pack walks rather than halting at range, since halting catches the picket and walking catches the squad. Content placeholders: the ghoul aggressive (its codex says it walks at the nearest heartbeat), the brute a hunter of the strongest at move 3, everything else a pusher; a dedicated hunter is a foe for Ryan to name. What it is for. Picking packs off one at a time must be resisted by the game, and fewer, longer fights are the aim. Formations with a front and a back are the threat: engaging the front puts the squad inside the back pack's larger zone. Heavy armour is a risk, since it is outnumbered easily; a scout trades power for the peel, ending behind the formation inside the back pack's zone and outside the front's. If the peel proves too strong, the lever is the stat skew between scouts and heavies, which the balance runner can measure as a skirmish matchup. Weighed and dropped: chaining in any form (enemies in lockstep would make the game unwinnable); a touch rule, packs standing together fight together (a formation in file could not then be split by anything the player does); double-booking a machine into two fights (merge is the intuitive read, and the contact panel keeps choosing the order and being the planning stop); the zone as the stop (it halts a ranged pack short of the squad); packs that re-aim at the plan without a drawn trigger; a separate aggro radius unrelated to reach (a ring that promises what the pack cannot deliver). The player splits a formation by getting behind it, blocking one column with a body while the other walks on, baiting a hunter or an aggressive pack out, and tempo between the rift's waves; attrition is not a lever, since a fight runs to one side's end or a withdrawal. Kiting: engage-and-withdraw costs the enemy's full turn and the pack stays where it is; dancing out of a reach ring is bounded by reach and by the biggest-catch rule, and a hunter of the slowest cannot be kited at all. On the map: every pack's zone where it will stop, in the warning red, a thin outline when empty and a tinted region under a heavy outline with a fight in it, a fight's packs sharing one outline; an aggressive pack's reach as a dashed crawling ring; a hunter's lock as the target rings on its prey; the states differ by weight, fill and structure, never by hue alone. The tethers, the tie rule in field order and the machines' flowers are gone. Still open: the radii and the hunters' move, by Ryan's play; the rift seeding and spawning formations rather than single packs; the map growing wider if zones crowd it.

Decided

Enemy targeting: every foe picks its own target by its sense, weighted and shown before the player acts. The counterplay is three ways to touch a telegraphed move and two ways to take it: Lure, Suppress and Disrupt; Shield and Armour.

Ryan, 2 October, decided in discussion from a survey of party-combat games and built the same day, in the cloud, with Godot fetched into the session and the suite green (Slice and Dice: a random pick per monster, shown before the turn, with abilities that override it; Darkest Dungeon: weighted picks by what the attack is good at, never a hard maximum, and a taunt that players read as broken whenever it was not absolute; Into the Breach: fully telegraphed and the game is moving the plan; Battle Brothers and XCOM 2: utility weights with easiest-to-hit first; Fire Emblem and the Tactics AI: kill-seeking determinism, documented as feel-bad). What the engine did until then: every foe struck the machine with the lowest shield, ties by lowest hull: the harshest point on the scale, deterministic and fully correlated, and an accidental permanent taunt on whichever machine was built tankiest, so the player never decided who got hit. The balance report hid it, since a single-machine cell has one target and in the team cell every blow landed on the tank at no cost. The scale has three axes: how hard the preference is (uniform, weighted, deterministic), how correlated the foes are (independent picks spread damage, a shared pick is focus fire, which is the difficulty dial), and when the pick is made and shown, which was already decided: before the player's turn, exact. The rule. Every foe picks its own target at round start by its aim, a word on the foe type that a move may override (aim): the exposed, weighted by the hull its blow would reach plus half of any shield it would break (a standing shield blocks a blow whole in this game, so hull alone read a shield of 10 as safe as one of 40; a shield about to fail is exposure too), the default for blows; anything, uniform, the Risen's register; heat, the hottest machine; the armed, the machine showing an intact weapon; fires as one, the rank shares a pick, for the casters. The pick is a commitment: a foe whose target shields up still swings at it. The arrows are the record. Machines do not taunt; monsters hunt by sense, and the codex says what each goes for, so the factions teach the player how to be hit on purpose. The pick is the one new roll and it lands before the player sees anything, the same category as the foe's choice of move, so the one-source rule holds; there is never a chance to dodge. The counterplay, in game terms after a pass in pure mechanics (redirect, remove the source, reduce, strip, recover, leave): Lure, a keyword riding on any foe-targeted entry, a damage or a status weapon or an entry that lures and does nothing else: every foe the entry reaches turns on this machine for the round, intents and all; reach and the area keywords decide who and how many; no chip, since the arrow changing is the record; a lured foe whose move has no target keeps doing it, and the hold says so before the spend. Suppress, unchanged: the move does nothing this round, resisted by the foe's own resistance plus Numbed, which decays. Disrupt: the foe does its other move instead, this round, the new intent shown at once, deterministic, the next in its pattern on a boss with three moves; the soft interrupt, cheaper and more common than a negate, rewarding a read of the foe's move list. Shield, the buffer, unchanged, also the answer to injuries. Armour, flat off every blow, unchanged, the anti-horde answer; Brace is temporary armour from a buff entry, flat, no longer a percentage. Breach beats the buffer on purpose and its answers are Lure, Suppress and Disrupt, never a bigger shield. Every threat shape (one big blow, a breaching one, many small, a rider, a move with no blow, the back rank) has at least two answers and none has every answer. Cut: Taunt and Beacon as words (a self-cast draw-all is one button with no decision behind it, redundant with the weighted pick, and with eight foes at most the choice of whom to lure is the whole point; the ally scope is parked until a support role proves to need it), Scramble as a player keyword and Confuse (a randomised or reflected intent: Disrupt covers the space without a second randomiser), Energy Armour (armour that works only on the shield: a number, not a lever), the percent Braced, a dodge chance ever. Parked: Phased, the rare "cannot be hit", and a reflect onto a foe as a boss-tier effect if deterministic. Kept for now: Cleanse, while riders stack and persist; Retaliate, reclassified as offence since it changes the next blow and not this one; the withdrawal screen as the one "blow lands on nothing". The targeting rules are measured on the team cells, since a single-machine cell cannot see them: today's lowest shield against independent weighted picks against a focused back rank, read as team hull lost and rounds per fight. Section 9 has the foes rewritten to this rule.

Range archetypes

Decided

No damage falloff at range.

Futuristic weapons hitting for less at range is counterintuitive. Range is expressed as archetypes with soft penalties, never smaller numbers.

Decided

Three reaches plus keywords.

Decided 18 September with Ryan's vocabulary audit. Melee hits the front rank, and the back rank only when the whole front is empty, so a melee build can be stranded by a held back rank and that is its cost. Line of sight hits the front rank freely and a back enemy only where its column is exposed. Indirect hits any foe in either rank; on the front rank it pays splash, half the damage off your own shield. Keywords ride on top: spread for neighbours, penetrate for the column. A passive may change a component's reach while a condition holds (the Badger's cracked weapons go melee). Melee is authored on a component too since 26 September (the Badger's claws); until then it only ever came from a passive. No delayed shells: delay adds cross-round state for no gain.

Shields, heat, injuries

Recommended

Shields are a timer.

Shield faces add shield. The shield buffer gives a few rounds to solve the pack before hull is at risk. Enemy damage should threaten to break a shield within two or three rounds against a typical pack, so shield faces buy time rather than immunity.

Recommended

Heat is the second resource.

Strong faces add heat. A vent face clears it. Running hot risks internal damage. Passives and support faces (vent an ally's heat) make heat a team problem, not a solo one.

Decided

Every chassis cools passively at the end of the round.

Ryan, 22 September. A chassis carries a cooling rating (content; 0 when absent) and sheds that much heat at the start of every turn, before the dice roll. Venting stays a tactic; a round spent on ballistics or shields is now also a way of waiting to cool down. Cool at the start, heat at the end (Ryan, same day): the Overheating status ticks at the end of the round, after the enemy has struck, so a scorch lands on the round's peak heat before the next turn's cooling relieves it; the first cut had it the other way round and cooling softened every scorch. Every overheat burns at the end of the round (Ryan, 22 September, third pass): heat climbs past the maximum during the round with no spike when a component is spent, the Overheating tick lands on top, and whatever stands over the cap then burns hull one for one as the gauge settles at the cap. So a machine run hot can still be vented before it pays, and the space is there for other ways to intervene. The old "heat max" chip is now the burn warning: the hull the round's end will take unless something changes. The forecast's heat counts the stacks carried plus one per scorching blow coming; it counts the die's own heat only while that die is being dragged, since shown unasked it read as something done to the mech rather than by the player. Cooling is a stat like the others (a passive may raise it). Tuning, not design: cooling must stay well under a typical weapon's heat, and a vent component must be worth a whole turn, so the heat sink wants a bigger number. Numbers are placeholders.

Decided

Injuries are one threshold and one roll of the die, on bare hull only.

When a blow lands on bare hull (no shield standing) and what gets past armour is at least a quarter of maximum hull, the mech's die is rolled and the component it lands on is injured. An intact component cracks: it loses its keywords, crit chance and unique effect but still does its basic action. A cracked component hit again is destroyed and goes blank. A destroyed component or an empty hardpoint takes the blow and nothing happens, so a badly broken mech shrugs off more of them. A passive may hold a cracked component short of destruction (the Badger's). The counterplay is shielding before a big telegraphed hit. The one-in-two coin on top of the threshold was cut on 17 September as randomness with no decision behind it; per-faction signature injuries were dropped earlier as an unnecessary layer. The threshold is a placeholder.

Decided

A damaged face is a damaged component, and it stays damaged for the battle.

Cracked and destroyed entries persist across the battle's skirmishes, so a lost skirmish costs something into the next. In the hangar (revised 5 October, section 3) a cracked component is repaired free and a destroyed hardpoint component is gone for good, replaced only by what the run offers; a destroyed fixed component is rebuilt free, so a chassis's identity can never be lost. This is the component damage system: there is no separate one, and it is the one permanent cost a bad fight leaves. It lands where Ryan wants the punishment: an injury needs a single blow of a quarter of max hull through to bare hull, which is what a suicided glass cannon or an over-trusted tank takes, and the die places it.

Pinned

Low-hull berserker or last-stand ability.

A mech at critical hull unlocking an overdrive face is Doom energy and an anti-spiral. Parked, not rejected.

Recommended

Terrain is a skirmish-wide condition.

The hexes involved set a modifier for the whole fight, so choosing where to engage still matters without positioning inside the skirmish. Water dampens burn, rubble slows a reload, and so on. Ryan's caveat (22 September, section 12): things the machines feel, never a bonus the foes get, since a pack stands on good ground for free; the ground a fight is on is decided and nothing acts on it yet.

Decided

Three rerolls, and a reroll always shows a different face.

Decided 15 September. Two rerolls did not feel like enough even before consumables and commander powers; the base is three. A reroll never lands on the face showing nor on one equivalent to it, as a known global rule: these are not really dice, and a reroll that changes nothing reads as a bug rather than as bad luck. A die whose faces are all alike keeps its face. The count shows on the Reroll button only, not in the header. This is a rule the player must be told: the Reroll button's tooltip and the die codex say it now, and the gesture tutorial should too. Knock-on: on a four-face die three never-repeating rerolls almost always find the wanted face. Decided

Decided

Few machines against a horde is the fantasy, and the hooks come from it.

Decided 15 September. With every foe on the field striking each round, incoming damage on the lowest shield is the pressure the whole skirmish is built around, and the answer is design, not a cap: area damage that earns its keep against a full formation, taunt and mitigation that shape who gets hit (the lure and the aims, since 2 October: the targeting row in section 5), and enemy behaviour that is not always optimal. The horde is an invasion, not an army: most rank and file may target badly or sometimes not attack at all, while the higher tiers of the demonic and eldritch factions execute plans that look thought through. A direction to build: packs that move with or natively include a commander unit; kill it and the pack's targeting scrambles, even mid-turn. Decided

Decided

Double-tap a unit for its codex entry.

Hold is the quick read: numbers and arrows while the finger is down. Decided

Numbers in the magnitude of tens.

Hull, shield, damage, and heat live in tens, not ones, so upgrades can be incremental and big numbers feel big; a 15 or a 33 is fine, and the validator only refuses negatives (clarified 18 September). Burn is one stack per twenty damage dealt at five per stack, splash is half the damage rolled, heat overflows one for one.

Decided

Decided 16 September, from Ryan's cleanup. Mechs and the builder

Mechwarrior's build depth, simplified and dialled back. The feel to keep is that a loadout is a real identity. The habits to drop are strict weight classes and gated equipment.

Decided

Three to four mechs per battlefield.

Enough for a real decision between splitting up and staying together for skirmish strength, without tedious planning. Recommended: design for four, start a campaign with three, unlock the fourth slot early.

Decided

Chassis are not classes and not only bipeds.

Tanks, hovercraft, quadrupeds, and flyers are all candidates. No light, medium, heavy labels. Chassis diversity comes from base stats: hull, shield, movespeed, hardpoints, and similar simple numbers that do not need prominent display.

Decided

The die is the equipment: fixed components plus hardpoints, one entry each.

Decided 18 September. A chassis has fixed components (built in, never removed or sold, no hardpoint used: the Badger's claw and plating) and hardpoints (free slots, any component in any one). Die size is fixed count plus hardpoints; the Badger rolled a d6 from two fixed and four hardpoints with four pins on its model until 6 October, and rolls a d5 since: one Chainsaw Claw, the Bullion Fragmenter and three hardpoints, three pins, every face with a model one face. Every component is exactly one entry: multi-slot components were considered and scrapped the same day as invisible complexity (where would it render, and would anyone pay for the fragility). The player never sees a polyhedron, so shown text says component, never face; Face stays the engine's word for one entry.

Earlier, recommended: hardpoints set die size.

Each component contributes one face; the chassis fills any remainder. A chassis with four hardpoints rolls a d4 and is consistent and focused. One with eight rolls a d8 and carries more but sees each face less. That is the deckbuilder's thin-deck tension as a chassis stat. First pass: d4, d6, d8. The builder shows the odds of each face.

Decided

Zero to one chassis passive, and it may be a skirmish passive.

Two flavours. A double-edged niche definer: a skirmisher aircraft with big shield, high movespeed, low hull, few faces, and "double damage, double heat," positioned by its manufacturer as a hit-and-run assassin, that becomes a carry beside an ally who vents its heat. Or a simple multiplier fantasy: "doubles all burn," where the player stacks burn and burns everything like a maniac.

A passive is one idea. Two complementary rules, such as "when I gain shield, one random ally gains the same amount" and "my shield does not recharge outside combat," are one passive with an upside and a cost, not two. The test is whether it can be named in a phrase and understood as one thing. Cap: three rule lines.

Recommended

A passive multiplies or redirects what faces already do.

It never introduces a verb of its own. If it needs a new mechanic to explain, it is a component, not a chassis.

Decided

Components are free.

Untyped hardpoints, any component in any slot. A niche you can leave is an invitation; the player steps outside it for experimentation or because resources force them to make it work.

Decided

Silhouettes become terrain traits.

Hovercraft cross water, walkers ignore broken ground, flyers pass over mountains but cannot body-block, wheels run fast on open ground. Team composition becomes a decision about the map as well as the fight. The words by way of moving are section 4's movement row (decided 2 October).

Decided

Every chassis has a maker's designation and a nickname.

Ryan, 16 September. The makers' names are deliberately dull (the EX-45XLE Combat Miner Frame); the names people use are not (the Badger). Content carries both: designation, the long name, and name, the nickname, which is what the game says everywhere short. The codex draws them as a spec plate: designation small above, nickname large, maker, traits and die under. Since 2 October (Ryan) the plate lists the chassis's traits by name where a silhouette word stood: a silhouette beside a turning model said nothing, and the traits (Walker, Tracked, Wheeled, Flyer, Hover, Stealth) are rules the player needs; the field is gone from the content. The first Bulwark was renamed the Badger, id and all; the "adjective animal" convention floated the same day is dropped in favour of this pair.

The shape of a designation, per maker (Ryan, 26 September: every chassis carries one, and the validator refuses a chassis without; the plate's shape says the maker before you read it). Minerva Heavy Works: an industrial catalogue code, letters, a hyphen, a number and a suffix of capitals for the variant, then the machine's job in plain words: EX-45XLE Combat Miner. Kestrel Dynamics: a type letter and number with a mark in Roman numerals, then the role: K-7 Mk III Interceptor (the Sparrowhawk). Wainwright & Sutler: a pattern number, the way an old arsenal numbers what it accepts into service, then the class: Pattern 1 Combat Walker (the Clydesdale, a century in service and still the first pattern). Aphelion: a programme name from the deep-space register and a flight number, then the frame's role, the way a mission is logged: Perihelion Flight 7 Support Frame (the Baboon). The Arsenal: a decree, the house's name and an instrument number in Roman numerals: Arsenal Instrument IV, its nickname a zealot's word. Rules across all five: the designation is the long dull name and the nickname the one the game says; a designation names the machine's job in-world, never a stat; no marketing words; and frame is Aphelion's word alone (Ryan, 26 September: it reads as anime everywhere else), so a Minerva machine is a miner or a vehicle, a Kestrel one an interceptor or an airframe's type, a Wainwright & Sutler one a walker or a pattern. The Sparrowhawk's and Clydesdale's are Claude's fills on this convention, Ryan's to move.

Decided

No chassis wears a machine's name from the properties we sit nearest.

Ryan, 28 September. Not a copyright worry: a design must read as ours or as a knowing homage, never as a copy, and Ryan's designs lean on BattleTech, which he knows well, so the clashes he cannot see are the ones in properties he knows less. content/reserved_names.json holds, by source, every BattleTech 'Mech (Sarna's full catalogue of 756 pages, Clan and Inner Sphere names both, animal-named or not, since a Huntsman or a Griffin reads as a lift as surely as a Timber Wolf), every Heavy Gear machine (Dream Pod 9's catalogue by faction: the property nearest ours, its North naming for bears, cats and dogs and its South for snakes and lizards), the Warhammer 40,000 vehicles, Knights and Titans, and Halo's vehicles; about 1,250 names. The Earth-animal subset of BattleTech is repeated grouped by kind as a reading aid for choosing nicknames. The validator refuses a chassis whose name or id matches any exactly (case, spaces and hyphens ignored; sharing a word is fine, so a Grey Wolf passes while a Timber Wolf fails) and its message names the source. A clash kept knowingly is recorded under design.name_clash with the reason, which keeps the build green and the fact in the entry. Flagged the day the rule landed, and Ryan's verdicts the same day, which set the tiers: iconic (the name alone conjures their machine: Warthog, Timber Wolf, Rhino for a wargamer; never), known (a hobbyist recognises it, mostly a Clan or Inner Sphere designation rather than the name people use; case by case, a homage when the design is ours) and coincidence (a name every military reaches for, obscure in theirs; keep). The Mandrill (a Fire Mandrill 'Mech that looks like ours, the one real problem) became the Baboon, which is itself the Howler's Clan designation and is kept as known-tier; the Goshawk (the Clan Vapor Eagle's Inner Sphere name) is kept the same way, and with it the 'hawk pair, which was the point; the Sparrowhawk (a Halo Wars gunship and a BattleTech fighter, both obscure) is a coincidence and kept; the Badger's id (a Heavy Gear APC; the shown name Honey Badger is nobody's) is kept; the Dingo (a Heavy Gear Northern gear, an obscure resin-era biped in a niche property, while the real ATF Dingo is a four-wheeled armoured car like ours) is kept as a coincidence (Ryan, 29 September). Every flagged name is decided and every note says why it stays. Real-world military names (the Bundeswehr's Dingo, Britain's Coyote) are listed for reference and not flagged: they read as the homage Minerva and Wainwright & Sutler intend. Considered and dropped: Pacific Rim (two-word codenames never clash with one animal), Zoids and Horizon (coined names; they matter for how a machine looks, not what it is called), Titanfall, Armored Core and Front Mission (no animals).

Decided

The passive and the fixed components are the design; the stock loadout is the maker's generic kit.

Ryan, 26 September, on the Mandrill's first fill. A chassis's flavour and its design live in its passive and its fixed (locked) components, which the player cannot swap; everything in the free hardpoints is stock that the player will replace, so it is never factored into the design of the unit. The stock kit will most likely be generic and shared between a maker's machines, with unique components unlocked through play (drops and blueprints, section 10). Two consequences: a passive must work with generic kit, never depend on one unique component (a mech must not feel terrible to run because the right blueprint has not dropped); and every mech wants enough depth that there is no straight-line optimisation and no single build path. For the Mandrill this means its identity has to come from its passive and from something fixed; its shoulder cannons or its backpack pods are the candidates for fixed slots, and the rest of its filled kit is in flux. Related, the same day: bosses should be resistant to complete stuns as a rule (today a suppress on a massive foe adds two Numbed stacks instead of one, so the second suppress already lands at 80%; a base resistance for bosses landed 26 September, the Numbed row below). Second-chance rolls are not avoided on principle: the bible's one-source-of-randomness rule is about decision density, so a chance the player can shift by their own choices is a decision, and a chance they cannot touch is noise.

Decided

Two component types, weapon and equipment, and equipment says whom it reaches.

The same day: overcharge is retired as a keyword (double damage and double heat was the Sparrowhawk's passive over again, and the Overclocked Railgun, a temporary component, now carries 100 and 40 heat outright); the word is reserved for an offensive buff on an ally, shape open. Decided

Numbed decays, and a foe may carry a base stun resistance.

Ryan, 26 September, after an analysis of three patterns: permanent scaling within the fight (Numbed's first form: honest, but a resource that only runs out, worst in the long boss fights where a stun matters most), Darkest Dungeon's one-turn immunity after a stun plus base resistance per foe (a rhythm of stun, skip, stun, but two stun sources alternating lock a foe for good), and decaying stacks (the same rotate-your-targets decision as a slope rather than a wall). Built as the third with the second's base resistance: a landed suppress adds two Numbed stacks (three on a massive foe), each stack is 10% resistance on top of the foe's own stun_resist from content (30 on the three bosses, 0 on the rank and file, placeholders), a stack is lost at the start of each of the foe's turns, and a shrugged-off suppress adds nothing. So a stun repeated on one foe stops landing while stuns spread across the formation keep working, and a boss is hard to stun twice running but never immune for the fight. The bot values a suppress at its odds. Ryan's caveat stands: whether any of this is needed hinges on the intended length of a skirmish and of a boss skirmish, to be judged in play; today the runner's greedy fights last two to six rounds against a pack and four to nine against a boss. Ryan also agreed that suppress, a complete stun, is allowed to choke itself: by the time a foe resists everything, the damage windows it gave should have ended the fight. Numbed is exempt from every interaction (Ryan, 2 October): nothing deepens it, spreads it, cleanses it or reads it beyond suppress itself and its own decay, since a touchable stun resist is where other games' combat loops broke; the hovercraft row has the hole that surfaced it.

Decided

Buff equipment: a component kind that grants a machine a status for the round.

Ryan, 28 September (the 29th's build). A buff is the third kind of equipment beside shield and vent: spent on a machine (itself, an ally, or either, as the component says) it grants one of the machines' statuses, so many stacks deep, through the end of so many rounds, and the pipeline reads it like any modifier. The status names what the buff does, so a buff component is data: status, stacks, rounds, and the validator holds the status to the machines' own (a debuff on a foe stays a weapon). Three statuses to start, names placeholders: Overcharged (+10% damage a stack; the word Ryan freed from the old keyword, and the same status devour will stack on the Ammit for the skirmish), Braced (15% less damage taken a stack) and Primed (every blow lands a Burning stack per stack). The die shows the buff glyph with the stacks as its number and the status's name in the row; the hold lists it on the machine like any in-combat effect. One draft component carries it, the Overcharge Coil, fitted to nothing. The bot weighs a buff by what its status does to the machine it lands on this round: more on the attack in hand, less of the incoming, or the burn its blows will leave, and nothing on a machine whose die is spent. The same day the bot learned to value a status for the stun it may cause through a machine's on_foe_status passive, at the loop's odds and the foe's odds of not shrugging it off, so the Baboon's balance row stops understating it.

Hardpoints are gun mounts on the model; a component wears a silhouette class at the next mount, and armour and vents show nowhere.

The pins, proposed 16 September and in force since the first lowforge units: an Empty named hp_1 … hp_n, one per hardpoint, its +Y where the weapon points; a fixed component is modelled into the machine and carries no pin. The rule (Ryan, 30 September, after judging the first pass, where a slab of plating sat on a gun socket and a vent block hung off a wing tip): hardpoints are gun mounts. Its faces are rechargeable abilities, so recharge is the roll and nobody hoards a die result. Examples: smoke that blanks the back rank's intents this round, a drone screen that absorbs the next hit, focus fire that adds one to every face on a single target, a scan that reveals the pack's next two rounds. Die size (d4, d6, d8) is a research path with stronger faces and a backfire face or two on the larger dice.

Decided

Reusable commander abilities never deal direct damage.

One-shots may. Damage one-shots belong at the battlefield layer (soften a pack before contact) so mech dice stay the killing power inside a skirmish.

Decided

Command faces come from the hangar, never from Fracture rewards.

Thematically wrong as loot, and they would dominate the reward pick because they affect every skirmish equally.

Open

Is the command die needed at all?

The alternative is one-shots only, with meta upgrades to inventory size and effectiveness, and reroll bundles among the rewards. Claude's view: keep the die, because a rolled face is inherently non-hoardable and the one-shots-only model pushes everything into the inventory the designer already knows they would hoard.

One-shots and the hoarding problem

Decided

Spending a consumable must not feel bad.

The designer is in the camp that hoards potions for a rainy day that never comes. If one-shots are core, the design has to make spending the default.

Recommended

Three fixes.

One-shots picked up in a battle expire when the rift closes, so there is nothing to save them for beyond this battle. The pouch holds three, so a fourth pickup forces use-or-discard on the spot. A starting stock chosen in the hangar restocks every battle; research raises capacity and quality. Doom Eternal solved the same problem the same way: resources regenerate on a short loop, so spending is the default and saving is the anomaly. Saving a full pouch for the boss remains a legitimate blitz strategy with a real cost in missed upgrades.

Fracture rewards

Recommended

Pick one of three.

Each Fracture of the Rupture meter grants the pick (Ryan, 7 October; it followed a won skirmish before, and its faction weighting is retired, as is the likely-loot icon packs were to show). A modifier on a mech face you already carry (lasts the battle), a one-shot for the pouch, or a battle-wide boon such as an extra reroll per round. Temporary upgrades amplify the build you brought and never replace it, so the pull is toward synergy.

On the battlefield: city abilities

Decided

Battlefield-level abilities belong to the city.

Sprint, airlift, teleport, tractor beam, orbital strike, and any number of silly ideas. They are upgraded and managed as city systems, and no command die is rolled on the battlefield.

Decided

Cooldowns in battlefield phases are acceptable here.

Each movement phase is a turn, so a cooldown is legible and cannot be hoarded. City research shortens cooldowns, adds charges, and unlocks abilities. Cooldowns still do not exist inside the skirmish, where the die is the recharge.

Recommended

Some city abilities are limited by distance from the gate.

Artillery reaches the southern half, airlift only lifts from the gate, the tractor beam has a range. The push north leaves city support behind, which is a real cost to blitzing and comes out of flavour rather than a rule.

Enemy factions

Decided

Archetype-based, not elemental.

Each faction has a behavioral signature that demands a different tactical answer, with internal diversity and general paradigms to guide decisions rather than strict rules. Elements are flavour and the reward currency, never a damage-type triangle. Certain faction combinations are favourable or unfavourable for a given team, which is what makes path choice build-driven.

Decided

Every foe's second action runs: a defend, buff or debuff it does instead of a blow, picked by weight and announced like one.

The moves and their weights were rewritten 2 October (the playtest set row below); the effect mechanism and its reading stand. Built 30 September from the ten actions written on 14 September, each an effect the engine runs rather than a rule keyed to a foe's id (Foe.ACTION_EFFECTS), so Ryan can move them between foes or write new ones: defend folds against damage (half taken until the foe's next action, the Spawn) or regrows hit points (the Colossus); buff readies a foe to get back up (it returns to the pool once, whole, after dying, unless an executing blow touched it in the fight: the Ghoul), calls a foe of a named type up into the pool for the round's end (the Necromancer's ghoul, made by the battle's content), or rallies every other foe on the field to hit harder next round (the Pit Lord); debuff costs the team rerolls next round (the Brute), stokes a machine's heat (the Hellcaster), scrambles a machine's next reroll so it lands anywhere, the same entry included (the Whisperer; a cleanse clears it), or cracks the component a machine's die is showing with no hull hit (the Maw). The statuses these leave (Folded, Kindled, Getting Back Up, Scrambled) are chips on the unit. Weights are placeholders: every attack now carries weight 3 against its special's 1, so a foe does the special about one turn in four; Ryan moves them per foe. Meta and resources

Decided 5 October in one session, Ryan and Claude, each point pressure-tested; the loop in hard terms is a draft until play moves it. Numbers in it are placeholders, as everywhere.

Decided

Two kinds of gain, one currency.

Persistent salvage, spent between battles; temporary upgrades, lasting the current battle only. Every kill yields salvage whatever its fight ends in, and every Fracture of the Rupture meter grants a temporary upgrade (the pick, section 8; amended 7 October, the salvage row below and section 4's pacing row). A won battle banks the battle's salvage and its boss drops a research token; a lost battle forfeits the battle's salvage and the city falls (section 3). A blueprint is permanent for the run; building from it is the spend. Revisited 6 October, when Ryan asked whether an earlier idea still held, that a front's draw could be the resources its pack type drops more often: one currency stays. The front's draw is already carried three ways, all decided: the host maker's catalogue after a won battle, and the research token themed on the faction it came from (a third, skirmish rewards weighted by faction, was retired 7 October with the reward pick's move to the Fractures). A resource per faction would let the map solve itself (go where the resource you lack drops) and was not adopted. A wipe that holds keeps the battle's salvage (section 3).

Decided

Salvage: a kill is a kill, the tally comes at the battle's end, and the field holds loot.

Ryan, 7 October. Every foe killed yields salvage, in a fight won, lost or withdrawn from: withdrawing never takes a kill away (the enemy's turn on a withdrawal is guaranteed, section 5, so a kill is never free). The salvage is tallied at the battle's end and never shown while the battle is fought: one less number cluttering the screen, and no visible count to farm against. A kill is weighted by its danger once the pacing row's weights exist (section 4), the same tally that fills the Rupture meter and a faction's Anathema. The numbers are large on purpose (ten a foe as a placeholder, so a won battle pays about a hundred): salvage is no combat stat and need not be small, which leaves room to price things finely. Battlefield salvage is loot on the map (Ryan's idea, open since September, decided now; "chest" is the design word only, never shown): wrecks and alien remains the rift belches out from other worlds, drawn so they plainly look lootable. Each always holds a random amount of salvage and a chance of blueprints, ready-to-fit components or both, with rarity tiers if it turns out to need them; the drops are the salvage's random factor, so the kill tally itself stays exact under the hood. A machine claims one by ending its move on it, which gives fast machines a purpose. They are seeded with the map, their density part of its variation, and not rare, since the detours make the play. Tapping one tells the player whether it is worth the path instead of defending the city: its rarity at least, perhaps its likely contents (open). What a machine claims joins the battle's salvage: unpacked on the battle's end screen, kept when the city holds, lost when it falls. Hit-and-run farming is accepted (Ryan, 7 October): a withdrawal feeds Rift Pressure and the enemy gets its hit, so withdrawing is a tactical decision, rarely the optimal one. Open: the shown name, how much a tap tells, how many a map holds, and whether the rarest can hold a research token (recommended: no, the token stays the boss's, and the rarest holds a blueprint directly).

Decided

The loop in hard terms.

Every kill: salvage, whatever its fight ends in (7 October). A Fracture: the pick, for this battle (the Rupture meter, section 4). Battle won: the salvage banks; the boss drops a research token; the host maker makes an offer. In the hangar: spend salvage to build a blueprint, fit a module or repair the capital's gate; unpack a research token into one blueprint themed on the faction it came from; wrecks sit out a battle. Campaign won: a difficulty tier, and everything first-found enters the pool. Fifteen to twenty-five real decisions across twelve battles, every one spending on something that gets better, none on a chore.

Decided

Offers, never trees; the front picks the catalogue.

Ryan, 5 October. Research trees (the 11 September line) are retired. After a won battle the host city's maker offers three things from its catalogue (a chassis, blueprints, modules) and the player takes one; the capital offers the Arsenal's trickle, tier gated by the Convergence (section 1). Choosing a front therefore chooses the fight and the build path at once, which is the agency the mission map needed. Research on demand for a time fee was weighed and refused: it turns the pool into a catalogue, every run orders the same best items, and a queue is the city management the mission select refused. Chassis come the same way, offered and bought, since every maker wants five or more and 25 chassis are not a tree. The randomness of the economy lives in what is offered and in the research token, never in what a thing is (18 September).

Decided

Components are flat; the chassis is tuned.

Ryan, 5 October, choosing among component modification (a tree applied freely is the straight-line optimisation the bible refuses, and the one thing the runner cannot measure), looted modules installed on compatible components (a second inventory with a compatibility matrix on a phone, and destroyed-is-gone would sting twice) and chassis slots. Components are never modified: growth is swapping for a better one from a tiered pool (the starter kits carry the tier hint), so a destroyed component stings exactly once. A chassis carries a few module slots, bought with salvage; a module is a modifier with scope machine and a source, read through the pipeline like a passive, and it lives on the chassis, which is always rebuilt, so nothing the player tuned is ever lost. This also answers where a chassis's numbers grow.

Decided

Modules carry rule text, in four archetypes.

Ryan, 5 October, over Claude's numbers-only version (boring, the ARPG trap: building as a maths problem). A module is high stats with no text, low stats with a good text, massive stats with a negative text, or negative stats with an amazing text, so one object type gives four real choices and keeps the chase. The budget in section 2 holds them to about three texts a machine. Chassis passives are never swapped: the replacements would have to be as interesting as a base passive, doubling the design work, and a passive is often what holds a chassis with amazing stats or fixed components back from being broken. Slot count is a chassis balance lever the runner measures like hull and cooling; the Clydesdale's is the most slots, the true sandbox, its nine-entry die the soft drawback (any one entry lands a ninth of the time) and the busiest readout on the field its codex line.

Decided

Between campaigns: the pool, never power.

Ryan, 5 October. A blueprint found once can be offered in later campaigns; the player still has to find or research it this run. Nothing is started with (Rogue Legacy's problem: after three campaigns the hangar is full at battle one and the loop the game is about is dead), and the cram pressure dissolves for a different reason: a run of twelve battles shows a third of a pool twice today's size, a different third each time. What carries: the pool, the codex, difficulty tiers (Slay the Spire's ascension; every tier clearable from a fresh start, or it is grind), and one rare piece from a maker whose favour the player earned (the next rows). Carrying a tuned machine was weighed and refused as Into the Breach's own gimmick.

Decided

The makers' favour: one rare piece, in the maker's voice, from the maker you pleased most.

Ryan, 5 October. Each maker keeps its own measure of a customer, and at the campaign's end (won or lost, since it is earned by use) every maker whose measure the player topped makes an offer: one rare component from its catalogue, placed in the next campaign's starting kit, with a short notice in that maker's voice (the voice field in content): as plain as "you are our best customer", in Kestrel's clipped numbers or the Arsenal's liturgy; the line to hold is no in-your-face advertising (section 1), not a register. The player takes one offer. Nothing else carries: Claude's first shape (the sponsor's catalogue in the capital's offer) was refused because a maker always on offer removes the reason to defend that maker's city, and scaling rewards are not wanted. A random rare piece is bounded (one entry on one die, lost like any other if destroyed) and makes the makers a second thing to play for.

Recommended

The measures are relative, one a maker, each the maker's own idea of use.

Claude's suggestion on Ryan's "Kestrel counts hexes moved". A maker offers when the player's machines of that make did more of the maker's thing this campaign than any other maker's machines did, so no threshold needs tuning, a player who fielded one maker all campaign hears from that maker alone, a mixed player hears from several and chooses, and each measure teaches the maker's identity by rewarding its machines played as designed: Kestrel, hexes moved (fast, over-tuned airframes); Minerva, damage taken on shield and armour (everything is a tank); Wainwright & Sutler, dice spent on attacks (quads and artillery, everything works); Aphelion, status stacks applied (high-tech suites over light hulls). The measures feed themselves, as Ryan said, and that is the vibe: loyalty looks like loyalty. The Arsenal does not compete for favour, so its measure is absolute and unforceable, in its lore: the Council's notice comes only to a commander whose campaign reached the Convergence's top tier, and offers one piece of the technology it never sells. Shown after the fact as each maker's notice, never as a live tally to optimise. The numbers that count are already in the sim (moves, blows on shield and armour, spends, stacks), so this is a tally in the campaign state, not a system.

Decided

Livery is the cheap carrot: colours unlocked by favour, applied free to any machine.

Ryan, 5 October. A livery is as simple as more colours: earn Kestrel's favour and more blues join the palette. Unlocked colours are the player's for good, cost nothing in the campaign, and go on any machine in any combination (Unit.paint, primary and secondary over the maker's livery, exists since the paint pass). Nothing in the rules reads a paint, so the game's own marks (the enemy red, the HUD, the outline) stay separable whatever the player chooses. Claude's suggestion for the trigger: every maker whose measure the player topped unlocks one of its colours, whether or not its offer was the one taken, so the piece is one and the colours are all of them. Not now; after the hangar exists.

Decided

Wrecks are recoverable, and the loadout is the cost.

Revised 5 October (it was "salvaged, repaired, or broken down into components"). A wrecked machine returns whole after a battle in the shop, keeping its intact components and losing the rest; the chassis is never lost. A roster of five or six for a squad of four is what the shop timer is for. Losses hurt but stay recoverable, which is what prevents a campaign from being lost three battles before it ends; machine permadeath was refused for that reason (a wreck in battle three is a quarter of the team with no replacement economy, and the wreck already cost the battle its machine).

Open

The numbers, measured before they are written.

Destroyed-is-gone holds only while the inflow (drops, offers, research tokens) beats the loss rate for a competent player, so the runner counts components destroyed per won fight (the cost-of-a-win table). First reading, 5 October, greedy, 20 seeds: nearly zero. The team loses none against any pack; a single machine loses a component only against a boss (the Stingray and the Prairie Dog one a won fight against The Maw, the Clydesdale 0.22 against the Pit Lord, the Goshawk 0.12 to 0.20 against the Colossus and The Maw), since destruction needs a cracked entry hit a second time. So the rule is safe from the spiral and, at the quarter-of-max-hull threshold, may be too gentle; the per-fight count understates a battle, where cracks carry from skirmish to skirmish, so the measure that decides is per won battle, once the runner plays whole battles. If that comes in over about one a battle the threshold moves, not the rule. Slot counts per chassis, the module archetypes' numbers, what a chassis and a build cost, the salvage a pack yields, the capital's bleed per fallen city and its repair price, the Convergence's threshold and the finals' unlock counts are all placeholders until then. The first whole runs (6 October, tools/campaign_run.gd, docs/campaign_run.md: 40 runs at the greedy rung with the starting team of three, a plain player that defends the front nearest to falling and hunts the nearest enemy on the map, nothing flowing in): 0.05 components destroyed per won battle, so destroyed-is-gone is safe from the spiral and probably too gentle, as the per-fight reading said; 38 runs of 40 won, under one city fallen a run. Squad size is the whole difficulty: three machines win 98% of battles, two 73%, one 24%; a machine is wrecked about every other battle, and a wreck out of a roster of three is the squad shrinking. Neither the tier nor a guest moves a full team (98% with a guest or without), because nothing in the foes escalates yet (elites, compositions and smarter aims are section 4's pacing and section 3's difficulty rows, not built). So the shop timer is the campaign's real pressure until the foes escalate, and the starting roster's size (the draft) decides how hard it bites.

Technical plan

Recommended

Godot 4 with GDScript.

Chosen on the project's merits, independent of any other codebase. A 2D-first renderer with pixel-art settings built in, hex tile layers, a strong UI toolkit for a game that is mostly panels and dice, Android export out of the box, no build step, and a permissive license with no runtime fee. Unity wins only if a deep ads and in-app-purchase SDK ecosystem is a day-one priority.

Recommended

Keep the simulation pure.

Hex math, movement, skirmish resolution, and map generation are plain scripts with no scene dependencies, driven by a seeded random generator. That makes them testable headless and gives reproducible runs for replays and daily seeds.

Decided

Balance is measured by a ladder of bots, and read as bands, not targets.

Ryan, 25 September: one person can play-test feel and micro balance, never the scale macro balance needs. So the pure simulation gets a player: sim/policy.gd drives a skirmish headless on a ladder of skill. Random never rerolls and spends every die on any legal target: the floor, and a pack it beats is too easy, no argument. Greedy reads the same forecast the screen shows the player and takes the best one-round trade, rerolling dice that come up under their die's average, holding a die whose every spend would cost more hull than it earns; it cannot see anything the player cannot. Personas are habits on top (withdraw under a hull line). A rollout rung (replay a few rounds ahead from the seed) waits until greedy stops being enough. tools/balance.gd plays every chassis alone, the starting team, and each maker's team of three (its first three machines in content order, for every maker with three; added 5 October, because a machine whose kit is for its allies, the Puffin with its allies-only Backwash Ionizer, is read by no solo cell and sat on no team cell) against every pack a faction sends, hundreds of seeds a cell, into docs/balance.md: win rate per rung, rounds and hull spent, and pick share per component, the signal for equipment (a component nobody spends is dead; one spent every time is the whole die). A cell where the rungs land together holds no decision, which is the Slice and Dice smell caught by machine. The runner says which matchups are off by a lot; feel decides the rest, and no simulated win rate is chased to a decimal. Battle and meta simulation stack on this in order, each level's bot using the one below as its auto-resolve, and neither is built before its loop exists.

Decided

Save at every phase boundary.

Android can kill a backgrounded app at any moment. If the whole state serializes at the end of each battlefield phase and each skirmish round, resuming is free rather than a retrofit. Built 30 September, after every change rather than every boundary (the save row below).

Recommended

Cube coordinates for hexes.

Pointy-top rendering with cube or axial coordinates in the logic, so distance, neighbours, and lines are trivial and orientation can change later.

Recommended

Prototype the skirmish first.

Done: the skirmish was the first thing built and is where the depth lives. It was the riskiest unknown and it defines what a component is, which defines the builder and the meta. It can be played with physical dice and index cards before any code exists, which is also the fastest way to catch overcomplication.

Decided

Getting the game onto a phone: two paths, and what each can tell you.

Decided 23 September, from the first phone session. The quick look is a Web build served over the LAN: tools/phone.sh exports and serves it, prints an https:// address, and the phone opens it in its browser on the same Wi-Fi (accept the certificate warning once; the first button press goes full screen). It shows sizes and touch at the phone's own pixels and nothing else: the frame rate is the browser's, the load is slow, pinch and hex outlines may misbehave. Ryan's verdict: good for layout, dismiss the rest. The real thing is a debug APK over USB: once, on the phone, tap Build number seven times under About, then turn on USB debugging under Developer options, plug in and accept the computer's key; then tools/android.sh exports, installs and launches it, and tools/android.sh log tails the game's output from the phone. tools/android.sh install reinstalls the last export without exporting. This is the build to judge frame rate, the hex outlines, the sound set and the layout at the phone's real height on. What the Mac needs, all in place as of 23 September: the 4.7.2 export templates under Godot's export_templates folder, the Android SDK at ~/Library/Android/sdk (command-line tools, platform-tools, build-tools 35, platform 35, from brew install --cask android-commandlinetools and sdkmanager), a JDK (Homebrew's openjdk serves; Godot's editor settings point export/android/java_sdk_path at it), and the debug keystore at the path Godot's editor settings name, made with keytool. The export presets (export_presets.cfg, Web and Android) are committed; the README carries the same commands.

Decided

The phone is the reference glass: what the build does differently on a device, from the first native days.

Decided 25 to 28 September, from Ryan's first sessions with the debug APK on a Pixel 9 (1080x2424, a Mali GPU, Godot's Compatibility renderer, the export edge to edge and immersive). Every read came as a screenshot; each was diagnosed by zooming the crop, then reproduced on the Mac at the phone's layout and density (RIFTGATE_SHOT_H, RIFTGATE_SAFE, RIFTGATE_SHOT_SCALE with the screenshot tool, and the Compatibility renderer by flag), then fixed once for every glass. What the phone taught, now rules: the design is 720 wide and a phone stretches it one and a half times, so nothing is drawn one device pixel thin: the hex grid is ribbons of geometry, not GL lines; the scanlines land on device pixels; the display's fonts are rasterised at the glass's density (the window did not oversample on its own). Both screens keep clear of the glass's safe bands: the display's cutouts plus a floor for the corners and the gesture zone it does not report; the layout is never shorter than the design, so on short glass the bands give way. The phone rests hotter: the grid at twice the Mac's gain, the feed's leaders and the foes' gauge leaders thicker on a dark edge. No 3D anti-aliasing on Android: the Compatibility renderer leaks native memory every frame a viewport draws with MSAA (godotengine/godot#97967, open), and with the contact panel's portraits also never freed the GL driver died out of memory after some minutes; both fixed 26 September, nine minutes watched flat. Measured on its own on 29 September, MSAA 2x with the fine map on: the native heap grew about 170 MB every twenty seconds and the driver aborted at 1.8 GB three and a half minutes in, so no setting reaches MSAA on a phone; screen-space anti-aliasing is refused by that renderer too, and the machines' edges come from resolution instead (the fine map, the phone's default since it holds 60 fps).
