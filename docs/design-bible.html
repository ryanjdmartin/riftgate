Concept and tone

Three nested loops. The campaign is the roguelike run: a sequence of battles, lost for good if the city gate falls. Each battle is a hex-grid battlefield with the gate on the south edge and a rift on the north edge, won by reaching and killing the boss that powers the rift. Each skirmish is a dice-driven fight triggered when mechs and enemy packs end a movement phase adjacent, and it is where the game's depth lives.

Decided

Doom and Space Marine energy.

Over-the-top badassery, played straight. Less overt irony than first planned: propaganda is fine, manufacturer advertisements and Borderlands-style gags are not, because this is the end of civilization. Never edgy, never grimdark fantasy. The city falling is never funny.

Decided

Mechs are grounded military mecha.

Mechwarrior lineage: rugged, brutal, heavy, built from parts that look serviced rather than cobbled together. Not Evangelion. In-world makers exist as provenance and a recognisable livery, in a maintenance-manual voice, never as marketing.

Decided

The Council for the Ultimate Liberation of Terra, and the world it runs.

Decided 16 and 17 September. Humanity's remaining cities are governed by the Council, formally the Council for the Ultimate Liberation of Terra; its acronym is never written or spoken in the game, however obvious, and the joke belongs to the player who notices. Plainly a martial-law regime pretending it is not, it believes its own cause even as it profits from it, which is how it has kept power: the populace has real believers, and the propaganda is sincere. It rewards innovation that helps the fight, which sets the manufacturers competing for its favour, and it keeps its own private military with the best hardware. The register is a god-emperor's court, not a junta; the tone is not the end of the world but "let them try, nobody stands against the march of human progress", said while everything quietly goes to shit. Closer to Pacific Rim than to BattleTech. Open on purpose, as room to expand: whether this is the last bastion of mankind at all, whether other colonies are doing fine and looking away, and whether the Council had a hand in the event that opened the rifts, its rise to central authority being the point of the play, which may become the campaign's spine. Retired: "houses" for the makers, and MARCH as the council's name. Lore is Ryan's; the fantasy field under design is where he keeps inspiration and homages, never shown.

Decided

The player commands for CARE: the underpaid true believers, not the politicians.

Decided 16 and 17 September. The Council pays for the city's defence through CARE, the Civil Auxiliary for Rift Emergencies: an arms-length body with the feel of an independent agency or a contracted corporation, named by the Council in its own condescension (civil, not military; auxiliary, not principal; the apocalypse filed as an emergency) and deliberately lame beside the aptitude of its people. If Ryan decides they named themselves, Agency replaces Auxiliary and the letters stay. The player came up through its ranks to Battlefield Commander: the general, never a politician. CARE's people are the war's true believers, who know why they fight and it is not the Council's reasons: practical, pragmatic, determined, genuine badasses with a rebellious streak, the lone-gunslinger action-hero register played straight and never winked at. The Council expects results and takes the credit, none of the responsibility. Its own R&D house, the Arsenal, sells CARE a trickle of its technology, the good stuff but never the best, and is the one research path the player cannot force: higher tiers come only as the campaign turns dire enough for the Council to loosen its grip. Holes to fill: what the Council does when the player fails, whether the contract can be lost, how the credit-taking turns into plot.

Decided

Enemies are old Final Fantasy monsters.

Detailed, over-the-top high fantasy, contrasted against the gritty realism of the player units. One genre invading another is the dichotomy the game wants, and it replaces most of the verbal irony.

Decided

Sound: industrial, grooving, grounded; two palettes, one for each world; the rules the listens left.

Ryan, 22 September, the direction for all audio. Music is a heavy industrial core with driving flow and tempo, carried by the beat: not too fast, more grooving than heavy even in the battle themes, musically complex and unique. Inspirations: Tool, Nine Inch Nails, The Omnific, Teho, Hozho. The map and the eventual hangar are more subdued than the skirmish but still groove. Effects follow the same rule: grounded and heavy, weight and machinery, nothing that sounds like a space opera. The reason it matters: the more grounded the sound, the more silliness the game can get away with elsewhere, so the sound carries the seriousness the tone rows above ask for. Everything is synthesised from written recipes by tools/soundforge/forge.py, the way lowforge builds the models; the forge on this computer is the instrument, and there are no real recordings to commission (closed 29 September). The recipes are the record of what each track is; this row keeps the rules.

Two palettes (decided 23 and 24 September from A/B sketches). The human side, the fight's: real-ish drums, a chugged guitar, a driven bass, electric piano, a synth lead, a metal chime, and the drone Ryan singled out as an element for every human track (the sweep pad's saws an octave down, held on one chord, the filter three quarters open and nearly still). The rift side, the map's: an orchestra (bowed contrabass and cellos, violins, horns, a harp, timpani, an orchestral bass drum), a choir on "oo", bells with a long echo, backwards swells, and dissonance written in (tritones, sevenths and ninths, chords changing at different times). The bells and the choir play on both sides for cohesion. The fight is the seam where both play at once; which palette goes to which screen is a one-line swap.

Rules for the music. Never a build, a bass drop or a speed snare: a section changes by what plays, not by a roll into it. Sections stay long enough to engage (eight bars; a track runs 70 to 144 bars) and complexity comes from harmony and counterpoint, never fills. The kick keeps its places for the whole track, so any play with time signatures (the 7/8 closes, a 6/8 triplet bar, a 3/4 stretch) happens over a bar of the same length with the beat steady under it; kick rolls are fine, snare rolls are not. Solos in the Dream Theater way; drums slap wherever they are a big part of a human track; a bass takes a back seat or kicks up a notch, never the middle. Bosses are epic, not intense, so a quiet build is a feature. A pattern whose length does not divide the bar walks round it by design (the seven-step chug), so lines are bar-length unless the walk is wanted; a stab's length is in steps, not seconds. Every drum part and voice draws its own seeded stream, so an unedited section never changes; crashes are placed by hand at section doors, never at the top. Tracks are levelled by body (mean RMS, pinned where each was judged), the mix sits under the saturator's knee with headroom (the human tracks' crackle was the master flattening a busier section, 30 September), and a section adds instruments rather than loudness: a volume knob is what a device is for. A loop resumes where it left when its screen comes back; a new battle starts the map's set from the top. When a note can be read two ways, ask before building.

The tracks (titles in the next row; Sound.TRACKS, SETS, LOOP_FROM). Hold The Line (human A, 104): the riff in three stages, lifts, the seven, the keys with Ryan's own bridge melody, a drum solo, a three-set climax at one loudness, two breaths; wraps to bar nine; locked. Night Shift (human B, 94): the human calm, four on the floor and industrial, the piano break its identity. Full Bore (human C): the dense prog-metal track, paired basses an octave apart in the Omnific construction, a section in seven. The Far Bank (rift A, 88): the rift theme, groove, lift, strings, triplet turn, interlude with 7/8 closes, a thirty-two-bar climax of rotating swells, a closer; the third act's boss theme for The Chorus, wrapping to bar nine; locked. Low Ground (rift B, 90): the rift calm, a ground bass descending A G F E under six variations, 6/8 at the 4/4 bar's length. The Crossing (rift C, 126): E Phrygian, a plucked ostinato in three-three-two over timpani, Ryan's kick-chug-snare engine as one section, a 3/4 stretch, extended to a second pass through its shape. The map's set cycles Low Ground then Night Shift, each loop crossing into the next over two seconds (equal-power on linear gain; a decibel fade gapped); the skirmish's set picks one of Hold The Line, The Crossing and Full Bore per visit and never crossfades mid-fight (the player's skip is exempt). Sound.TEMPO follows the map's track, since the rift's vortex steps on the beat.

Boss themes (25 September): one per family in the acts' order, since a boss skirmish is a long fight and an act's identity. The theme starts from its top the moment the boss is contacted on the battlefield, so its intro plays under the reveal and the fight arrives in the groove; a family without one takes the skirmish's pick. The Risen's would grow from Low Ground's ground bass (a dirge that returns after every breakdown), The Infernal Horde's from The Crossing's brass and timpani (telegraphed stabs with a bar of air before each), melodies proposed in chat first. Open: whether the final battle's source rift has a boss of its own with The Far Bank as its track, leaving The Chorus a theme from a seed (Ryan's lean, 29 September: probably).

The phone (26 to 30 September, from A/B sketches on a listening page; the phone-speaker model is high-passed at 220 Hz with a presence bump, rolled off at 9 kHz). One master for both palettes: a gentle tilt (1.5) with a narrow presence lift at 1 to 2 kHz, no hard clip anywhere. The driven bass is two asymmetric saturation stages whose drive follows the string, through a bass cabinet (voice 12, "hands down"); the backing guitar lost its clip; the kick is a metal kick at the middle size; the toms keep their body and are hit hard (tightening them made them hollow); the crash is soft and closed above 8 kHz; the soft sub carries a speaker shadow. The rift tracks sit half their phone gap above where they were (The Far Bank -17.9 dB, The Crossing -17.7; a quarter was wrong in the game). Weight lives in the 150 to 800 Hz band a speaker can play, the sub a bonus for headphones. The track levels stay until Ryan says otherwise; a speakers/headphones setting (shipped levels for headphones, an offset per track and a bus EQ on the speaker profile) is the answer to what one file cannot do for both. Ryan judges music on his phone, so a change is heard as an A/B before it lands; a sketch never overwrites a shipped track, a pick lands in the forge, then build and one --import. His melodies come through the Melody Desk (30 September onward): the session interprets what he sends, it is not drop-in.

Rules for the effects. Nothing sweeps in pitch, anywhere: a glide reads as the arcade (the laser's "pew", the shield's rise, the kill's wind-down were all glides and all went), so every sound is an impact, a mechanism or a burst. Damped knocks that die in tens of milliseconds, never a bell or an anvil left to ring; a little inharmonic metal at low gain is allowed. The interface is StarCraft's Terran console, grounded computer gibberish, irony not parody: a relay closing under a flat tone with no glide, and chatter (tiny flat tones and filtered ticks off any scale) for the console working; the dice belong to the interface. Combat and the map are over-the-top weighty, Doom's punch, for both sides: a machine's blow follows its weapon (energy a flat laser, kinetic gunfire with the cannon on a crit, indirect the cannon, melee a slash with a slam on a crit), a foe's blow a slash or a slam, a kill and the gate struck explosions. The mix is a loudness per sound in the forge; big moments (contact, the dive, the cut, the gate, a kill, the outcome) dip the music for a moment. The display's scan and idle stay silent: a constant sound on a phone is fatigue.

The effects' second pass (30 September, proposed; re-cut to Ryan's first notes 1 October; on the listening page, nothing shipped). A new set from the UI, the lore, the music and both sides' unit design, sorted into the screen's registers. The console (green): one grammar for every press, a beep (a flat tone tuned to the music's E, a quiet octave over it) leading and a membrane key under it, chatter as work; the die a mechanism (lifted, seated with no tone, rolled as the readout's own scramble, locked as corners snapping on); a tick for an intent settling. The iron: a blow by the weapon's shape (barrel, twin barrel, rack, blade, dish; the laser a flat beam, the Arsenal's plasma a whoomp with a windup and a sub hum), a punch for blunt melee (metal into flesh, no plate), a hit, a block that glances, a hit on a standing shield, and a two-second kill with a second blast and the hull hitting the ground, each in the struck machine's metal (Minerva steel, Kestrel chrome, Wainwright iron, Aphelion ceramic, Arsenal gunmetal with its red glass and a hum), footfalls by silhouette. The rift (violet): each family its own blow, heavy blow and arrival (the Risen dry bone and grave-light, the Horde fire, The Chorus a gated and crushed choir cluster ending on the console's own ticks), a boss reveal of the feed tearing and the name resolving. The red: contact, the gate, victory and defeat tuned to E, the dive's spin-down replaced by the floor rising under the count-in, defeat ending on the feed losing signal. Each pick is a small hook in the game (shape, maker, faction, silhouette, a content word for blunt melee, the replay telling shield hit from block from hit); the page says which. Rendered by tools/soundforge/fx_sketches.py beside the shipped files at the shipped levels, as AAC by afconvert on the Mac or ffmpeg at 256 kbps elsewhere (its encoder smears a transient's front at 128).

Decided

The tracks have titles, and the player can see and skip them.

Ryan, 25 September: give the tracks proper names, then in-game music controls: a ticker on the top bar, and music controls in the pause menu, on the page itself rather than a settings item, showing the current track with next and previous. The titles (proposed the same day, Ryan picking from alternatives; one table, Sound.TRACKS): human A Hold The Line, human B Night Shift (the city's machines kept running between fights), human C Full Bore (the machinery flat out), rift A The Far Bank (the other side, as of a river), rift B Low Ground (a ground bass, and the lowland the rift opens on), rift C The Crossing (the horde fording the river that The Far Bank names; Ryan's pick over Procession, Spring Tide, Quickstep, Headlong and Drumhead). The human titles are shop-floor and military plain words in the Terran console's register; the rift titles are places and movements. The files, the forge's builders and every id carry the titles too (music_hold_the_line, music_night_shift, music_full_bore, music_far_bank, music_low_ground, music_crossing; Ryan, the same day: apply the names to the files themselves), so the old screen-named ids (music_map, music_skirmish) and the interim mood ids are gone. The working labels (human A, rift C) stay beside the titles in the menu, since listens are still discussed by letter. The ticker: mono, right-aligned against MENU in the bar's free space, with a three-bar meter that kicks on the beat, the half bar and the bar from Sound.beat(); a new title runs in from the right edge, and a title too long for the box crawls. The menu's plate sits under PAUSED in the scrim's empty space: previous, the label over the title, next. A skip moves within the screen's set (the map's two calms; the skirmish's human A, rift C and human C, human C joining the set here, at the skirmish's level it was already written at), from the new loop's top with the screen's crossfade; the loop left behind restarts from its top next time, and a picking set goes on from the skipped-to loop at its next visit. Rift A still has no screen, so the controls never reach it. The skirmish's rule that a fight never crossfades mid-fight is the game's rule for itself; the player's hand is exempt.

Decided

Map tokens are chibi; combat units are full size.

Chibi 24x28 map tokens that make no attempt at scale, and full-size units in the skirmish, in the old Final Fantasy overworld-versus-battle split. Heavies, elites and bosses overflow their hex on the map, anchored at the hex base, drawn south over north, with the hex as the tap target. Resolved 21 September: the map draws the same models as the skirmish, and the chibi read is now the enemy's alone, since a pack on the map is a flat picture of the other world.

Decided

Few, non-disposable war machines.

Each player unit is an apocalyptic war machine that takes on many foes over many battles. No unit micromanagement. Enemy hexes are packs, so every skirmish is a few machines against a horde.

Decided

Learnable in play.

The game is not simple by nature, but a player must never need a wiki, a build planner, or DPS math. Tooltips and an in-game cheat sheet are fine. External tools are a design failure.

Decided

Player units are native 3D; enemies stay painted 2D. The genre mash is literal.

Decided 16 September from the art lab (sections 8 to 10). The human side is real geometry in the engine. Ryan masses a mech in Blender from boxes and cylinders with materials named by role (steel, steel_dark, livery, glass, amber, rubber, bore, hot, white; "detail" on parts a map token drops); tools/blendforge polishes it by rule (bevels, recessed panels and rivets, tracks with cleats and road wheels, chamfered caps) and exports it with baked occlusion and edge wear as vertex colour; the engine draws it in a viewport at the sprites' angle with the toon shader (four tones per material, one fixed light, the livery a shader colour per maker, an outline pass, wear in the clean look) so it sits beside the painted foes. Target quality: an early-to-mid-2000s RTS model, not a painting. Everything human follows: gate, defences, the hangar. What it buys: every facing free, animation, livery swaps, damage states as hidden parts, no image model near a mech. Art direction that carries over from the sprite era: cold steel with one bold livery per maker and amber markings, no olive drab, chunky hard-bevelled geometry, Mechwarrior and StarCraft Terran rather than Evangelion, a three-quarter view 30 degrees down and 45 off head-on. History, for the record: hand-typed grids (13 September), a primitive raycaster spriter (14), isometric 80x96 sprites refined by the image model (15), Blender rendered into the spriter's style with edit-mode finish (16); each was superseded within a day, and the 2D sprite path remains only for the map tokens until they too go 3D. Superseded 21 September: the blendforge polish pipeline and the toon-shaded look gave way to the mid-nineties 3D register built by lowforge (section 5, "Ally art is the mid-nineties 3D register"); the native-3D decision itself stands, and the map draws the same models, so map tokens are no longer a question.

Decided

Enemy art comes from an image model on the development Mac.

Decided 15 September. The enemy sheet's hand-typed grids are exported as a floor (tools/sheet_export.py). For real enemy art, tools/foeforge/ runs FLUX.2 Klein 4B locally through mflux (Apple silicon, about eight seconds a candidate), prompts built from each foe's codex text plus a fixed Final Fantasy VI style brief, then a cutter that keys the flat background, crops, scales into the 48x64 or 64x96 frame, flattens the shading to sixteen colours and adds the one-pixel outline. The model's own colours are kept by default; snapping to the faction palette is an option that so far reads muddy. Ryan picks a candidate per foe from a contact sheet. Check the model's licence before shipping.

Design tests

Every mechanic proposed for the skirmish is held to these. They exist because the designer's stated tendency is to overcomplicate for complexity's sake, and the goal is elegant fun.

Decided

A round takes about thirty seconds

of thought on a phone and contains one to three real decisions.

Decided

The build is visible every round.

You watch your components act. They are never just stat bonuses.

Decided

Two sources of randomness, both the player's, both visible.

The die decides what you can do; the damage roll decides how well. Kinetic faces roll a range and may crit; energy faces are static. No hidden hit rolls. Enemies never roll: their intents are telegraphed as exact numbers, as in Griftlands.

Decided

The player always has tools against the roll.

Rerolls before or after attacking, faces that raise the floor or deal the average, execute and finish effects that guarantee lethal, and visible enemy health. Variance is a tactical choice to mitigate, never a punishment.

Decided

Every pack poses a question,

such as finishing the resurrectors or interrupting the big hit, rather than being a damage race with different art.

Decided

Mismatch costs time, never ability.

A wrong loadout for a pack chews longer or eats more damage. It is never unable to act.

Decided

Never helpless, sometimes unlucky.

A bad roll with an interesting answer is where the fun lives. The design removes helplessness, not luck.

Decided

Hard cap on effects in play.

Zero to one chassis passive, where a passive is one idea in at most three rule lines: an upside and its cost count as one passive, two unrelated benefits count as two. Component passives are rare. Every trigger is shown on screen when it fires.

3

Campaign loop

Decided

The campaign is the run.

Battles are its floors. Permadeath applies to the campaign: if the gate falls, everything is lost. In-campaign progression (resources, research, upgrades) is lost with it.

Decided

Between battles: repair, salvage, research.

Resources collected in battle repair mechs and the city, upgrade the city's automated defenses and city abilities, and research new chassis, components, and commander tools. Wrecked mechs are salvaged, repaired, or broken down into components. This is core gameplay, not a menu.

Recommended

Bounded campaign, about ten battles in three acts.

One faction leads each act: undead, then demonic, then eldritch. The final battle seals the source rift. Winning unlocks difficulty tiers and new research for the next campaign. An endless mode can come later. Framing the campaign as a countdown to a convergence, one battle per night, gives the escalation a story for free.

Open

Campaign length and the ultimate goal

are not yet decided beyond "increasingly difficult battles." Target for mobile: a full campaign finishes in a few sittings; a battle resumes at any phase (built 30 September, the save row in section 11).

Battlefield layer

The battlefield decides where, when, and with whom you fight. It is deliberately the layer with positioning, so the skirmish can have none.

Map

Decided

Hex grid, gate south, rift north.

Procedurally generated terrain: water, impassable mountains, and more. One mech per hex. One enemy pack per hex. City automated defenses stand on the field near the gate and can be targeted.

Decided

The battlefield may be taller than one screen.

Seven wide stays the rule for thumbs. Height may exceed a screen and scroll vertically, with the camera following the active unit. Pointy-top hexes make defensive lines straight horizontal rows and force advancing enemies to zigzag. With cube coordinates in the logic, orientation stays a rendering choice.

Decided

Multi-hex objects.

A massive boss can occupy three hexes in a triangle. The gate and the rift are wider than one hex. Packs and mechs stay single-hex. Epic scale without player overhead. Simulation cost: units gain a footprint, and blocking, adjacency, and pathing treat every footprint hex as occupied.

Decided

Map presentation rules.

A pack is one sprite plus a number badge; tapped, it wears its strength as one segment a foe (section 11, the second phone pass). Machines are the same models as the skirmish (section 1, resolved 21 September), turned the way they move, with the mounts their die carries (the mount rule, section 7); enemies get no animation except attacks, in the old Final Fantasy way. If overflow hides something that matters, the overflow allowance is too big; partially hidden objects get an outline highlight. Retired with the sprite era: three authored directions mirrored to six, and battlefield sprites that omit customization.

Phase resolution

Decided

Enemy moves are previewed. Player plots routes and commits.

Every unit has a movespeed. Every pack commits its route when the round opens: a pusher walks to the gate, an aggressive pack turns for a machine that ends inside its reach, a hunter chases the machine it has locked (the engagement-zone row in section 5). The player sees where each pack will go, then plots each mech's route and commits.

Recommended

Player routes resolve first, then enemies execute their previewed moves and stop at the first blocked hex.

The preview is exact unless a mech ends its move on a pack's path. Body-blocking is the core operational verb, and forming lines is how the gate gets defended. Flyers can engage but cannot block, or blocking breaks. Superseded 22 September by the zone of control (a pack stops beside a grounded machine, packs never swerve), itself superseded 1 October by the engagement-zone row in section 5: a pack stops only where a body stands in its path, and the pack's own zone decides who fights. Flyers are still passed through.

Decided

Movement is in tens: plain ground costs 10, and each way of moving has its own prices.

Ryan, 2 October, replacing the placeholder of whole hexes. Tens over doubled moves with half costs, because they are the same system at a finer grain and tens suit incremental upgrades (+5 move is a reward). Every machine's and foe's move becomes today's times ten, so the Badger's 20 fords water in one phase. The prices, placeholders, by way of moving:

Wheels is a new way of moving for the cars (the Prairie Dog and the Dingo, which moved as tracks until now); its trait name is Ryan's. The tank trait is tracks, and its faster roads are the table's road row. Zones stay in hexes, since a zone is a radius and not movement; an aggressive pack's reach is its move spent by cost and then its zone in hexes, already drawn as the ring, so nobody adds the two. The balance runner does not play the map, so Ryan's play sets the prices.

Decided

A route is tapped or drawn: the tap takes the cheapest way, the drag says which way round.

Built 25 September, from the rough edge that routes were tap-to-destination only: a tap could never say which side of a pack to walk, and the zone of control makes the way there matter as much as the end. Drag from one of the player's machines and the route follows the finger: each hex it reaches joins the draft along the cheapest continuation that fits the move left (a finger that skips a hex still leaves a walkable route), a hex already on the draft cuts it back to there, back onto the machine clears it, and letting go keeps the route exactly as drawn, with a plaque at its end counting the move spent against the whole. A drag that starts anywhere else still scrolls; a second finger cancels the draft and pinches. A drawn route is judged on the same terms as a tapped one and planned as a re-plot (the machine last): every step adjacent and standable in the planned view, within the move, its end nobody else's destination; refused, it plays the back sound and leaves the machine unplotted. The tap keeps its meaning, so nothing a player already knows changed.

Decided

Adjacency after movement triggers a skirmish.

A player unit adjacent to an enemy pack, plus any units touching those, forms a skirmish. Multiple skirmishes in one phase resolve one at a time. Amended 28 September by the engagement range, and superseded 1 October by the engagement-zone row in section 5: a pack's zone decides who fights, and touching is no longer the seed.

Recommended

A skirmish is the whole connected group of adjacent units.

Superseded 1 October by the engagement-zone row in section 5: fights join only through a machine standing in two packs' zones, so nothing chains. What stands: a clustered wave becomes one dangerous fight, which teaches the player to split formations and pick off stragglers. Cap participants only if phone readability demands it; closed 2 October, since four columns is the most a formation will be (the formation row).

Decided

The boss's contact takes the phase: one fight, every machine in it, no choice.

Ryan, 25 September, built that evening. When any contact after a resolve holds the boss, the phase's other contacts are dropped (packs standing in the boss's own group included), every machine on the field is committed whatever its hex, and the fight opens on its own: no contact panel, no ENGAGE. Positioning has done its work by then; reaching the boss is the battle's end or a withdrawal. On the screen the family's boss theme starts from its top at the moment of contact (section 1 sound row), the map dims and looks at the boss, and a reveal plays over it (the black band with red rules, the boss's picture flying up out of its hex and growing with red afterimages, brackets collapsing onto it as the feed acquires it, its family typing in, its name resolving out of a scramble, the order to engage typing under, the picture tearing while it is held; a tap ends it), then the ordinary handover dives into the skirmish. The withdraw rule is unchanged: a withdrawal returns to the field with the boss still standing. Rift Pressure does not yet feed packs into the boss fight; whether it should is open (section 12). Pacing: a race to the rift

Decided

No in-field repair. The battle keeps pushing forward.

It must feel like a race to close the rift before the city is overwhelmed, not a back-and-forth war simulation. Slow-and-steady versus blitz is the player's choice, including leaving a defender at the gate.

Decided

Damage sticks. Mitigation is a real concern.

Hull never repairs during a battle. Mitigation comes from recharging shields and from defensive tactics, not from healing.

Decided

A standing shield blocks the blow whole; in a fight, shield comes only from components.

Decided 18 September, after discussion. However small the shield and however big the blow, nothing bleeds through to the hull; the blow still costs the shield its damage. That makes a ten-point shield a real decision every round a big hit is telegraphed, gives glass cannons tension, and leaves no room for an in-combat trickle (dropped the same day: a passive may bring it back for one chassis as a niche). Out of combat, shield returns each battlefield phase at the chassis's field rate; never a free refill between skirmishes, so withdraw-and-re-engage is not a reset. Foes get past a shield by striking twice (double strike) or, for bosses, by breaching: the excess overflows into hull. Rates and sizes are placeholders.

Decided

Armour is a flat number off every bare-hull blow, and zero is fine.

Decided 18 September. A chassis stat (components may add to it later), never a percentage, taken off each blow that lands on hull with no shield standing. A blow smaller than the armour does nothing: a Badger drives its treads over a horde of ghouls laughing, and that is the point. A ghoul that only ever claws for ten deserves to be walled; the answer is monsters that do more than claw (double strike, breach, abilities), not a damage floor. The Sparrowhawk has no armour and stays afraid of ghouls. Shield is not armoured: a blow on shield costs the shield its full damage, so shield answers the big blow and armour answers the swarm, and a bruiser and a glass cannon are different builds. The per-round Rift Pressure tick is what keeps a long wall from being free.

Decided

Foes strike in the order they stand, and the heaviest deploy left.

Decided 18 September. Every blow resolves on its own against the shield standing at that moment, so strike order decides what a small shield blocks. No initiative numbers, nothing to micromanage: front rank left to right, then the back rank. At first contact the heaviest hitters (attack times strikes) deploy left, ties in arrival order, so the player sees the big blow coming from the jump. A round cap on skirmishes was rejected: it breaks the flow like an advertisement in a show.

Recommended

One Rift Pressure clock.

A single bar that ticks once per battlefield phase and once per skirmish round, and spawns a fresh pack at the rift when it fills. Dawdling on the map and grinding in a skirmish feed the same meter. It is the race made mechanical, and it replaces any hard limit on skirmish length.

Decided

No persistent movespeed penalties from damage.

Combined with terrain, limping mechs make runs unwinnable with no warning. Hull damage has no secondary penalty in the first prototype.

Round loop

Recommended

No positioning on the player side.

Mechs have no ranks and never shuffle. Enemies organize around them.

Recommended

Attack, then reroll: one interleaved phase, then the enemy, then straight back.

Every mech's die is rolled. Tap to lock, drag to spend, reroll up to three times a round (section 6) at any point, before or after attacking, so a low damage roll is information for the rest of the round. End turn resolves the enemy and starts the next round at once, with no click to begin your turn. This is the loop damage ranges earn, and it is the thing that keeps the game from being Slice and Dice with extra layers. Rerolls are per round, never a pooled stock.

Decided

Damage ranges and crits, by weapon class.

Kinetic faces show a minimum and a maximum, roll between them the moment they land, and may crit for double. Energy faces show one number and never roll or crit: reliable and hot against swingy and cool. Crits are where the exciting passives live. There is no undo; the roll is the commitment. Numbers are in tens.

Decided

Hold a die to see every face.

Deciding whether to reroll needs the whole die, not the face showing. Hold lists every face with the current one marked; double-tap opens the same list as a codex box. Passives are not shown on the die; they belong to the mech.

Decided

No hard round limit.

A cap would punish defensive, value, status, reflect, and mitigation builds in favour of burst. Skirmish length is governed by the Rift Pressure clock, the withdraw option, and tuning (typical skirmish three to five rounds; enemy damage must outpace sustain so stalemates cannot happen).

Decided

Player-initiated withdraw, and its price is one enemy turn.

Decided 19 September. Withdraw is offered at any point of the player's turn, the first round included. Its price is always the same: the enemy takes its turn, then the team flees at the end of the round, so there is no leapfrogging around contact unscathed. Being forced into contact is bad strategy, not bad luck, and gets the same price. Withdrawing ends the skirmish; survivors on both sides carry their damage back to the map and the pack marches on. Replaces the 15 September rule (start of round only, before any face was spent).

Decided

The enemy's turn is seen, blow by blow, before the next round or the flight.

Ryan, 23 September, after a withdrawal put the outcome up over a turn nobody saw. The enemy's turn replays one blow a beat: the foe lunges at its machine, and as the blow lands that machine's shield and hull step down to what it left, a broken shield shattering; the log types the blows in as they play. Only then do the new dice show, or the outcome (a withdrawal included: the parting blows are the price, so they are watched). The player's taps are held off while it plays. The beat is a setting, ENEMY PACE, whose top is the old instant replay (0.12 s between blows) and whose default is 0.45 s.

Decided

Ends on: pool empty (victory), all hulls zero (defeat), or withdraw.

Victory awards resources and a temporary upgrade choice. Defeat turns the mechs to scrap: wrecks to be salvaged after the battle. Surviving enemies continue their assault.

No ground is controlled: a pack stops only where a body stands in its path (a machine, a pack, the gate), and a machine beside its path is walked past unless the zone reaches it where the pack stops. A flyer fights by the zone like anyone and is still passed through. A stealth machine is never pulled in by a zone alone: it fights when it stopped one of the fight's packs, or when another machine is in the fight. A static defence inside a zone is in. The enemy commits first (Ryan: a pack whose route changes with the plan is a hidden rule, however deterministic the engine). When the round opens every pack commits a route, and the route the player reads is the route; the plan only cuts it where a body stands. Three behaviours on the foe type (behaviour): a pusher walks its committed route to the gate and never reacts, the pressure; an aggressive pack does the same unless a machine ends inside its reach, its move plus its zone, drawn as a dashed ring at the round's start, in which case it goes for the hex within its move whose zone holds the most machines, ties toward the gate; a hunter locks one machine when it spawns, by its caveat (hunts: nearest, weakest, strongest, slowest or fastest; weak and strong read hull plus shield, slow and fast read move, stealth machines are never picked), wears the display's target lock on it, and chases wherever that machine ends until one of them is dead, picking again by the same caveat if its prey is wrecked. The ring and the lock answer "will it come for me" before the plan is touched; the live preview then shows exactly whom it catches. A ranged pack walks rather than halting at range, since halting catches the picket and walking catches the squad. Content placeholders: the ghoul aggressive (its codex says it walks at the nearest heartbeat), the brute a hunter of the strongest at move 3, everything else a pusher; a dedicated hunter is a foe for Ryan to name. What it is for. Picking packs off one at a time must be resisted by the game, and fewer, longer fights are the aim. Formations with a front and a back are the threat: engaging the front puts the squad inside the back pack's larger zone. Heavy armour is a risk, since it is outnumbered easily; a scout trades power for the peel, ending behind the formation inside the back pack's zone and outside the front's. If the peel proves too strong, the lever is the stat skew between scouts and heavies, which the balance runner can measure as a skirmish matchup. Weighed and dropped: chaining in any form (enemies in lockstep would make the game unwinnable); a touch rule, packs standing together fight together (a formation in file could not then be split by anything the player does); double-booking a machine into two fights (merge is the intuitive read, and the contact panel keeps choosing the order and being the planning stop); the zone as the stop (it halts a ranged pack short of the squad); packs that re-aim at the plan without a drawn trigger; a separate aggro radius unrelated to reach (a ring that promises what the pack cannot deliver). The player splits a formation by getting behind it, blocking one column with a body while the other walks on, baiting a hunter or an aggressive pack out, and tempo between the rift's waves; attrition is not a lever, since a fight runs to one side's end or a withdrawal. Kiting: engage-and-withdraw costs the enemy's full turn and the pack stays where it is; dancing out of a reach ring is bounded by reach and by the biggest-catch rule, and a hunter of the slowest cannot be kited at all. On the map: every pack's zone where it will stop, in the warning red, a thin outline when empty and a tinted region under a heavy outline with a fight in it, a fight's packs sharing one outline; an aggressive pack's reach as a dashed crawling ring; a hunter's lock as the target rings on its prey; the states differ by weight, fill and structure, never by hue alone. The tethers, the tie rule in field order and the machines' flowers are gone. Still open: the radii and the hunters' move, by Ryan's play; the rift seeding and spawning formations rather than single packs; the map growing wider if zones crowd it.

Decided

The battlefield screen is CARE's tactical reconstruction of the field.

Decided 20 September. Ryan's framing was a war table in-world: an approximated satellite view with the HUD, grid, mechs and labels projected over it, the only explanation for seeing the corrupted 2D landscape at all; he took Claude's refinement as the same idea with more to hang on it. Sensor language follows and is wanted for its strategic depth: a pack not yet seen is a contact, the edge tags are sensor returns, the intent lines are projections; the reconstruction's quality is a possible research path later. Scale is a feel question, not a plausibility one: the mechs need not fit through the gate, but the gate must look like it keeps behemoths out.

Decided

The city is under a field, and the gate is its projector.

Ryan's hole, 20 September: a wall with one reinforced gate makes no sense against things that climb and fly. Decided the same day: the city sits under an impenetrable energy field, the Arsenal's one technology the Council funds in full, and the gate is its projector. The field is cast from the gate outward and over the city, and the projector stands in its own shadow: the mouth is the one place the field cannot cover, which is why every pack heads for it and why the gate falling loses the campaign (the projector goes, the field goes, the city is open). Rift Pressure is literally pressure on the field; the sortie exists because the field strains as pressure climbs and the rift must be closed before it fails. The walls on the bottom row are the projector's emitter line, and the blades on every top edge are its vanes, so the ornament has a job. Known holes, accepted: why not a second field over the mouth (every projector has a mouth; the innermost still has one). Still to do in art: the field itself, if it shows at all (a faint edge at the bottom row, or nothing), and the walls reshaped as emitters. The gate's later direction (Ryan, 21 September): keep the fortress brief but add StarCraft Terran, more machinery and lights, fewer vanes, a wider door that shows how it opens.

Decided

Every machine can ford water; each way of moving has its own worst ground.

Ryan, 21 September: machines this size cross water, so it is a penalty, not a wall, and terrain should make the ways of moving feel different rather than "slow and slower". Tracks wade slowly (3), a walker wades (2) and shrugs off rough and rubble, hover skims water (1) but chokes on rubble (3), a flyer ignores everything and is the only thing that crosses a crag. Numbers are placeholders. Found 2 October: a step may not cost more than the whole move, so a tracked machine on a move of two never fords at all. Superseded 2 October by movement in tens (section 4).

Decided

Front and back ranks, enemy side only.

Simple two-rank structure. It adds a second axis to target choice: a front rank of bodies and a back rank of problems.

Recommended

A pool with ranks, in columns.

Two rows by four columns visible at a time (three until 23 September). Dead enemies are replaced from the pack's pool, so a horde keeps coming and elites surface at set points. Each back enemy stands behind one front slot, so "exposed" is visible rather than a rule.

Range archetypes

Decided

No damage falloff at range.

Futuristic weapons hitting for less at range is counterintuitive. Range is expressed as archetypes with soft penalties, never smaller numbers.

Decided

Three reaches plus keywords.

Decided 18 September with Ryan's vocabulary audit. Melee hits the front rank, and the back rank only when the whole front is empty, so a melee build can be stranded by a held back rank and that is its cost. Line of sight hits the front rank freely and a back enemy only where its column is exposed. Indirect hits any foe in either rank; on the front rank it pays splash, half the damage off your own shield. Keywords ride on top: spread for neighbours, penetrate for the column. A passive may change a component's reach while a condition holds (the Badger's cracked weapons go melee). Melee is authored on a component too since 26 September (the Badger's claws); until then it only ever came from a passive. No delayed shells: delay adds cross-round state for no gain.

Shields, heat, injuries

Recommended

Shields are a timer.

Shield faces add shield. The shield buffer gives a few rounds to solve the pack before hull is at risk. Enemy damage should threaten to break a shield within two or three rounds against a typical pack, so shield faces buy time rather than immunity.

Recommended

Heat is the second resource.

Strong faces add heat. A vent face clears it. Running hot risks internal damage. Passives and support faces (vent an ally's heat) make heat a team problem, not a solo one.

Decided

Every chassis cools passively at the end of the round.

Ryan, 22 September. A chassis carries a cooling rating (content; 0 when absent) and sheds that much heat at the start of every turn, before the dice roll. Venting stays a tactic; a round spent on ballistics or shields is now also a way of waiting to cool down. Cool at the start, heat at the end (Ryan, same day): the Overheating status ticks at the end of the round, after the enemy has struck, so a scorch lands on the round's peak heat before the next turn's cooling relieves it; the first cut had it the other way round and cooling softened every scorch. Every overheat burns at the end of the round (Ryan, 22 September, third pass): heat climbs past the maximum during the round with no spike when a component is spent, the Overheating tick lands on top, and whatever stands over the cap then burns hull one for one as the gauge settles at the cap. So a machine run hot can still be vented before it pays, and the space is there for other ways to intervene. The old "heat max" chip is now the burn warning: the hull the round's end will take unless something changes. The forecast's heat counts the stacks carried plus one per scorching blow coming; it counts the die's own heat only while that die is being dragged, since shown unasked it read as something done to the mech rather than by the player. Cooling is a stat like the others (a passive may raise it). Tuning, not design: cooling must stay well under a typical weapon's heat, and a vent component must be worth a whole turn, so the heat sink wants a bigger number. Numbers are placeholders.

Decided

Injuries are one threshold and one roll of the die, on bare hull only.

When a blow lands on bare hull (no shield standing) and what gets past armour is at least a quarter of maximum hull, the mech's die is rolled and the component it lands on is injured. An intact component cracks: it loses its keywords, crit chance and unique effect but still does its basic action. A cracked component hit again is destroyed and goes blank. A destroyed component or an empty hardpoint takes the blow and nothing happens, so a badly broken mech shrugs off more of them. A passive may hold a cracked component short of destruction (the Badger's). The counterplay is shielding before a big telegraphed hit. The one-in-two coin on top of the threshold was cut on 17 September as randomness with no decision behind it; per-faction signature injuries were dropped earlier as an unnecessary layer. The threshold is a placeholder.

Decided

A damaged face is a damaged component, and it stays damaged.

Cracked and destroyed faces persist across skirmishes and battles until repaired in the hangar. This is the component damage system: there is no separate one. It makes a lost skirmish cost something into the next battle and gives the meta layer's repair and salvage real work to do.

Pinned

Low-hull berserker or last-stand ability.

A mech at critical hull unlocking an overdrive face is Doom energy and an anti-spiral. Parked, not rejected.

Recommended

Terrain is a skirmish-wide condition.

The hexes involved set a modifier for the whole fight, so choosing where to engage still matters without positioning inside the skirmish. Water dampens burn, rubble slows a reload, and so on. Ryan's caveat (22 September, section 12): things the machines feel, never a bonus the foes get, since a pack stands on good ground for free; the ground a fight is on is decided and nothing acts on it yet.

Die faces and iconography

Decided

Primary icon, a number, small secondary icons, one catch-all star.

The primary icon is the face's type: the reach archetype for attacks, a shield for shield faces, and so on. The number is its power. Small superscript or subscript icons mark common attached effects. One catch-all icon marks a rare, unique effect (legendary faces). Long-press a face to inspect secondary numbers, unique effects, and temporary boosts. An icon cheat sheet is available from the pause menu.

Decided

Every entry on the die is a component.

There is no generic equipment and nothing fills a slot for you: an entry is a component, or an empty hardpoint that does nothing when it comes up, or a destroyed component. A pristine, fully fitted war machine is reliable; a light or injured one is not. Decided 18 September, replacing "every face does something".

Recommended

A keyword vocabulary of about ten.

Variety comes from combining number, reach, keywords, and heat cost with passives, not from more rules. Slice and Dice gets hundreds of distinct faces from a vocabulary this size.

Decided

Keywords are a list, not a single slot.

A face may carry several keywords, so faces can be upgraded during a battle or in the meta. The content validator caps authored faces at three keywords; upgrades may push past that at runtime. Iconography stays readable because the primary icon and the number are fixed, and extra keywords show as small secondary icons.

Decided

Minimal chrome in the skirmish.

No cards, no boxes. The log stays in the centre for now because it is needed for testing and the space is free; it can hide behind a button later. Health and status are small floating slider bars beside the sprite. Machines face the foes (a rear three-quarter, turned left) and enemies face left (section 5, the ranks row); machines idle their joints, enemies have only attack animations. A massive elite may occupy both rank slots of a column. Tap, double-tap, or hold shows full numbers and details. Passives are simple enough not to be displayed at all times. Enemy health stays visible (the JRPG-style hidden-health option was dropped with the Griftlands damage model).

Decided

The vector HUD is the UI.

Adopted 14 September from the layout canvas. Everything that is not a sprite is a flat vector overlay: slanted gauges, rounded-square dice with corner brackets when locked, bracketed [ATK 10] readouts, a terminal-style log. The HUD colour (green by default, amber, blue; red was dropped as the enemy colour, section 5) is a player setting saved to the user settings file. Decided

Skirmish readouts.

Decided 15 September from playtest notes and refined since. No unit names on screen: hold or double-tap for them. Gauges hug the drawing, not the frame. Decided

Three rerolls, and a reroll always shows a different face.

Decided 15 September. Two rerolls did not feel like enough even before consumables and commander powers; the base is three. A reroll never lands on the face showing nor on one equivalent to it, as a known global rule: these are not really dice, and a reroll that changes nothing reads as a bug rather than as bad luck. A die whose faces are all alike keeps its face. The count shows on the Reroll button only, not in the header. Decided

Intents show the action, not the target.

Each foe shows what it will do and for how much. Holding a foe or a mech draws the arrows for that unit; an overlay button draws every arrow. This is Slice and Dice's model.

Decided

Few machines against a horde is the fantasy, and the hooks come from it.

Decided 15 September. With every foe on the field striking each round, incoming damage on the lowest shield is the pressure the whole skirmish is built around, and the answer is design, not a cap: area damage that earns its keep against a full formation, taunt and mitigation that shape who gets hit, and enemy behaviour that is not always optimal. The horde is an invasion, not an army: most rank and file may target badly or sometimes not attack at all, while the higher tiers of the demonic and eldritch factions execute plans that look thought through. A direction to build: packs that move with or natively include a commander unit; kill it and the pack's targeting scrambles, even mid-turn. The current foes and pack compositions are proof-of-concept content and will be replaced.

Decided

A foe type is not limited to one action.

Each foe in content/foes.json carries an actions roster: attack, defend, buff, or debuff, each with a player-facing line and a status. Sometimes a foe attacks, sometimes it shields, sometimes it buffs the horde or weakens a mech. Every action runs since 30 September (section 9): a foe picks by weight between its blow and its special. A draft the engine cannot run yet carries "status": "planned" and stays out of the codex until coded.

Decided

Tap a die to lock it, hold it for details.

No separate Keep button. A locked die is highlighted and skipped by reroll.

Decided

Double-tap a unit for its codex entry.

Hold is the quick read: numbers and arrows while the finger is down. Double-tap opens a box that stays: the codex text, stats, actions, faces shown visually in a column with the current face highlighted, the passive with its rules, and last the flavour line as an italic quote. The head-on sprite sits top-right of the box across from the name. Every foe has its own flavour line, not the faction's. The design question a unit poses is never shown; fantasy and question are internal design fields, and any hint the player needs goes into the codex text. A tap outside closes it.

Decided

Numbers in the magnitude of tens.

Hull, shield, damage, and heat live in tens, not ones, so upgrades can be incremental and big numbers feel big; a 15 or a 33 is fine, and the validator only refuses negatives (clarified 18 September). Burn is one stack per twenty damage dealt at five per stack, splash is half the damage rolled, heat overflows one for one.

Decided

Content text is split by shape: shown at the top level, internal under design.

Decided 16 September, from Ryan's cleanup. Every content entry (chassis, foe, component, faction, vocabulary term) has exactly two shown texts, codex (the inspect entry, in combat and in the hangar) and flavour (an in-world quote), plus a passive's name and description and a foe action's text. Everything internal sits under one design object: fantasy (the pitch, the inspiration, deliberate homages), niche (the role it fills or the problem it poses, the old foe-only "question" generalised), holes on vocabulary terms, signature and answer on factions, notes. The validator refuses any of those keys at the top level, so nothing internal can leak into a codex by accident. Passives lost their rules text: the description says what the passive does in the player's words and the effects list is what the engine runs, capped at three. Components now carry codex and flavour too, since they are inspected.

Mechs and the builder

Mechwarrior's build depth, simplified and dialled back. The feel to keep is that a loadout is a real identity. The habits to drop are strict weight classes and gated equipment.

Decided

Three to four mechs per battlefield.

Enough for a real decision between splitting up and staying together for skirmish strength, without tedious planning. Recommended: design for four, start a campaign with three, unlock the fourth slot early.

Decided

Chassis are not classes and not only bipeds.

Tanks, hovercraft, quadrupeds, and flyers are all candidates. No light, medium, heavy labels. Chassis diversity comes from base stats: hull, shield, movespeed, hardpoints, and similar simple numbers that do not need prominent display.

Decided

The die is the equipment: fixed components plus hardpoints, one entry each.

Decided 18 September. A chassis has fixed components (built in, never removed or sold, no hardpoint used: the Badger's claw and plating) and hardpoints (free slots, any component in any one). Die size is fixed count plus hardpoints; the Badger rolls a d6 from two fixed and four hardpoints, and its model carries four pins. Every component is exactly one entry: multi-slot components were considered and scrapped the same day as invisible complexity (where would it render, and would anyone pay for the fragility). Decided

Zero to one chassis passive, and it may be a skirmish passive.

Two flavours. The chassis pass of 28 September: eight machines given their shapes, and the rules that came out of it.

Decided

The passive and the fixed components are the design; the stock loadout is the maker's generic kit.

Ryan, 26 September, on the Mandrill's first fill. A chassis's flavour and its design live in its passive and its fixed (locked) components, which the player cannot swap; everything in the free hardpoints is stock that the player will replace, so it is never factored into the design of the unit. The stock kit will most likely be generic and shared between a maker's machines, with unique components unlocked through play (drops and blueprints, section 10). Two consequences: a passive must work with generic kit, never depend on one unique component (a mech must not feel terrible to run because the right blueprint has not dropped); and every mech wants enough depth that there is no straight-line optimisation and no single build path. For the Mandrill this means its identity has to come from its passive and from something fixed; its shoulder cannons or its backpack pods are the candidates for fixed slots, and the rest of its filled kit is in flux. Related, the same day: bosses should be resistant to complete stuns as a rule (today a suppress on a massive foe adds two Numbed stacks instead of one, so the second suppress already lands at 80%; a base resistance for bosses landed 26 September, the Numbed row below). Second-chance rolls are not avoided on principle: the bible's one-source-of-randomness rule is about decision density, so a chance the player can shift by their own choices is a decision, and a chance they cannot touch is noise.

Decided

Two component types, weapon and equipment, and equipment says whom it reaches.

Ryan, 26 September, built the same day. A weapon is spent on a foe within its reach and need not deal damage; equipment is spent on a machine. Every shield and vent is equipment, and each says whom it may be spent on: self and allies (the default), self only, or allies only (targets on the face; the die shows a chip for a narrowed one, the description says "self only" or "allies only", and the screen lights only the machines it may land on). It is not a general rule that a shield reaches an ally: the design space stays open, one component at a time. Buffs, when they exist, are equipment too. The same day: overcharge is retired as a keyword (double damage and double heat was the Sparrowhawk's passive over again, and the Overclocked Railgun, a temporary component, now carries 100 and 40 heat outright); the word is reserved for an offensive buff on an ally, shape open. And every component carries design.stage, draft until Ryan signs it off as final, so nobody mistakes a filled-in placeholder for a decision; nothing is final yet. On the Baboon the backpack pods are its fixed component, the Solar Flare Array, so it applies a status on its own and the passive has something to read in stock configuration.

Decided

The codex is the machine's base state; the fight's effects live on the hold.

Ryan, 26 September. The codex shows a machine as it is between fights: its base numbers with any battle-duration component buffs applied (the skirmish rewards of section 8) and no combat effect at all; no status is ever listed there. The hold-press popup is where in-combat effects are listed, and where the chips on a unit expand into their descriptions. Decided

Numbed decays, and a foe may carry a base stun resistance.

Ryan, 26 September, after an analysis of three patterns: permanent scaling within the fight (Numbed's first form: honest, but a resource that only runs out, worst in the long boss fights where a stun matters most), Darkest Dungeon's one-turn immunity after a stun plus base resistance per foe (a rhythm of stun, skip, stun, but two stun sources alternating lock a foe for good), and decaying stacks (the same rotate-your-targets decision as a slope rather than a wall). Built as the third with the second's base resistance: a landed suppress adds two Numbed stacks (three on a massive foe), each stack is 10% resistance on top of the foe's own stun_resist from content (30 on the three bosses, 0 on the rank and file, placeholders), a stack is lost at the start of each of the foe's turns, and a shrugged-off suppress adds nothing. So a stun repeated on one foe stops landing while stuns spread across the formation keep working, and a boss is hard to stun twice running but never immune for the fight. The bot values a suppress at its odds. Ryan's caveat stands: whether any of this is needed hinges on the intended length of a skirmish and of a boss skirmish, to be judged in play; today the runner's greedy fights last two to six rounds against a pack and four to nine against a boss. Ryan also agreed that suppress, a complete stun, is allowed to choke itself: by the time a foe resists everything, the damage windows it gave should have ended the fight.

Decided

Buff equipment: a component kind that grants a machine a status for the round.

Ryan, 28 September (the 29th's build). A buff is the third kind of equipment beside shield and vent: spent on a machine (itself, an ally, or either, as the component says) it grants one of the machines' statuses, so many stacks deep, through the end of so many rounds, and the pipeline reads it like any modifier. The status names what the buff does, so a buff component is data: status, stacks, rounds, and the validator holds the status to the machines' own (a debuff on a foe stays a weapon). Three statuses to start, names placeholders: Overcharged (+10% damage a stack; the word Ryan freed from the old keyword, and the same status devour will stack on the Ammit for the skirmish), Braced (15% less damage taken a stack) and Primed (every blow lands a Burning stack per stack). The die shows the buff glyph with the stacks as its number and the status's name in the row; the hold lists it on the machine like any in-combat effect. One draft component carries it, the Overcharge Coil, fitted to nothing. The bot weighs a buff by what its status does to the machine it lands on this round: more on the attack in hand, less of the incoming, or the burn its blows will leave, and nothing on a machine whose die is spent. The same day the bot learned to value a status for the stun it may cause through a machine's on_foe_status passive, at the loop's odds and the foe's odds of not shrugging it off, so the Baboon's balance row stops understating it.

Decided

Hardpoints are gun mounts on the model; a component wears a silhouette class at the next mount, and armour and vents show nowhere.

The pins, proposed 16 September and in force since the first lowforge units: an Empty named hp_1 … hp_n, one per hardpoint, its +Y where the weapon points; a fixed component is modelled into the machine and carries no pin. Since 29 September, night, every pin carries the frame and the scale a mounted part wears and parents its stock part (with b.socket(...) in models_low.py), so MechView3D.mount() hides exactly the component and never the mount it sits on.

The rule (Ryan, 30 September, after judging the first pass, where a slab of plating sat on a gun socket and a vent block hung off a wing tip): hardpoints are gun mounts. Every pin points forward-ish and can take a gun. A component with a shape takes the next mount in the die's order; a component with none (armour panels, vents) takes a die entry and no mount, since it is applied to the whole machine and shows nowhere. Ryan's caveat: equipment that can reasonably look like a gun keeps a shape, so shield projectors and a buff coil are a dish. Five classes then: barrel, twin barrel, rack, blade and dish; slab and vent block are detail helpers. Every blade is katar-style, punching forward, which fits the mecha register, lets every mount point the same way, and gives the flyers wing blades. A twin barrel is one component on one mount, two barrels at a fixed spacing times the pin's scale; a hand-modelled stock pair may sit wider than the class, and on a swap the class is what shows. The stock weapons are modelled at the first mounts; a machine with more hardpoints than stock guns carries bare mount pads (b.pad()), so a fitted gun never floats, and a mount is never a slab or a vent. MechView3D.mount_plan() counts mounts over the shaped entries of the die and of the stock loadout and names only the mounts that differ; a mount left bare hides its stock part. The codex names no socket per entry (Ryan: the order is internal, the hangar will be visual, and our dice are not really dice); it checks the model's mount count against the hardpoints once per die and shows each component's class as a dashed chip, "none" for what shows nowhere.

Commander and city

The mechs are the team; the player is the commander. Commander tools do what mechs cannot: manipulation, mitigation, information, and movement. They are never a fifth gun.

In the skirmish

Recommended

One command die, rolled with the mech dice.

Its faces are rechargeable abilities, so recharge is the roll and nobody hoards a die result. Examples: smoke that blanks the back rank's intents this round, a drone screen that absorbs the next hit, focus fire that adds one to every face on a single target, a scan that reveals the pack's next two rounds. Die size (d4, d6, d8) is a research path with stronger faces and a backfire face or two on the larger dice.

Decided

Reusable commander abilities never deal direct damage.

One-shots may. Damage one-shots belong at the battlefield layer (soften a pack before contact) so mech dice stay the killing power inside a skirmish.

Decided

Command faces come from the hangar, never from skirmish rewards.

Thematically wrong as loot, and they would dominate the reward pick because they affect every skirmish equally.

Open

Is the command die needed at all?

The alternative is one-shots only, with meta upgrades to inventory size and effectiveness, and reroll bundles among the rewards. Claude's view: keep the die, because a rolled face is inherently non-hoardable and the one-shots-only model pushes everything into the inventory the designer already knows they would hoard.

One-shots and the hoarding problem

Decided

Spending a consumable must not feel bad.

The designer is in the camp that hoards potions for a rainy day that never comes. If one-shots are core, the design has to make spending the default.

Recommended

Three fixes.

One-shots picked up in a battle expire when the rift closes, so there is nothing to save them for beyond this battle. The pouch holds three, so a fourth pickup forces use-or-discard on the spot. A starting stock chosen in the hangar restocks every battle; research raises capacity and quality. Doom Eternal solved the same problem the same way: resources regenerate on a short loop, so spending is the default and saving is the anomaly. Saving a full pouch for the boss remains a legitimate blitz strategy with a real cost in missed upgrades.

Skirmish rewards

Recommended

Pick one of three.

A modifier on a mech face you already carry (lasts the battle), a one-shot for the pouch, or a battle-wide boon such as an extra reroll per round. Temporary upgrades amplify the build you brought and never replace it, so the pull is toward synergy.

Decided

Reward types are faction-weighted, never guaranteed.

Undead might give a greater chance of multiple or rarer one-shots, for example. Flavour to be determined per faction.

On the battlefield: city abilities

Decided

Battlefield-level abilities belong to the city.

Sprint, airlift, teleport, tractor beam, orbital strike, and any number of silly ideas. They are upgraded and managed as city systems, and no command die is rolled on the battlefield.

Decided

Cooldowns in battlefield phases are acceptable here.

Each movement phase is a turn, so a cooldown is legible and cannot be hoarded. City research shortens cooldowns, adds charges, and unlocks abilities. Cooldowns still do not exist inside the skirmish, where the die is the recharge.

Recommended

Some city abilities are limited by distance from the gate.

Artillery reaches the southern half, airlift only lifts from the gate, the tractor beam has a range. The push north leaves city support behind, which is a real cost to blitzing and comes out of flavour rather than a rule.

Meta and resources

Decided

Two kinds of gain.

Persistent resources, spent between battles. Temporary upgrades, lasting the current battle only. Skirmish victory yields both.

Decided

Wrecks are recoverable.

Defeated mechs become scrap, salvaged, repaired, or broken down into components between battles. Losses hurt but stay recoverable, which is what prevents a campaign from being lost three battles before it ends.

Research trees.

Chassis and components, city defenses and city abilities, commander dice and one-shot capacity. Manufacturers give the chassis tree its structure.

Technical plan

Recommended

Godot 4 with GDScript.

Chosen on the project's merits, independent of any other codebase. A 2D-first renderer with pixel-art settings built in, hex tile layers, a strong UI toolkit for a game that is mostly panels and dice, Android export out of the box, no build step, and a permissive license with no runtime fee. Unity wins only if a deep ads and in-app-purchase SDK ecosystem is a day-one priority.

Recommended

Keep the simulation pure.

Hex math, movement, skirmish resolution, and map generation are plain scripts with no scene dependencies, driven by a seeded random generator. That makes them testable headless and gives reproducible runs for replays and daily seeds.

Decided

Balance is measured by a ladder of bots, and read as bands, not targets.

Ryan, 25 September: one person can play-test feel and micro balance, never the scale macro balance needs. So the pure simulation gets a player: sim/policy.gd drives a skirmish headless on a ladder of skill. Random never rerolls and spends every die on any legal target: the floor, and a pack it beats is too easy, no argument. Greedy reads the same forecast the screen shows the player and takes the best one-round trade, rerolling dice that come up under their die's average, holding a die whose every spend would cost more hull than it earns; it cannot see anything the player cannot. Personas are habits on top (withdraw under a hull line). A rollout rung (replay a few rounds ahead from the seed) waits until greedy stops being enough. tools/balance.gd plays every chassis alone and the starting team against every pack a faction sends, hundreds of seeds a cell, into docs/balance.md: win rate per rung, rounds and hull spent, and pick share per component, the signal for equipment (a component nobody spends is dead; one spent every time is the whole die). A cell where the rungs land together holds no decision, which is the Slice and Dice smell caught by machine. The runner says which matchups are off by a lot; feel decides the rest, and no simulated win rate is chased to a decimal. Battle and meta simulation stack on this in order, each level's bot using the one below as its auto-resolve, and neither is built before its loop exists.

Decided

Save at every phase boundary.

Android can kill a backgrounded app at any moment. If the whole state serializes at the end of each battlefield phase and each skirmish round, resuming is free rather than a retrofit. Built 30 September, after every change rather than every boundary (the save row below).

Recommended

Cube coordinates for hexes.

Pointy-top rendering with cube or axial coordinates in the logic, so distance, neighbours, and lines are trivial and orientation can change later.

Recommended

Prototype the skirmish first.

Done: the skirmish was the first thing built and is where the depth lives. It was the riskiest unknown and it defines what a component is, which defines the builder and the meta.
